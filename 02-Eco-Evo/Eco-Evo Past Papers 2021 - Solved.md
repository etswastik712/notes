## Section 2: Descriptive answers
> [!question] D1 (2.5) · Environmental temperature against body temperature for (a) a tiger and (b) a lizard, with a sentence of justification each.

> [!example]- Solution ([[EE02 Structured Population Models]] temperature section)
> (a) **Tiger (endotherm): a flat line.** Body temperature stays ~37–38 °C over a wide range of environmental temperature. Metabolic heat and insulation regulate it. (The line holds only inside the thermoneutral and tolerance range; beyond it the animal dies.)
> (b) **Lizard (ectotherm): a rising line close to $T_b=T_{env}$.** Body temperature follows the environment because it gains heat mainly from outside. Basking and shade seeking flatten it partly in the middle range. The evaluator's comment on the sheet: "should have mentioned terms ectotherm and endotherm".

> [!question] D2 (3) · (a) Difference between the population regulation of the migratory and non-migratory wildebeest of the Serengeti. (b) Two adaptations that let wildebeest migrate so far.

> [!example]- Solution ([[EE01 Introduction to Biology and Ecological Thinking]], [[Serengeti Wildebeest Migration]], [[Predator Satiation]])
> (a) **Migratory herds are food-limited (bottom-up).** By following the rains they escape most resident predators, which cannot follow them because of their dens and territories, so their numbers are set by dry-season grass. **Resident herds are predator-limited (top-down)**: they stay within the territories of lions and hyenas all year, so predation keeps them far below the food limit. This is why migrants number over a million and residents only tens of thousands.
> (b) Any two:
> - the most efficient muscles known in mammals (62.6 %);
> - an energy-cheap walking gait;
> - synchronous calving: precocial calves stand within minutes and travel with the herd within days;
> - the ability to track rainfall and green flush over long distances.

> [!question] D3 (3) · (a) The rationale of Hassell et al. (1976) for claiming that most natural populations show simple dynamics. (b) Turchin and Taylor's (1992) main criticism.

> [!example]- Solution ([[Hassell Model]], [[Time-Series Reconstruction]], [[EE03 Population Dynamics and Chaos]])
> (a) They fitted the one-lag **Hassell model** $N_{t+1}=\lambda N_t(1+aN_t)^{-b}$ to 24 insect populations (field and lab) and placed each fitted $(\lambda,b)$ on the model's stability diagram. **22 of 24 fell in the monotonic-damping region** (one damped oscillation, one limit cycle, no chaos), so they concluded natural populations are mostly stable and simple.
> (b) The **model structure forced the answer**. A first-order (single-lag) model cannot represent **delayed density dependence** (from predators, parasites, maternal effects), which produces cycles and more complex dynamics. Turchin and Taylor refitted with lagged response surfaces and found the whole range of dynamics. The time series were also short and noisy.

> [!question] D4 (3) · Lotka–Volterra competition with $r_1=1.9$, $r_2=3.4$, $\alpha_{12}=1.5$, $\alpha_{21}=0.5$, $K_2=100$, starting at 10 of each. (a) The minimum $K_1$ for stable coexistence? (b) How large must $K_1$ be for species 1 to exclude species 2 whatever the starting conditions?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Lotka-Volterra Competition Model]])
> Isocline intercepts: species 1 at $(K_1,0)$ and $(0,K_1/\alpha_{12})$; species 2 at $(K_2/\alpha_{21},0)=(200,0)$ and $(0,K_2)=(0,100)$. The $r$'s and the starting point do not matter for these questions.
> (a) Stable coexistence needs each species to inhibit itself more than the other: $K_1<K_2/\alpha_{21}=200$ **and** $K_2<K_1/\alpha_{12}$, i.e. $K_1>\alpha_{12}K_2=150$. **$K_1$ must exceed 150** (and stay below 200). ($\alpha_{12}\alpha_{21}=0.75<1$, so a stable crossing is possible.)
> (b) Species 1's isocline must lie entirely outside species 2's: $K_1>K_2/\alpha_{21}=$ **200**. Then $K_1/\alpha_{12}>133>100$ too, so species 1 wins from any start.

> [!question] D5 (3) · Distinguish: (a) population-persistence and resource-utilisation concepts of the niche; (b) crop rotation and slash-and-burn agriculture; (c) nitrogen fixation and denitrification.

> [!example]- Solution ([[Ecological Niche]], [[EE06 Eutrophication and Alternative Stable States]], [[Nutrient Limitation]])
> (a) **Persistence niche** (Hutchinson): the set of conditions and resources under which a population has positive growth ($\lambda>1$), i.e. where it *can* persist. **Utilisation niche** (MacArthur and Levins): what individuals actually use (the distribution of food sizes, habitats, times), measured from behaviour. Overlap of utilisation curves gives competition coefficients.
> (b) **Crop rotation**: alternating crops on the same field across seasons, e.g. cereals with nitrogen-fixing legumes. It restores soil N, breaks pest cycles and keeps land permanently in use. **Slash-and-burn** (shifting cultivation, *jhum*): clear and burn forest, so the ash releases nutrients; farm for a few years until fertility falls; then move on and leave the plot fallow for years to recover. It is sustainable only with long fallows and low population density. (General knowledge; not in this year's slides.)
> (c) **Nitrogen fixation**: N₂ → NH₃/NH₄⁺ by nitrogenase in bacteria (*Rhizobium* in legume nodules, cyanobacteria, free-living soil bacteria), or industrially and by lightning. It brings N into the biosphere. **Denitrification**: NO₃⁻ → N₂O → N₂ by anaerobic bacteria in waterlogged soils and sediments. It returns N to the atmosphere and removes it from ecosystems.

> [!question] D6 (3) · $\dfrac{dN}{dt}=rN\Big(\dfrac NA-1\Big)\Big(1-\dfrac NK\Big)$. (a) Which assumption of the continuous logistic is relaxed? (b) What is $A$? (c) The name of the phenomenon?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Allee Effect]], [[Logistic Growth]])
> (a) The logistic assumes that per-capita growth is **highest at low density and falls monotonically with $N$** (negative density dependence everywhere). Here per-capita growth *increases* with density at low $N$: positive density dependence.
> (b) $A$ is the **critical threshold density** (Allee threshold): for $0<N<A$, $dN/dt<0$ and the population goes extinct; for $A<N<K$ it grows to $K$. $N=A$ is an unstable equilibrium; $0$ and $K$ are stable.
> (c) The (strong) **Allee effect**. Mechanisms include mate finding, group defence and social facilitation, as with the passenger pigeon ([[Passenger Pigeon Extinction]]).

> [!question] D7 · Butterflies, continuous logistic, $K=500$, $r=0.1$: the maximum possible realised growth rate $dN/dt$? (Derive the $N$ at which it is maximal.)

> [!example]- Solution ([[Logistic Growth]])
> $\dfrac{d}{dN}\big[rN(1-N/K)\big]=r(1-2N/K)=0$ ⇒ $N=K/2=250$. $\big(\tfrac{dN}{dt}\big)_{max}=rK/4=0.1\times500/4=$ **12.5 butterflies per unit time**.

> [!question] D8 (3) · (a) Two ways the drainage basin (the area whose runoff flows into a lake) influences eutrophication. (b) How does mapping the nutrient distribution of agricultural fields help?

> [!example]- Solution ([[EE06 Eutrophication and Alternative Stable States]], [[Cultural Eutrophication]], [[Trophic State of Lakes]])
> (a) Any two:
> - **catchment size relative to lake volume**: a large basin delivers more nutrient load per unit lake;
> - **land use**: agriculture with fertiliser and manure, or towns with sewage, against forest;
> - **soils and geology**: P-rich rocks and erodible soils;
> - **slope and vegetation cover**: erosion carries particle-bound P.
>
> (b) Fertiliser can be applied **only where and as much as the soil lacks** (precision application), so the surplus that washes into streams and lakes falls. The external load is cut at its source: prevention, the "best method" of the restoration lecture.
