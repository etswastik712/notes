# Population Thinking

<span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟡 **extra · book:** the whole note comes from Mayr's *Growth of Biological Thought* ([[Extract from Growth of Biological Thought_Mayr.pdf#page=25|PDF p.25–27]]), a *detailed reading* the instructor marks as not examinable. It explains *why* the course treats the **population** as the unit.

> [!intuition] The average family has 2.1 children
> No family has 2.1 children. The mean is a statistic about a collection of unique families, not a "typical" family that the real ones imperfectly copy. Biology, said Mayr, has to think the same way about every species.

> [!key] Definition
> **Essentialism (typology):** each kind has a fixed essence; variation is imperfect copying of the type; the average is the reality. **Population thinking:** every individual is unique; the population of unique individuals is real, and the mean is an abstraction. Variation is the raw material of evolution, not noise.

- Roots: the animal breeders, who saw that every animal in a herd has different heritable traits, and the naturalists, who found that no two specimens of a "species" are alike (Darwin's barnacles).
- Why it matters for ecology: populations have **distributions** of age, size and trait values, so growth, extinction and selection depend on the whole distribution, not on one "typical" individual. See [[EE02 Structured Population Models]] (age/stage structure) and [[Jensen Inequality]] (the average of a function is not the function of the average).

> [!pitfall] Typological slips
> "The wildebeest travels 1,500 km" is a population mean (Hopcraft: 1,550 km per year on average, up to 58 km in a single day). Quote the spread, not only the type value.
