# Anti-Predator Behaviour and Chemical Defence

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** behavioural (catalepsis, intimidation, group living) and chemical strategies ([[05_01_Sep_2026_Predation.pdf#page=22|slide 22]])

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Beyond looks: play dead, startle, hide in a crowd, or make yourself too smelly or poisonous to eat.

> [!key] Definition
> **Behavioural:** *catalepsis* (playing dead: opossums, Costa Rican katydids), *intimidation displays* (startling moths), *group living* (watchfulness, mobbing, confusion; alarm calls, aphid alarm pheromones). **Chemical:** the prey is too toxic, smelly or distasteful (skunk, badger, bombardier beetle *Brachinus crepitans*). Toxins can poison a predator without saving the prey unless tied to warning colour.

## Group living in numbers, and chemistry
**Dilution:** with $n$ equally exposed individuals and one attack, each has risk $1/n$. **Many eyes:** if each individual detects an approaching predator with probability $p=0.1$, a group of $n$ detects it with probability $1-(1-p)^n$: 0.10 for $n=1$, 0.65 for 10, 0.99 for 50, allowing each to spend more time feeding. **Confusion** (a swirling group is hard to target), **mobbing** (birds attack a predator together) and **alarm calls and pheromones** (aphids release alarm pheromones) complete the benefits. Costs: more attention to the group, competition for food, disease.

> [!example] The behavioural list on the slides
> *Catalepsis*: playing dead (opossum *Didelphis virginiana*; Costa Rican katydids); *intimidation display* (a moth startles the predator long enough to escape: eyespots, flashes of colour); *group living* (watchfulness, mobbing, confusion; alarm calls). *Chemical*: skunk and badger spray; the bombardier beetle *Brachinus crepitans*, whose hot spray (hydroquinones and hydrogen peroxide reacting in a reaction chamber) is ejected at about boiling temperature (background knowledge, not on the slide).

> [!pitfall] A poison does not protect the victim
> The slide: "toxins can poison the predator, but this often does not save the prey. It needs to be associated with aposematic colouration." An individual eaten is not saved; the benefit goes to its relatives and neighbours through predator learning ([[Aposematism]]). Group living and predator satiation can reduce per-capita loss to the point where a predator is overwhelmed: [[Predator Satiation]] (synchronous calving, mass emergence of cicadas).
