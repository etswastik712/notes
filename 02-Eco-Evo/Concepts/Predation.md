# Predation

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** the definition, the reasons for studying it, and the five types ([[05_01_Sep_2026_Predation.pdf#page=2|slide 2]])

> [!intuition] In one picture
> One organism's meal is another's death: the strongest and fastest-acting selective force on both parties.

> [!key] Definition
> **Predation** is the process by which an organism eats all or part of another organism. Types: **herbivory** (animals eat plants, sometimes partially), **carnivory** (animal or plant eats animal), **parasitoidism** (eggs laid on or near a host that the offspring later kill), **parasitism** (lives on or in the host, depends on it, does not consume it), **cannibalism** (same species). Reasons to study it: economic, ecological, evolutionary.

## Five kinds, one family of models
The slide's types differ in *what happens to the victim*. **Carnivory** and **parasitoidism** kill (the parasitoid larvae eventually kill the host); **herbivory** and **parasitism** usually consume part without killing at once; **cannibalism** has predator and prey of one species, so it is also a form of density dependence ([[Density Dependence]]). Examples: moose browsing willow (herbivory), lynx taking hare, a pitcher plant digesting an insect (carnivory by a plant), a wasp laying eggs in a caterpillar (parasitoid), a tick on a deer (parasite), tadpoles eating smaller tadpoles (cannibalism).

> [!example] What a predator does to prey numbers
> In the Lotka-Volterra model the prey are removed at $pHP$ per unit time. With $p=0.02$, $H=50$, $P=50$ that is $0.02\times50\times50=50$ prey per unit time, balancing a prey growth $rH=50$ at $r=1$: the equilibrium. Field experiments quantify it: Krebs et al. found that excluding mammalian predators doubled hare density, and adding food tripled it ([[Predator-Prey Cycles]]).

**Why study it.** Economics (pest and resource management), ecology (community organisation, conservation) and evolution: the interaction is one of the strongest selective forces on both sides, giving the arms race of [[Aposematism]], [[Crypsis]] and [[Mimicry]]. Prey also defend by being numerous ([[Predator Satiation]]).

> [!pitfall] Predator and prey are not symmetric
> A prey individual that fails loses its life; a predator that fails loses a meal ("the life-dinner principle"). That asymmetry is one reason prey defences are often more elaborate than predator offences. Models treat the interaction as one term; what *feeds* it (the functional response, [[Functional and Numerical Responses]]) is a separate question.
