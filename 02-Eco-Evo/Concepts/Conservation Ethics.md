# Conservation Ethics

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** the three historical ethics and the summary slide ([[07_Conservation Biology.pdf#page=4|slide 4]])

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Three answers to 'why bother?': nature is sacred (Muir), nature is a resource to use wisely (Pinchot), people are members of the land community (Leopold).

> [!key] Definition
> **Preservation** (John Muir, romantic-transcendental): communion with nature is morally superior to destroying it; conclusion: preserve. **Resource conservation** (Gifford Pinchot, after Dietrich Brandis): the greatest good for the greatest number for the longest time ('Bahujan Hitay, Bahujan Sukhai'); conclusion: economic management. **Land ethic** (Aldo Leopold): 'a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community'; conclusion: conserve.

## The three positions side by side
| | Muir | Pinchot | Leopold |
|---|---|---|---|
| value of nature | intrinsic, spiritual | instrumental: resource for people | membership in a community |
| human role | visitor | manager and user | plain member and citizen of the land community |
| policy | strict preservation (the National Parks bill of 1899: Yosemite, Sequoia) | sustained yield, "greatest good for the greatest number for the longest time" | conserve: use and manage while respecting the integrity of the community |

Pinchot was a protégé of Dietrich Brandis, the first Inspector General of Forests in India; the slide's Indian phrase *Bahujan Hitay, Bahujan Sukhai* ("for the benefit and happiness of the many") renders Pinchot's principle.

> [!example] One reserve, three readings
> **Chitwan (Nepal):** local people may cut thatching grass for ten days a year (Hunter and Gibbs). A *Muir* reading objects (human use in a reserve); a *Pinchot* reading welcomes it (a resource used sustainably, goodwill for the park); a *Leopold* reading asks whether the harvest harms the community (here the cost is small logs stolen for firewood). **Project Tiger** shows all three: core areas closed (preserve), buffer zones with regulated use (resource), corridors and prey (community) ([[Project Tiger]]).

> [!pitfall] Ethics do not give numbers
> They tell you what to value, not how big a reserve or population should be; the biological rules ([[Rabinowitz Rarity Classification]], [[Minimum Viable Population]], [[Reserve Design and Management]]) do that. Modern debate adds *ecosystem services* (instrumental value of intact ecosystems) and rights-based and justice arguments (who bears the costs: local people near reserves).
