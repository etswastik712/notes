# Passenger Pigeon Extinction

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** the 'complicated story': six candidate causes ([[07_Conservation Biology.pdf#page=12|slide 12]])  ·  🟡 **extra · book:** Bucher (1992), *Current Ornithology* (paper tier); Murray et al. (2017), *Science* 358: 951-954 (genomics)

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Hunting killed billions, but the last commercial catch was in 1878 and the last bird died in 1914. Other pigeons rebound after hunting; this one did not. Why?

> [!key] Definition
> Candidate causes (slide): (1) climatic change and catastrophes (no records); (2) human hunting (part of the story, not all); (3) disease and parasites (no records); (4) competition with domesticated species (unlikely); (5) **habitat alteration with social facilitation** in food finding (forest cutting cut food, made worse by dependence on flock-mates); (6) genetic issues (Murray et al. 2017). The slide stresses that how reduced genetic diversity mattered is unknown.

## Putting Bucher's argument into a model
Timeline (slides 11–14): the last bird died in 1914; commercial hunting ceased to pay after 1878; the species did not rebound as other pigeons do. Slide assessment of the six causes: climate (no records), hunting (part), disease (no records), competition with domesticates (unlikely), habitat alteration + social facilitation in food finding (plausible), genetics (Murray et al. 2017; role unknown).

> [!example] Why hunting plus a threshold is enough (toy)
> Take the strong-Allee model of [[Allee Effect]] with threshold $A$: for $N<A$ growth is negative even with *no* hunting. Heavy hunting moves $N$ below $A$; stopping the hunt at $N<A$ does not bring recovery. In Bucher's picture the threshold is high because the pigeon was a **nomadic mast specialist** whose food patches are irregular in space and time and large relative to a flock's daily range. Social facilitation (following flock-mates to patches) makes the per-capita finding rate fall with flock size, so $A$ is big and the process has a positive feedback: fewer birds, worse food finding, still fewer birds. Deforestation and introduced domestic pigs (eating mast) reduced patch size and density; "minimal viable size was reached when the bird was still relatively common".

**Other pointers.** The decline was "precipitous" from 1871 to 1880; remnant flocks nested in Wisconsin and Pennsylvania in the 1880s; one of the last solitary birds was found among domestic pigeons. Compare the Eared Dove (colonies of millions in Argentina): a living analogue.

> [!pitfall] A single cause is the wrong question
> The slide's list is a list of *hypotheses* assessed on evidence, several of which may act together; "hunting killed it" fits the numbers of the 19th century but not the 36 years after 1878. Genetic issues remain uncertain: slide 14 answers "We have no idea" about how reduced diversity mattered.
