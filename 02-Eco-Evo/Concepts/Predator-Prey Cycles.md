# Predator-Prey Cycles

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** Elton's (1924) fur-return data and the question 'intrinsic or extrinsic?', and the three-part puzzle on the reflection slide ([[05_01_Sep_2026_Predation.pdf#page=4|slide 4]])

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Prey and predator numbers rise and fall in regular, lagged waves: more prey, then more predators, then fewer prey, then fewer predators.

> [!key] Definition
> Regular oscillations in prey and predator densities in the Hudson's Bay Company fur returns (Elton 1924). Are they **intrinsic** (generated by the predator-prey interaction) or **extrinsic** (driven by weather, food)? The puzzle: (1) real populations cycle; (2) the unrealistic Lotka-Volterra model cycles; (3) realistic Lotka-Volterra models do not.

## Data, hypotheses and the experiment
Elton's (1924) Hudson's Bay Company fur returns give lynx and snowshoe hare series from about 1850 to 1930 with peaks roughly every ten years, the lynx following the hare. Molles lists the hypotheses: **sunspots** (Elton; rejected by MacLulich 1937 and Moran 1949); **overpopulation** (disease, physiological stress at high density, starvation); **predation**. Keith concluded that none alone explains the cycle and called for long-term team studies.

> [!example] Krebs et al. (1995): do effects add or interact?
> Nine 1-km$^2$ boreal-forest blocks over 8 years: control 1; predators reduced 2; food added 3; both 11; fertiliser (plants only) no change. If the two factors were independent and additive the combined effect would be $1+(2-1)+(3-1)=4$; if multiplicative $2\times3=6$. The observed 11 shows **synergy**: the cycle involves three trophic levels (hares, their food, their predators). Molles also notes that lynx and coyote numbers rose six- to sevenfold after hare numbers rose (a numerical response), and that hare densities can fluctuate 10- to 30-fold (up to 100-fold in places).

**Intrinsic or extrinsic?** The experiment shows the cycle is intrinsic to the food-hare-predator system and not driven by an external clock; a two-species Lotka-Volterra model is too simple to account for it quantitatively ([[Lotka-Volterra Predator-Prey Model]]).

> [!pitfall] Correlation and lag
> Lynx tracking hare is what a predator should do, but the *same* pattern arises if a third factor drives both; and lynx records are pelts trapped, which depend on effort and prices. Slide 4's question stays open at the level of the data; the field experiment is what separates the hypotheses.
