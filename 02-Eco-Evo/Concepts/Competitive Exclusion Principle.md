# Competitive Exclusion Principle

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** Gause's principle as the slide states it: no two species with the same niche can coexist ([[04-Competition_25-Aug_2026.pdf#page=7|slide 7]])  ·  🔵 **extra · beyond:** the 'n species need at least n limiting factors' form

> [!intuition] In one picture
> If two species want exactly the same thing, the one that is even slightly better at getting it will eventually take it all.

> [!key] Definition
> **Gause's principle:** two species with the same niche cannot coexist at constant population sizes; the better competitor eliminates the other. In resource terms, $n$ species can coexist only if there are at least $n$ independent limiting factors (compare the single-resource case, [[Tilman R-Star Rule]]).

## Statements and the test
Gause (1934) showed that *Paramecium aurelia* and *P. caudatum* each reach a plateau when grown alone, but in mixed culture *P. aurelia* continues to rise and *P. caudatum* declines (slide 7). The modern, sharper statement: **$n$ species cannot coexist at constant sizes on fewer than $n$ limiting factors**. For one resource this is the $R^*$ rule ([[Tilman R-Star Rule]]); for the competition equations it is the case $\alpha_{12}\alpha_{21}>1$ or one-sided dominance ([[Lotka-Volterra Competition Model]]).

> [!example] Two ways to coexist anyway
> *Use different resources* (two limiting factors for two species): *P. caudatum* and *P. bursaria* coexist in Gause's second experiment because one feeds in the top layers of the culture and the other in the bottom layers ([[Niche Partitioning]]). *Change the conditions:* Park's flour beetles swap winners as temperature and humidity change, so neither is permanently excluded.

> [!pitfall] What the principle does not say
> "Same niche" is only meaningful if the niche is defined ([[Ecological Niche]]); the principle applies to a *constant environment* and stable sizes. Real communities appear to violate it: Hutchinson's "paradox of the plankton" asks how dozens of phytoplankton species coexist on a handful of nutrients; the answer given today is that the environment fluctuates and exclusion is slow, so equilibrium is never reached. Competitive exclusion also explains invasions: an invader with a lower $R^*$ excludes natives ([[Invasive Species]]).

The principle is a *prediction* of a model (that is why it is testable), not a law of nature.
