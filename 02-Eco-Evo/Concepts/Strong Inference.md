# Strong Inference

<span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟡 **extra · book:** from Pigliucci (2002) ([[Pigliucci_Philosophy of Ecology.pdf#page=5|PDF p.5–6]]), a *detailed reading*. It is not examinable; it names the method that the case-study papers in [[EE01 Introduction to Biology and Ecological Thinking]] actually follow.

> [!intuition] Twenty questions
> You do not win twenty questions by asking "is it a dog?" over and over. You win by asking questions whose answers split the remaining possibilities in half. Strong inference is the same: design the test so that each outcome kills some hypotheses.

> [!key] Platt's four steps (1964)
> 1. Devise **several** (not just two) alternative hypotheses.
> 2. Devise a crucial experiment with alternative outcomes, each excluding one or more hypotheses.
> 3. Carry it out as cleanly as possible.
> 4. Recycle: make sub-hypotheses to refine what remains.

- Roots: Bacon's *New Organon*, Chamberlin's **method of multiple working hypotheses** (1897), Popper's falsification. It addresses *under-determination*: one data set can fit many theories.
- Danger it guards against: falling in love with one theory. With only a hypothesis and its "null", rejecting the null wins the alternative by default.
- **Limits** (Pigliucci, after Lakatos): a result falsifies only the whole bundle *hypothesis + auxiliary assumptions + methods*, so a clean kill is rarer in ecology than in molecular biology. Hence the move towards weighing evidence rather than a binary reject: [[Bayes' Theorem]] and [[Akaike Information Criterion]].

> [!example] Where you can see it in the case study
> Holdo et al. compared **14 rival movement rules** at once; Hopcraft et al. eliminated explanations by a chain of comparisons (relatives, residents, zebra). Neither frames the question as "forage versus a null". See [[Serengeti Wildebeest Migration]].
