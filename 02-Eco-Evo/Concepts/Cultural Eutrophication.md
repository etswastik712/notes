# Cultural Eutrophication

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** the chain: nutrient (esp. P) enrichment, algal blooms, loss of light, oxygen loss in the hypolimnion, P release, fish kills; the global phosphorus numbers ([[06_04-Sep-2026_Biotic-Abiotic Interactions.pdf#page=7|slide 7]])

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Feed a lake and it turns green: more algae, then more dead algae, then less oxygen, then dead fish.

> [!key] Definition
> **Cultural eutrophication** is human-driven nutrient enrichment (fertiliser, domestic and industrial waste). Chain: P enrichment, blooms (the cyanobacteria *Anabaena*, *Microcystis*), less light, less dissolved oxygen especially in the hypolimnion (anoxic reactions that release more P, [[Internal Phosphorus Loading]]), higher pH, fish kills (Caspian Sea 2003). Globally about 1.5 Tg P per year is discharged into fresh water (China 30 %, India 8 %, USA 7 %, Spain and Brazil 6 % each; Mekonnen and Hoekstra 2018).

## Loading, ratios and consequences
Human-driven enrichment comes from sewage (domestic sewage has an N:P ratio of about 4:1, so phosphorus-rich relative to need), phosphorus detergents (dominant in Lake Constance's loading before treatment), fertiliser and erosion; nitrogen also arrives from the atmosphere. Phosphorus, "unlike nitrogen, is relatively immobile once it is in the soil", but floods can carry fertiliser phosphorus to lakes.

> [!example] A global budget (slide 21)
> About 1.5 Tg P a year (1 Tg = $10^9$ kg) enter fresh waters: China 30 % = 0.45 Tg, India 8 % = 0.12 Tg, USA 7 % = 0.105 Tg, Spain and Brazil 6 % each = 0.09 Tg (Mekonnen and Hoekstra 2018). **Ratio check:** the Redfield N:P is 16 by atoms ($\approx7.2$ by mass); sewage at about 4:1 (the reading does not say by mass or by atoms) is phosphorus-rich relative to need, so enriched lakes can shift from P to N limitation, and nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (*Anabaena*, *Aphanizomenon*) are then favoured ([[Nutrient Limitation]]).

**Consequences** (slides 6–8, Lampert and Sommer): blooms of cyanobacteria (also *Microcystis*, *Planktothrix*; some strains toxic), less light, periphyton overgrowing macrophytes, hypolimnetic oxygen consumption, anoxic release of phosphorus (internal loading, [[Internal Phosphorus Loading]]), pH rise and fish kills (summer: pH-driven ammonia toxicity; winter: oxygen use under ice). The Caspian Sea (2003) shows it is not only a small-lake problem.

> [!pitfall] Cutting loading is necessary, not sufficient
> After external loading falls, lakes lag for years (water renewal, sediment P, N or light limitation at the peak, planktivorous fish) and shallow lakes may stay turbid because of hysteresis ([[Alternative Stable States]]). Restoration: [[Lake Restoration Methods]].
