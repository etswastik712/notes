# Project Tiger

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** the case study from the 1972 baseline to the 2018 estimate ([[07_Conservation Biology.pdf#page=17|slide 17]])

> [!intuition] In one picture
> A national conservation effort that worked, then partly unravelled, then was rebuilt: a test of 'science, common sense, economics and politics' together.

> [!key] Definition
> Tigers in India: about 40-45 thousand early in the 20th century; **1,827** in 1972. **Project Tiger** was launched on 1 April 1973 with 9 reserves covering about 13,017 km² (Bandipur, Corbett, Kanha, Manas, Melghat, Palamau, Ranthambhore, Similipal, Sundarbans). Numbers rose to over 4,000 in 11 years (a questioned figure), then reversals (political will, encroachment, prey loss, poaching, funds). 2006: National Tiger Conservation Authority and Wildlife Crime Control Bureau. Estimates: 1,411 (2008), about 1,706 (2011), **2,967** (2018; 2,603-3,346). About 70 % of the world's wild tigers live in India.

## Effort versus outcome, with the numbers
| year | event or count |
|---|---|
| early 1900s | 40–45 thousand tigers in India |
| 1972 | 1,827 |
| 1 Apr 1973 | Project Tiger launched; 9 reserves, 13,017 km² |
| ~1984 | "more than 4,000" in 11 years (questioned) |
| 2006 | NTCA and Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (amendment of the Wild Life (Protection) Act) |
| 2007 | more than 40 reserves, 37,761 km² |
| 2008, 2011 | 1,411; 1,706 |
| 2012 | Supreme Court bans tourists in core areas |
| 2018 | 2,967 (2,603–3,346); 2,461 unique photographs |

> [!example] Growth rates and cautions
> 1,827 to 4,000 over 11 years: $\ln(4000/1827)/11=0.071$ per year; $1411\to1706$ (2008–2011): 6.3 % per year; $1706\to2967$ (2011–2018): 7.9 % per year; the 2018 interval is $-12\,\%/+13\,\%$. These come from different survey methods (pugmarks versus camera traps), so they are not one time series. Reserve area rose $\times2.9$ (1973–2007); the 2018 count is about 7 % of the early-20th-century figure.
> **Design:** site criteria (undisturbed, high prey and predator numbers, no mining or timber, spread across states), core and buffer areas, corridors, no cattle or forest harvest, patrolling. **Reversals:** political will under farmer pressure, encroachment of buffers, prey loss, poaching, funds; Sariska lost its tigers; Madhya Pradesh lost 65 % in the period cited.

**Global and local.** About 70 % of the world's wild tigers are in India; CITES (1973) regulates trade. Human-wildlife conflict: elephants and railways, and **lemon trees in Assam** as an elephant-repellent barrier.

> [!pitfall] The four-things lesson
> The slide's "science, common sense, economics, politics" has to converge: design (science) and surveys ([[Tiger Population Surveys]]) are necessary but the reversals were political and economic. Counting reserves measures *effort*; counts of animals measure *outcome*. Related: [[Reserve Design and Management]], [[Conservation Ethics]], [[Rabinowitz Rarity Classification]].
