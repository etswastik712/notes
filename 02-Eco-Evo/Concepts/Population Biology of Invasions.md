# Population Biology of Invasions

<span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟡 **extra · book:** Sakai et al. (2001), *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics* 32: 305-332 (paper tier, 'a nice review of the basic biology of invasive species')

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Whether an arriving species takes hold is a population question: how many arrived, how many were born and died, and how much genetic variety they carried.

> [!key] Definition
> A review of the **population-level processes** that decide whether an introduced species establishes and spreads: number and size of introductions (propagule pressure), demographic and genetic stochasticity and Allee effects in small founding populations, adaptation after arrival, and the invasibility of the receiving community.

## What Sakai et al. (2001) add
The review (15 authors, *Annu. Rev. Ecol. Syst.* 32: 305–332) treats an invasion as a sequence of steps, each with its own population-biological questions: *why do some species become invasive and others do not*, what limits establishment, what drives spread, what role does evolution play. Sections and claims, from the paper:
- **Life-history traits.** Baker's "ideal weed" (sexual and asexual reproduction, fast growth, plasticity), largely untested; broad analyses show partial patterns (a discriminant model classified 86 % of woody invaders correctly using traits such as vegetative reproduction; *r*-selected life histories are common among colonists); Newsome and Noble's "gap grabbers, competitors, survivors, swampers".
- **Introduction and establishment.** Mostly human-mediated (ballast water, crop seed, pet trade, forestry). Isolated individuals that self-fertilise, or have several modes of reproduction, are good colonists.
- **Genetics.** A founding **bottleneck** reduces diversity (introduced Argentine ants are less diverse than native ones), risking inbreeding depression and limiting adaptation; **lag times** between arrival and spread may be time for adaptive evolution.
- **Invasibility** is an emergent community property (species interactions, mutualisms, disturbance, fragmentation); models of range expansion predict spread.
- **Management.** Demographic models identify the life stage at which control is most effective ([[Sensitivity and Elasticity of Growth Rate]] and [[Lefkovitch Stage-Structured Matrix]]).

> [!example] Small founders are fragile
> A founding group of $N_0$ with $b=1.1,\ d=1.0$ goes extinct with probability $(d/b)^{N_0}$: 0.91 for one founder, 0.39 for ten, 0.0085 for fifty. This is why invasion success rises with the number of individuals released ([[Demographic Genetic and Environmental Stochasticity]], [[Allee Effect]]).
