# Mimicry

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** the two broad types, with the drone fly and *Heliconius* butterflies ([[05_01_Sep_2026_Predation.pdf#page=20|slide 20]])

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Look like something predators already avoid. In one form you bluff (Batesian); in the other you really are unpalatable and share the signal (Müllerian).

> [!key] Definition
> **Mimicry:** attempts by one species to resemble another. **Batesian:** the mimic sends the model's signals but lacks the attribute (e.g. unpalatability) that makes it unprofitable (drone fly mimicking a honey bee). **Müllerian:** two or more species share a similar warning signal and genuine defences (*Heliconius* butterflies), so a predator confusing them kills fewer of each.

## Batesian versus Müllerian, and frequency dependence
**Batesian**: a palatable mimic resembles an unpalatable model (a drone fly mimicking a honey bee). **Müllerian**: several unpalatable species share a signal (*Heliconius* butterflies) and share the education cost ([[Aposematism]]). The signal's information to the predator is its reliability: the fraction $q$ of signal-bearers that are really distasteful.

> [!example] When is a Batesian mimic safe?
> A predator gains $G$ from a palatable mimic and loses $C$ when it eats a model. Expected gain per attack on a signal-bearer: $(1-q)G-qC$; it attacks if this is positive, i.e. if $q<G/(G+C)$. With $G=C$ the predator attacks once fewer than **half** of signal-bearers are distasteful. A Batesian mimic is therefore protected only while it is **rarer than the model** (negative frequency dependence); as it becomes common, protection decays and selection favours imperfect or polymorphic mimics. In Müllerian mimicry all signal-bearers are noxious ($q=1$) and each added species lowers the risk per individual (1.5 % instead of 3 % for two equal species in the arithmetic of [[Aposematism]]).

**Evolution.** Bates (1862) described the mimicry of palatable butterflies; Müller (1878) explained the benefit of shared warning signals mathematically; modern work adds imperfect mimicry and the role of predator learning and memory.

> [!pitfall] Resemblance is not the test
> Two species can look alike by common ancestry or by convergence without any predator-mediated selection; the evidence needed is that predators discriminate by the signal. Also "mimicry" in the slides' sense (resembling another species) differs from crypsis (resembling the background) though the concealment of a masquerader overlaps ([[Crypsis]]).
