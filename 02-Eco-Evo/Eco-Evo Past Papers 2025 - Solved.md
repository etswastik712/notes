> [!question] Q3 (2) · 46 rabbits marked and released; next week 37 caught, 27 of them marked. Best estimate of population size?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Mark-Recapture Estimation]])
> Lincoln–Petersen: $\hat N=\dfrac{MC}{R}=\dfrac{46\times37}{27}=$ **63**. (Chapman's less biased version: $\frac{47\cdot38}{28}-1=62.8$.)

> [!question] Q9 (3) · Sediment dredging is the most common lake-restoration method. What is it, how does it help, and what are its problems?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Lake Restoration Methods]], [[Internal Phosphorus Loading]])
> **What:** removing the nutrient-rich upper sediment layer (by suction or excavation). **Why it helps:** after the external load is cut, the P stored in the mud keeps feeding the water (internal loading, especially when the sediment surface goes anoxic or is resuspended). Dredging **removes the stock** permanently and deepens shallow lakes. **Problems:** expensive; needs **disposal sites** for large volumes of (possibly toxic) mud; resuspends nutrients and contaminants during the work; destroys the benthic community temporarily; pointless if the external load continues.

> [!question] Q11 (4) · LV competition: species 1 ($r_1=1.1$, $K_1=250$, $\alpha_{12}=1.4$), species 2 ($r_2=0.76$, $K_2=1500$, $\alpha_{21}=0.9$). Outcome, with isoclines and vectors.

> [!example]- Solution ([[Lotka-Volterra Competition Model]])
> Species 1's isocline: $(250,0)$–$(0,250/1.4=178.6)$. Species 2's isocline: $(1500/0.9=1667,0)$–$(0,1500)$. **Species 2's isocline lies entirely outside species 1's** on both axes, so **species 2 wins** from any start, ending at $(0,1500)$. Vectors: between the isoclines $N_1$ decreases and $N_2$ increases; inside both, both increase; outside both, both decrease.

## End-semester (27 Nov 2025)

> [!question] Q1 (3 × 4) · Distinguish: (A) mutation-accumulation and antagonistic-pleiotropy theories of ageing; (B) the complexifying and adaptive forces in Lamarck's theory; (C) overdominance and underdominance.

> [!example]- Solution ([[EE08 Evolution - An Introduction]], [[EE10 Natural Selection]])
> (A) **MA** (Medawar): the force of selection weakens with age. Deleterious mutations that act only late in life are hardly selected against and accumulate by mutation and drift; ageing is their combined effect. It predicts increased *genetic variance* in late-life fitness traits. **AP** (Williams): alleles with **early benefits and late costs** are *actively favoured*; ageing is a by-product of selection for early fitness. It predicts **negative genetic correlations** between early and late fitness (the trade-off seen in selection experiments). (General knowledge; not in this year's slides.)
> (B) In Lamarck's theory (EE08, slides 36–40): the **complexifying force** drives lineages *up a master plan* towards greater complexity over generations. It explains the diversity and graded similarity of life (lineages started at different times by spontaneous generation are at different rungs). The **adaptive force** fits organisms to their **local environment** through **use and disuse** and the inheritance of acquired characters. It explains variation within groups and adaptation.
> (C) See [[Eco-Evo Past Papers 2024 - Solved|2024]] ES Q1c (stable polymorphism against an unstable equilibrium).

> [!question] Q2 (2) · A technologically advanced country with low birth and death rates has the pyramid shown: a narrow base widening into a bulge of middle-aged adults (urn-shaped). An analyst predicts a boom in 20 years from a large demographic dividend (a high ratio of working-age to non-working population). Agree?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Age Pyramid]])
> **Disagree.** The bulge is in the working and middle ages *now*. In 20 years those cohorts will be **old (non-working)**. The narrow base means the cohorts entering working age then will be **smaller**. The ratio of workers to dependants will fall: an ageing population, a demographic *burden*. A dividend comes from a pyramid with a wide young base *after* fertility falls.

> [!question] Q3 (2) · A species with an extensive range and broad habitat tolerance but consistently small local populations survives a localised catastrophe. Two *intrinsic* vulnerabilities that can still drive it extinct?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Rabinowitz Rarity Classification]], [[Demographic Genetic and Environmental Stochasticity]], [[Allee Effect]])
> This is Rabinowitz's "everywhere but sparse" type (the tiger). Because every local population is small:
> - **demographic stochasticity**: chance runs of deaths or a skewed sex ratio can end each local group;
> - **genetic erosion**: drift, loss of variation, **inbreeding depression**;
> - **Allee effects**: mates hard to find at low density;
> - often **large body, slow reproduction and big area needs**, so recovery is slow and every individual counts.

> [!question] Q4 (2) · Rohu fish: state A has a *minimum-size* limit (only fish above a length may be caught, sparing immature juveniles); state B has a *slot* limit (only fish within a length range; the same lower limit). Maturity at 3–5 years. Which is more sustainable?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Sensitivity and Elasticity of Growth Rate]], [[Leslie Matrix]])
> **The slot limit (B).**
> - It protects the **largest, oldest fish**, which in fish have by far the **highest fecundity** (egg number rises steeply with body size) and often better egg quality: the stages to which $\lambda$ is most sensitive.
> - A minimum-size limit removes exactly those breeders and **selects for early maturation at small size** (fisheries-induced evolution), lowering future productivity.
> - The slot keeps a reserve of big spawners and the age structure intact.

> [!question] Q5 (2) · A locus with 4 alleles in HWE: maximum combined heterozygote frequency?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Allele and Genotype Frequencies]])
> $H=1-\sum p_i^2$ is maximised at equal frequencies ($p_i=\tfrac14$, as $p=q=\tfrac12$ for 2 alleles): $H_{max}=1-4\cdot\tfrac1{16}=$ **0.75** (in general $1-1/n$).

> [!question] Q6 (3) · If Kelvin's few-million-year age of the Earth had been right, why would that be a problem for Darwin's theory but not necessarily a falsification of Lamarck's?

> [!example]- Solution ([[EE08 Evolution - An Introduction]] §5, [[Blending Inheritance]])
> Darwin's mechanism is **slow, gradual and undirected**: selection accumulating small random variations over very many generations. Producing the diversity of life that way needs **vast time** (Darwin assumed hundreds of millions to billions of years). A few million years would be too short, which is the second objection in the EE08 table, later answered by radiometric dating. Lamarck's mechanism is **directed**: the complexifying force drives lineages up the plan, and the adaptive force works **within each lifetime** through use and disuse, with gains inherited. Change is purposeful and fast, so it does not need deep time; a short history does not contradict it.

> [!question] Q7 (3) · A tiger reserve surrounded by semi-urban settlements and farmland: a circle or a five-pointed star of equal area?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Reserve Design and Management]])
> **The circle.** For a given area it has the **least perimeter (edge)**: a star of the same area has far more boundary, and long arms that are narrow and almost all edge. Edge is where poaching, livestock grazing, fires, dogs and disease enter and where **human–tiger conflict** occurs (tigers ranging out to kill cattle). A circle also has the largest **interior (core) far from any edge** for breeding tigers, and is cheaper to fence and patrol. (Diamond's principle "as circular as possible".)

> [!question] Q8 (3) · One locus, two alleles, HWE, the two homozygotes equally frequent. Probability that two random individuals have exactly the same genotype?

> [!example]- Solution
> $p^2=q^2$ ⇒ $p=q=\tfrac12$: genotype frequencies 0.25, 0.5, 0.25. $P(\text{same})=0.25^2+0.5^2+0.25^2=$ **0.375**.

> [!question] Q9 (3) · Serious degradation of a reserve's buffer zone can threaten the long-term viability of the **tiger** population even if the core stays untouched. How?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Project Tiger]], [[Reserve Design and Management]], [[Metapopulation]])
> - **Tigers need huge areas.** Cores are often too small for a viable population, so the buffer provides foraging range and a **prey source**.
> - **Dispersing subadults** (especially young males pushed out by residents) live in and move through the buffer. Without it they die in conflict or on roads, or cannot reach other reserves. Populations become **isolated** (no gene flow, inbreeding) and lose their recruitment.
> - A degraded buffer brings people, cattle and dogs **to the core's edge**: more conflict and retaliatory killing, poaching access, livestock disease.
> - Prey that move seasonally between core and buffer decline.

> [!question] Q10 (3) · The "programmed death" theory says ageing is adaptive, evolved to weed out old individuals for the good of the species. What evolutionary logic does it violate, and what captive-versus-wild observation contradicts it?

> [!example]- Solution ([[Natural Selection]])
> **Logic:** it relies on **group selection** ("good of the species"). Selection acts on individuals' genes: an allele that killed its carrier early would be outcompeted by a "cheater" allele that kept its carrier alive and reproducing, so programmed death is not evolutionarily stable. It is also **circular**: it assumes old individuals are already worn out, i.e. that ageing already exists. **Observation:** in the **wild** almost no animals live long enough to age; they die of predation, disease and accidents first. Senescence is seen mainly in **captivity** (zoos, labs, pets). If ageing existed to remove old individuals in nature, it would have to act there, but in the wild there are hardly any old individuals for it to remove, so selection could never have shaped it for that role.

> [!question] Q11 (5) · About 6000 wildebeest drown each year crossing the Mara. A foundation builds bridges so none drown; nothing else changes. Effects on the Serengeti's groups of organisms over the next 10–15 years, with qualitative magnitudes.

> [!example]- Solution ([[EE01 Introduction to Biology and Ecological Thinking]] §2.4, [[Serengeti Wildebeest Migration]])
> - **Wildebeest: minor.** 6000 out of ~1.2–1.3 million is ~0.5 % a year. The migratory herd is **food-limited** in the dry season (bottom-up), so the saved animals are soon offset by density-dependent starvation. A slight transient rise at most.
> - **The Mara river ecosystem: major.** Subalusky et al. (2017) showed the annual drownings (≈ 1100 t of carcass a year) are a large **nutrient and food subsidy**: fish feed on the soft tissue, and the **bones release phosphorus for years**. Losing it means fewer fish and invertebrates, and lower river productivity downstream (and in Lake Victoria's inflow).
> - **Nile crocodiles: moderate to major.** They depend on crossings for a large part of their annual intake, so condition and recruitment fall.
> - **Scavengers (vultures, marabou storks): moderate.** They lose a predictable pulse of carcasses.
> - **Predators on land (lions, hyenas): minor.** Their prey base is unchanged.
> - **Grassland and fire: minor.** A 0.5 % change in grazing is negligible.
