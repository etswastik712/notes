## 1. Three burning questions
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=1|slides 1–2]]*

The first lecture opens with three complaints that nearly every student has:

1. Why does biology involve so much **mugging up**?
2. Why is it so **description-heavy**?
3. Why is there **no apparent structure**, "a few nice equations from which the rest can be derived"?

The slide promises an answer from the *philosophy of biology* and sets out a deliberate detour ([[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=2|slide 2]]): do not defend or attack the subject; ask instead **what biologists do and study, and what kinds of question they ask**. That leads to a *framework* (the levels of life, §3), then to some *peculiarities* of biological systems (§4), and from those the answers (§5). In between comes a long worked example, the wildebeest (§2), so that the abstractions have something to be about.

> [!intuition] Physics learns the rules of chess; biology studies one game that has been running for four billion years
> Once you know the rules of chess you can derive a great deal. Now suppose you were handed a single game in the middle of play, with a die rolled at every move, and asked to explain why the position is what it is. You would have to *describe* the pieces, *reconstruct* the history, and give *probabilistic* rules of thumb. That is much closer to ecology and evolution than a set of equations is. Sections 4–6 turn this picture into the four features and the two ways of coping.

## 2. A case study: the Serengeti wildebeest
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=3|slides 3–11]]* · <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> *Holdo 2009, Hopcraft 2015*

The wildebeest or gnu (*Connochaetes taurinus*) is introduced as an animal "to be studied in the context of …" its migration. Fact sheet, concept note and sources: [[Serengeti Wildebeest Migration]].

### 2.1 Two kinds of question
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=5|slides 5]] and [[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=7|7]]*

Slide 5 splits the migration into **two major kinds of question, *how* and *why***:

- **How** are they able to travel about 1,500 km a year? What is the *mechanism*?
- **Why** are they doing it? What is the *benefit*? Is there a benefit?

![[EE01 How and Why of the Migration.svg|720]]
*Figure EE1.1. Both questions about one behaviour. Blue boxes answer "how", green boxes "why"; the grey box is what the migration does to its surroundings (§2.5). Editable: [[EE01 How and Why of the Migration.excalidraw]].*

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): Mayr's names for the two questions
> The instructor's "how" and "why" are Ernst Mayr's **proximate** and **ultimate** causation (*detailed reading*, [[Extract from Growth of Biological Thought_Mayr.pdf#page=47|PDF p.47–48]]). His example is a warbler that leaves North America on 25 August. *Proximate:* day length has fallen below a threshold and the weather favours departure. *Ultimate:* an insect-eater that stayed would starve in winter, so selection favoured a genotype that migrates. A screech owl in the same wood sees the same days and the same weather but stays, because its genotype differs. Every biological phenomenon has both kinds of cause, because organisms (unlike rocks) carry a **genetic program**: proximate causes concern its *decoding* in one individual, ultimate causes the *changes of programs through time* and why they changed. Mayr calls the two enterprises the two biologies: **functional** (mechanism, usually experimental, "How?") and **evolutionary** (history, usually comparative, "Why?"). See [[Proximate and Ultimate Causation]].

> [!intuition] The kettle again
> "Why does the kettle whistle?" has two honest answers: steam forced through a slot (how it works now) and a designer who wanted a signal (why it exists). A living thing has both because its design was built by selection and is read out afresh in each life.

### 2.2 The "how": muscles, synchronous births, precocious calves
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=5|slides 5–7]]*

**Muscles.** Slide 5: wildebeest have "the most efficient muscles known among mammals": **62.6 %** efficiency, against **41.8 %** in cows and **34 %** in mice (Curtin et al. 2018, *Nature* 563, 393–396). *Efficiency* here is the fraction of the chemical energy used by the muscle that comes out as mechanical work; the rest leaves as heat.

> [!example] Worked example: what 62.6 % buys
> For a given mechanical work $W$ the muscle must burn $E=W/\eta$ and releases heat $E-W=W(1/\eta-1)$.
>
> | | $\eta$ | fuel per unit work | heat per unit work |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | wildebeest | 0.626 | 1.60 | **0.60** |
> | cow | 0.418 | 2.39 | 1.39 |
> | mouse | 0.340 | 2.94 | 1.94 |
>
> A cow needs **1.50×** the fuel of a wildebeest for the same work, a mouse 1.84×. The heat to be got rid of differs far more: **2.3×** (cow) and **3.3×** (mouse), because heat is what is *left over*. In a hot dry habitat, where shedding heat costs water, that is the point of the adaptation (`scripts/ee01_numbers.py`, §2).

> [!verify] What the 62.6 % is, and who was measured
> The numbers are copied from the slide. From the abstract and the Royal Veterinary College summary I could confirm that the 62.6 % comes from **muscle biopsies** (forelimb *flexor carpi ulnaris*) compared with the same muscle of cattle, and that the animals were **desert-dwelling wildebeest in northern Botswana** (up to 80 km in five days without drinking), not Serengeti migrants. The mouse value I could not check. So 62.6 % is a property of *muscle tissue*, not of the whole animal's cost of walking. This vault quotes the slide.

**Two other adaptations** (slide 5, then slide 7 after a video):

1. **Reproduction is highly synchronous, creating a glut for the predators.** Hopcraft et al. give the numbers: over **250,000 calves** are born in about **three weeks** from mid-February, about **80 %** of the year's calves ([[Hopcraft+_Why are Wildebeest the most abundant herbivore.pdf#page=5|PDF p.5]]). That is $250{,}000/21\approx11{,}900$ a day, or $\approx$ **500 an hour**. This is **predator satiation** ([[Predator Satiation]]; §2.6).
2. **Wildebeest babies are precocious.** A calf stands within about **6 minutes** and walks within **30**, and can outrun a hyena within a day; topi and hartebeest calves can take 30 minutes or more to stand, and hartebeest calves cannot keep up with their mothers until they are more than a week old ([[Hopcraft+_Why are Wildebeest the most abundant herbivore.pdf#page=13|PDF p.13]]). A herd that must keep moving cannot afford helpless young.

> [!pitfall] Do not confuse the cause of synchrony with its benefit
> *How* the females synchronise (Hopcraft's hypothesis: a fall in forage protein starts the diestrus cycle, a silent first ovulation cues the bulls and other females) is a **proximate** question. *Why* synchrony pays (swamping predators) is **ultimate**. They are different answers to different questions about the same trait. And as Hopcraft notes there is a rival ultimate hypothesis: synchrony may be a by-product of the migration, since February might be the only month with enough food and time to calve ([[Hopcraft+_Why are Wildebeest the most abundant herbivore.pdf#page=10|PDF p.10]]).

### 2.3 The "why": a landscape with opposing gradients
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=7|slide 7]]: "we need to look at where these animals are found, the Serengeti plains…"* · <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> *Holdo et al. 2009*

The "why" cannot be answered from the animal alone; the slide sends us to the landscape. Holdo et al. (2009, *Am. Nat.* 173, 431; the **paper** the instructor marks "do not get awed by the details, read the intro and the discussion") ask a sharper question. The seasonal rainfall gradient from the short-grass plains in the south-east to the tall-grass woodland in the north and west explains why the herds *leave* the plains when they dry out. What is *not* obvious is what draws them onto the plains at the start of the rains, since **rain is higher in the woodland all year**.

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): Holdo, Holt & Fryxell (2009) in brief
> - **Landscape** ([[Holdo_2009_Opposing rainfall and nutritional gradients best explain the wildebeest migration in the Serengeti.pdf#page=2|p.432–433]]): ecosystem 30,700 km²; rainfall from < 400 mm (rain shadow of Ngorongoro) to > 1,200 mm near Lake Victoria; plains soils are shallow, nutrient-rich volcanic ash, northern soils sandier and poorer.
> - **Method:** a rainfall-driven model of grass growth plus **14 rival movement rules**: each says *which resource a wildebeest compares between sites* (green biomass $G$; green-grass **intake** $I$; plant nitrogen $N$; tree cover $T$; sodium; water; terrain). Each rule was fitted to monthly aerial-survey maps, Aug 1969 – Aug 1972 (33 months), and compared by **ΔAICc** at 10 km and 25 km resolution ([[Akaike Information Criterion]]).
> - **Result 1:** intake beats raw biomass, and adding plant **nitrogen** improves the fit in every like-for-like pairing in Table 1 (for example at 25 km, $I$: ΔAICc 103 → $I,N$: 26); the best models are $I,T,N$ (10 km) and $I,T,N,F$ (25 km, $F$ = water). Tree cover and water add less consistently; **sodium is not supported** ([[Holdo_2009_Opposing rainfall and nutritional gradients best explain the wildebeest migration in the Serengeti.pdf#page=5|Table 1, p.435]]).
> - **Result 2, a threshold:** the fit improves up to a resource-tracking radius of **80–100 km** and not beyond; when animals compare only *local* sites the simulated herd gets "trapped" and **no migration emerges** ([[Holdo_2009_Opposing rainfall and nutritional gradients best explain the wildebeest migration in the Serengeti.pdf#page=1|abstract]]; [[Holdo_2009_Opposing rainfall and nutritional gradients best explain the wildebeest migration in the Serengeti.pdf#page=8|p.438]]).
> - **Conclusion** ([[Holdo_2009_Opposing rainfall and nutritional gradients best explain the wildebeest migration in the Serengeti.pdf#page=13|p.443]]): the two ingredients that unequivocally produce a migration are **countervailing rainfall and fertility gradients**. Rainfall is a seasonal *switch* controlling how much green grass exists; fertility determines how good it is. Plains forage lasts only a few months, but is high in protein while it lasts. The authors stress that this does not refute genetic or learned components, only that they may not be *necessary*.
> - **Versus the earlier model** (Boone et al. 2006, built on rainfall and NDVI): at 5 km, the overlap between modelled and observed occupied cells was **27.4 %** against **13.4 %** (an index of the cells occupied in both divided by those occupied in either, where *occupied* means ≥ 250 animals).

> [!intuition] The best grass, not the most grass
> Imagine two restaurants: the one on the wet side serves huge portions of dry rice; the one on the dry side serves small portions of rich food, but only for a few weeks after the rains. A diner that needs *protein* heads for the second when it opens, and moves to the first when it closes. Rain and fertility run in **opposite** directions across the Serengeti, so no single place is best all year, and a herd that can see far enough must keep moving.

![[fig_ee01_gradients.png|760]]
*Figure EE1.2. **Toy model** (my construction, not Holdo's model or data). **A:** rainfall rises and plant nitrogen falls from the south-east plains to the north-west woodland. **B:** if a site's attraction is *intake × N³*, the best site starts on the plains and the herd moves north-west as the dry season advances (black line). **C:** if only intake counts, the best site is always the wet woodland: no migration. See: the migration needs both gradients, as the paper concludes.*

![[fig_ee01_aicc.png|760]]
*Figure EE1.3. Holdo et al., Table 1, redrawn: ΔAICc for the 14 candidate rules. See: the best models (green) all contain plant N; the two worst rules in both panels, G and G,T, use green biomass alone.*

> [!connection] A threshold in a model, a tipping point in a planet
> That migration appears only when the tracking radius exceeds about 80 km is a **threshold**: a smooth change in a parameter (how far animals see) produces a qualitative change in outcome (migration or none). The same structure, written with a stability analysis, is [[Bistability and Tipping Points]] and the ice–albedo fold of [[CL04 Feedback and Multiple Steady States]].

### 2.4 The ecosystem consequences: death and fire
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=9|slide 9]] (death), [[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=10|slide 10]] (fire)* · <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span> *numbers and mechanisms*

Slides 9 and 10 ask the other half of the question: what does the migration *do* to the ecosystem?

1. **Death.** Subalusky et al. (2017, *PNAS* 114, 7647) showed that **annual mass drownings** at the Mara River crossings influence **nutrient cycling and storage** in the river. The carcasses are not wasted: a recurring pulse of food and nutrients for fish and scavengers, and of phosphorus released slowly from the bones.
2. **Fire.** Slide 10 gives only the heading and a video. The link between herbivory and fire is the ecosystem-engineering side of the story.

> [!extra-beyond]- 🔵 Extra (beyond): numbers for the two effects
> - **Drownings.** About **6,200 wildebeest a year** drown in the Kenyan reach, **~1,100 tonnes** of carcass biomass, with mass drownings in **13 of the 15 years 2001–2015**; a commentary on the paper gives about 107 t of carbon, 25 t of nitrogen and 13 t of phosphorus per year. `[verify]` I took these from the Cary Institute release and the *PNAS* commentary, not from the paper.
> - **Fire.** Holdo et al. (2009, *PLoS Biol.* 7, e1000210) combined 44 years of data (1960–2003): after **rinderpest** was eradicated in the 1960s the wildebeest population surged, grazing removed fuel, **fire declined** and **tree density recovered**; fire, rather than elephants or annual rainfall, best explained tree-density change. The authors estimate this may have turned the ecosystem from a carbon source into a carbon sink. This is a **trophic cascade** that runs from a virus to a landscape.

> [!pitfall] "Rain" is not the whole story
> Slide 7's question is subtle: if forage depends on rain, why would the herd move onto the *drier* plains? The naive answer "they follow the rain" is what Holdo et al. had to qualify, because the rain-rich woodland has the more *biomass* but the poorer *quality*.

### 2.5 Why so many? Hopcraft's chain of comparisons
<span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> *Hopcraft et al. 2015, [[Hopcraft+_Why are Wildebeest the most abundant herbivore.pdf|Serengeti IV, ch. 6]]; a *detailed reading*, "a brilliant example of how complex scientific questions … should be handled" (slide 20)*

Wildebeest outnumber all other mammal populations of the Serengeti combined; they are **six times** as many as the next herbivore (zebra) and **a hundred times** as many as their closest relative the hartebeest. The chapter is a model of **eliminating alternative explanations** step by step ([[Strong Inference]]):

| comparison | asks | finding |
|---|---|---|
| vs topi, hartebeest | does their biology explain it? | partly: wide dental pad for short mat-forming grass (the most protein), synchronous and precocial calving; but not enough, since wildebeest are not so dense elsewhere |
| vs four resident herds | does the *place* matter? | residents live only where grass quality varies at fine scale and rain exceeds 900 mm with permanent water: **habitat-limited** |
| vs zebra, also a migrant | is migration enough? | no: zebra are ≥ 6× fewer, probably **predation-regulated**; wildebeest outcompete them on moderate-to-high-quality grass |

![[fig_ee01_abundance.png|720]]
*Figure EE1.4. Population sizes quoted by Hopcraft et al. (log axis). See: the one migratory herd is 30 to 240 times bigger than any resident herd of the same species, though all share the same biology.*

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): the conclusion in one paragraph
> The superabundance is "a tight feedback between the organism and its environment": a species whose diet and reproduction are fitted to a landscape that has a **unique countergradient of soil fertility and rainfall**, creating more than **3,500 km²** of high-quality grass at a scale the herds can reach. Grazing may even feed back positively, raising the grass's protein. The general recipe for migratory superabundance has three parts: **refuge areas**, **ephemeral high-quality resources**, and **connectivity** between patches. In Boma-Jonglei (southern Sudan) the same recipe favours kob and tiang, not wildebeest ([[Hopcraft+_Why are Wildebeest the most abundant herbivore.pdf#page=41|PDF p.41]]).

> [!verify] "5 km radius of perception"
> Hopcraft et al. (p.157) say that models based on historic data give wildebeest a "5 km radius zone of perception (roughly 80 km²)" and cite Holdo et al. (2009). Holdo et al.'s own result is that fit improves up to a tracking radius of **80–100 km**. The two statements cannot both describe the same quantity; I could not find "5 km" as a perception radius in Holdo et al. (5 km appears there only as a lattice resolution); it may be a mix-up with 80 km and 80 km². This vault uses Holdo's 80–100 km.

### 2.6 Why synchronous births help: a toy bound
<span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span> *my construction; calving numbers from Hopcraft et al.*

> [!extra-beyond]+ 🔵 Extra (beyond): how much can a fixed set of predators take?
> Let $N$ calves be born over a window of $w$ days, and let the predators together kill at most $K$ calves a day (a *capacity*: the time to chase, kill and eat). Then in the window at most $Kw$ calves are lost, so
> $$f_{\max}=\min\!\left(1,\frac{Kw}{N}\right).$$
> The actual wildebeest values: $N=250{,}000$, $w=21$ d, so the births arrive at $N/w\approx11{,}900$ a day. **If** $K=1{,}000$ per day (an *assumed* value of the right order for ~300 lions with access to the calving grounds plus up to about 7,000 commuting hyena), then $f_{\max}=21{,}000/250{,}000=\mathbf{8.4\,\%}$ at $w=21$ d, $36\,\%$ at 90 d (about topi's three months), and $100\,\%$ once $w\ge N/K=250$ d, i.e. nearly year-round calving (hartebeest). The same calves spread over a year can all be taken.
>
> ![[fig_ee01_swamping.png|720]]
> *Figure EE1.5. Left: births per day against the assumed kill capacity. Right: the cap on losses. A shorter window lowers the cap in proportion, as long as $K<N/w$.*
>
> **Limits.** $K$ is assumed; real predators also *learn to follow* the herd; if $N/w<K$ (small herds) there is no cap at all and, as Hopcraft notes, synchrony can even attract predators. The bound shows the *logic* of the argument, not the size of the effect.

## 3. Abstraction: levels of life and their time scales
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=12|slides 12–14]]* · <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> *Mayr on kinds of hierarchy*

After the story, the slides step back ("Abstraction of the story so far…") and draw the framework: the **structural organisation of life**, together with the **temporal organisation** (the longer time scales). Full definitions in [[Levels of Biological Organisation]].

![[fig_ee01_levels.png|520]]
*Figure EE1.6. Slide 13 redrawn. The ladder runs from biomolecules to the biosphere. **Population and above** change on ecological time scales ("CHANGING"). **Changed trait values** in a population are **microevolution**; accumulated, they lead via **speciation** to **macroevolution**. The individual's **life-cycle loop**, reduction division and gene re-arrangement to a gamete, fertilisation to a cell, links the sub-organismal levels back to the individual. See: slide 14 removes the sub-organismal levels, leaving the **individual and above as the domain of BI2113**.*

![[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=13&height=360]]
*The original slide 13 (embedded from the course PDF).*

- The wildebeest story lives at the **population** level (herd numbers, regulation around 1.2 million), reaches **down** to the individual (muscle efficiency, calf behaviour) and **up** to the ecosystem (nutrient pulses, fire).
- **Questions at different levels have different clocks.** Muscle contraction takes milliseconds, a migration a year, the rise of wildebeest-like grazers millions of years. A model that tracks one clock can treat the others as fixed or as instantaneous, which is what makes simple models possible at all ([[EE02 Structured Population Models]] and [[EE03 Population Dynamics and Chaos]] each fix one clock).

> [!intuition] A city seen from different heights
> From the street you meet people, from a roof the neighbourhoods, from a plane the road network. Each height shows patterns invisible from the one below (a traffic jam exists only at the scale of the roads) and has its own natural clock.

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): not every ladder is a level
> Mayr separates **integrated hierarchies** (cells → tissues → organs: lower units interact to make a new whole) from **aggregational** ones such as Linnaean ranks (species → genus → family), which are the taxonomist's grouping of units that do not combine into anything. Only the first kind describes a *level of organisation* ([[Extract from Growth of Biological Thought_Mayr.pdf#page=44|PDF p.44–45]]).

> [!connection] Scale separation in other subjects
> The same idea organises the atmosphere ([[Scales of Atmospheric Motion]]) and the planet ([[Earth System]]); in both, the art is to choose the level at which a simple model works.

## 4. Four features to be observed
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=15|slide 15]]* · <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> *Mayr, "Special characteristics of living organisms"*

Slide 15 lists what a student of biological systems should watch for:

1. **Diversity and interconnectedness**, *within* a level (many species in a community) and *across* levels (a change in a gene or a muscle can alter a herd, and a herd can alter a river).
2. **Stochasticity**: "chancy, chancy world". Which calf is taken, which seed lands where, which mutation arises: outcomes are probabilistic.
3. **Historicity**: today's state depends on the particular path that led to it, not only on present conditions.
4. **Emergence**: "reductionism often fails": properties of the whole are not found by dissecting the parts.

> [!example] The wildebeest has all four
> **Diversity/interconnection:** 29 other large herbivores, predators, grass, soils, rain and rinderpest interact; a virus changes fire regimes (§2.4). **Stochasticity:** which year the rains fail, which calf is caught. **Historicity:** residents and migrants once shared a gene pool; the Maasai Mara herd fell 70 % after the 1970s when mechanised farming took the Loita calving plains ([[Hopcraft+_Why are Wildebeest the most abundant herbivore.pdf#page=23|PDF p.23]]). **Emergence:** a migration appears in Holdo's model only when individuals' simple movement rules act on a big enough landscape.

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): Mayr's list of what is special about life
> Mayr gives eight characteristics (his own list, "incomplete and somewhat redundant"): **complexity and organisation**; **chemical uniqueness** of the macromolecules; a world of **quality** rather than only quantity (relational properties such as species or ecosystems); **uniqueness and variability** (populations of unique individuals); a **genetic program**; a **historical nature** (classes defined by common descent, not by similarity); **natural selection**; and **indeterminacy** (randomness of a mutation with respect to its significance, uniqueness, the size of stochastic perturbations, complexity, emergence) ([[Extract from Growth of Biological Thought_Mayr.pdf#page=31|PDF p.31–38]]). He adds that *none* conflicts with a strictly mechanistic world: life is not outside physics, only more than physics (see [[Emergence and Reductionism]]).

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): why "reductionism fails" is a precise statement
> Mayr distinguishes three claims. **Constitutive** reductionism (organisms are made of ordinary matter and break no law of physics) is accepted. **Explanatory** reductionism (understand a whole by dissecting it) is often useful but limited: parts behave differently inside the whole, and higher-level processes are often independent of the details below. **Theory** reductionism (biological laws are special cases of physical laws) he says has never succeeded: the concepts of genetics (gene, mutation, segregation) "are not chemical concepts at all" ([[Extract from Growth of Biological Thought_Mayr.pdf#page=39|PDF p.39–43]]).

> [!intuition] A traffic jam has no cause in any single car
> Every car follows the same simple rules, yet a jam can travel backwards along a motorway at a speed no car has. You cannot find it by taking one car apart. Emergence is that: a property of the interacting whole.

> [!extra-book]- 🟡 Extra (book): population thinking
> Mayr contrasts *essentialism* (a fixed type; variation is noise; the mean is real) with **population thinking** (every individual is unique; the mean is an abstraction). The course's unit, the *population*, embodies the second view: [[Population Thinking]].

## 5. The result, and what the course does about it
<span class="scope-taught">taught</span> *[[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=16|slides 16–17]]*

Slide 16 states the **result** of the four features:

- **Mathematical formulation is typically difficult.**
- When it can be done, **the relation with reality is typically statistical and conditional**, "which leads to other issues".

"What does one do then?" Two approaches: **(1)** *just figure out what is there* (description) or **(2)** *build simplified models with all kinds of caveats* (limited predictive power). BI2113 ([[01-IntroToBiology_-07-Aug-2026.pdf#page=17|slide 17]]) uses four tools:

1. **Oversimplified mathematical modelling**;
2. **focus on concepts**;
3. **historical narrative**;
4. **examining the pedagogy** (how the subject is taught and learnt);

and accepts a **fundamental trade-off between breadth and depth: "we choose breadth."**

> [!pitfall] "Oversimplified" is a method, not an apology
> The course calls its models *oversimplified* on purpose. A model is judged by what it teaches, not by how realistic it is: the logistic equation is an idealisation of every real population and still explains why populations level off. So the models of EE02–EE06 are to be understood, not believed; always ask what each one leaves out.

This is also the reply to the three burning questions of §1: *mugging up* and *description* are what you get from a subject with huge diversity and history; *no apparent structure* is what you get when mathematics works only locally, with caveats, and the connecting threads are concepts and narrative.

## 6. How science proceeds when laws are scarce
<span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> *Pigliucci 2002 and Mayr, both *detailed readings* (slide 19)* · <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span> *worked numbers*

The two readings Slide 19 recommends ("a classic", "strongly recommended") are about exactly the problem of §5.

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): are ecology and evolution "soft" sciences?
> Pigliucci's argument ([[Pigliucci_Philosophy of Ecology.pdf#page=1|Ann. Zool. Fennici 39, 87–98]]):
> - Windelband's distinction: **nomothetic** (seeking general laws) versus **idiographic** (describing the unique, reconstructing events). Ecology and evolution lie **between** them, and Pigliucci argues that the feeling of being a "soft" science comes from not appreciating the full consequences of lying between a-historical disciplines (physics) and fully historical ones (palaeontology) ([[Nomothetic versus Idiographic Science]]).
> - Atoms are a-historical; organisms and communities are outcomes of history, so generalisations have **short half-lives**. Meehl's list of 14 shared difficulties (non-linear divergent causality, feedback loops, many variables, lab–field mismatch, outliers) sits in Box 1 ([[Pigliucci_Philosophy of Ecology.pdf#page=4|PDF p.4]]).
> - **Against the null hypothesis:** most null hypotheses are false before the experiment, so rejecting one teaches little, and "the alternative does not win by default". Prefer several precisely stated hypotheses, and give up the idea that one experiment will settle anything ([[Pigliucci_Philosophy of Ecology.pdf#page=8|PDF p.8]]).
> - **Strong inference** (Platt 1964): several hypotheses, a crucial experiment that kills some, repeat ([[Strong Inference]]); but because a test falsifies only the bundle *hypothesis + auxiliary assumptions*, the clean kill is rarer than in molecular biology.
> - **Bayesian reasoning** as the working philosophy: the posterior probability of each hypothesis is the likelihood times the prior, normalised over **all** stated rivals ([[Bayes' Theorem]]). The instructor's note on slide 19 says that the Bayesian-versus-frequentist discussion "may be a little advanced and can be safely ignored", so treat it as background.
> - Pigliucci also *disagrees* with Mayr: he does not accept that a focus on concepts is the key to evolutionary ecology. The course's choice (item 2 of §5) is therefore a choice, not a consensus.

> [!extra-book]+ 🟡 Extra (book): Mayr on laws, mathematics and narrative
> Mayr ([[Extract from Growth of Biological Thought_Mayr.pdf#page=17|PDF p.17–24]]) argues that generalisations in biology are almost always **probabilistic**, that the Galilean ideal "only so much science as mathematics" cannot be the test of science (what then of the *Origin of Species*?), that mathematics is "a language relating to all sciences or to none", and that explanations in evolutionary biology are often **historical narratives**, where earlier events make a causal contribution to later ones ([[Extract from Growth of Biological Thought_Mayr.pdf#page=51|PDF p.51–52]]). The course's item 3 ("historical narrative") is this.

> [!example] Worked example: Akaike weights from Holdo's Table 1
> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span> Weight of model $i$: $w_i=e^{-\Delta_i/2}/\sum_je^{-\Delta_j/2}$. At the 10 km lattice the top three have $\Delta=0,\ 0.4,\ 4.1$, so $e^{0}=1$, $e^{-0.2}=0.819$, $e^{-2.05}=0.129$; sum $1.948$; **weights 0.513, 0.420, 0.066**. Models with plant nitrogen hold all of the weight at both lattices, and the biomass-only rules hold $\sim10^{-10}$ or less: strong support for "intake and quality, not biomass", yet no verdict on water. This is "weighing hypotheses" in Pigliucci's sense: rivals compete, none wins by default ([[Akaike Information Criterion]]).

> [!example] Worked example: one Bayes update (illustrative numbers)
> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span> Priors for four reasons why wildebeest enter the plains: biomass 0.35, intake+protein 0.25, water 0.20, predator escape 0.20. The observation (intake-and-protein models fit best, biomass-only worst) has likelihoods 0.05, 0.60, 0.10, 0.15. Then $P(D)=0.2175$ and the posteriors are **0.080, 0.690, 0.092, 0.138**. Belief moves, nobody reaches 0 or 1 ([[Bayes' Theorem]]).

## Summary
> [!summary] Cheat-sheet
> - Biology feels descriptive because its systems are **diverse and interconnected, stochastic, historical and emergent**; maths is hard and ties to data are **statistical and conditional**. Two ways to cope: describe, or model simply. **BI2113: oversimplified models + concepts + historical narrative + pedagogy; breadth over depth.**
> - Domain of BI2113: **individual and above**; micro- → speciation → macroevolution on longer clocks.
> - **How** (proximate, mechanism) vs **why** (ultimate, benefit); both apply to every trait.
> - Wildebeest: ~1,500 km/yr (4.25 km/day), muscle efficiency **62.6 %** (cow 41.8 %, mouse 34 %), synchronous calving (250,000 in 3 weeks, 80 %, ~500/hour) swamps predators, calves stand in ~6 min, drownings feed the Mara River, the herd (≈ 1.2 million) shapes fire.
> - Why migrate: **rain rises and fertility falls from SE to NW** (400→1,200 mm); best forage = intake × nitrogen; migration needs resource tracking over ≥ 80–100 km (Holdo). Why so many (Hopcraft): biology + migration + a 3,500 km² countergradient; residents are habitat-limited, zebra predation-limited.
> - Reading rule: **mandatory + slides = syllabus; detailed reading and papers are not examinable.**

## Practice
1. **Proximate or ultimate?** Classify each: (a) calves stand within about 6 minutes; (b) synchronous calving lowers the fraction of calves taken; (c) movement follows grass high in nitrogen; (d) the south-east plains' soils are volcanic ash that gives protein-rich grass. Then give one *other* question of the opposite kind for each.
2. A cow's muscle is 41.8 % efficient and a wildebeest's 62.6 %. For the same mechanical work, find the ratio of **fuel** burnt and the ratio of **heat** released. Why does the second ratio matter more in a hot dry habitat?
3. Draw the ladder of slide 13 from memory. Mark the domain of BI2113, the levels that are "CHANGING", and where microevolution, speciation and macroevolution sit.
4. **Numbers.** 250,000 calves are born in 21 days. (a) Births per day and per hour. (b) If predators can take at most $K=1{,}500$ calves a day, what is the largest fraction lost in the 21 days, and for which window length does the cap disappear? (c) Why does Hopcraft warn that synchrony fails at low density?
5. In a model comparison the best three models have $\Delta=0,\ 1.5,\ 6$. Compute the Akaike weights and say which models are credible.
6. **Bayes.** Three hypotheses have priors 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 and the data have likelihoods 0.1, 0.4, 0.8 under them. Find the posteriors and explain why the hypothesis with the highest prior is no longer favoured.
7. Which of Mayr's three kinds of reductionism is accepted by all biologists, and which does he say has never succeeded? Use the wildebeest migration to illustrate **emergence**.
8. **Exam-style.** "Explain why the wildebeest is the dominant herbivore in the Serengeti." Outline an answer using a biological argument, an environmental argument and the general recipe.
9. **Discussion.** Two rival *ultimate* hypotheses explain synchronous calving: (A) predator swamping, (B) a by-product of migration. Name one observation that favours A over B, and say what B predicts instead (hint: Hopcraft's Fig. 6.7, calf survival in large versus small groups).

> [!example]- Solutions
> 1. (a) proximate (a development/physiology fact; its ultimate counterpart: escaping hyena, keeping up with a moving herd). (b) **ultimate** (the benefit that selection acts on; the proximate counterpart is *how* females synchronise: the diestrus cycle). (c) proximate (a decision cue; the ultimate question is why selection favoured following protein: reproduction and growth need it). (d) a *condition* of the environment that makes (c) pay: part of the ultimate (ecological) explanation, with the proximate question being how soil ash produces high-N grass.
> 2. Fuel $=1/\eta$: $(1/0.418)/(1/0.626)=0.626/0.418=1.50$. Heat $=1/\eta-1$: $1.392/0.597=2.33$. Heat is what remains after work is extracted, so it grows much faster as $\eta$ falls; shedding heat costs water.
> 3. See Fig. EE1.6: domain = individual and above; "CHANGING" = population to biosphere; microevolution from population (changed trait values), speciation, macroevolution beyond.
> 4. (a) $250{,}000/21=11{,}905$ per day; $/24=496$ per hour. (b) $f_{\max}=Kw/N=1500\times21/250000=12.6\,\%$; the cap vanishes at $w=N/K=166.7$ d. (c) If $N/w<K$ the predators can take every calf they find, so a pulse brings them in without saturating them; concentration then raises losses, which is why synchrony works only with large numbers.
> 5. $e^0=1$, $e^{-0.75}=0.472$, $e^{-3}=0.0498$; sum $1.522$; weights **0.657, 0.310, 0.033**. The first two are credible ($\Delta<2$); the third has "considerably less support".
> 6. Products: $0.05,\ 0.12,\ 0.16$; sum $0.33$; posteriors **0.152, 0.364, 0.485**. The prior-favourite's likelihood was low, so the data move belief to the hypothesis that predicts them best.
> 7. Constitutive reductionism is accepted; theory reductionism has never succeeded. Emergence: a migration is not a property of any single animal: it appears when many animals applying simple rules (compare sites, move to better ones) act on a landscape with opposing gradients, and only when their perception radius is large enough (Holdo).
> 8. *Biology:* wide dental pad suited to short, protein-rich mat-forming grass; synchronous, precocial calves swamp and outrun predators. *Environment:* opposing rainfall and fertility gradients make > 3,500 km² of high-quality grass reachable by moving; residents are habitat-limited and zebra predation-limited. *General recipe:* refuge, ephemeral high-quality resource, connectivity.
> 9. If swamping is the benefit, calves born into **large groups and at the peak** should survive better than calves in small groups or out of season (as Hopcraft's Fig. 6.7 shows). If synchrony were only a by-product of migration, predation would be uncorrelated with group size and timing, and the explanation would instead be found in the food and time budgets of the mothers. This is a [[Strong Inference]] design: a test whose outcomes remove one hypothesis.
