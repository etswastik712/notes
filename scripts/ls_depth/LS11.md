@@ AFTER ## Chemical weathering (slides 101–107)
> [!derivation] 🟡 Extra · book / 🔵 beyond: the weathering thermostat, in numbers (A&A ch. 7, "long-term carbon cycle")
> A&A (PDF pp.218–219) write the key silicate weathering reaction for a calcium silicate (wollastonite stands in for Ca and Mg silicates):
> $$\mathrm{CaSiO_3}+2\,\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}+2\,\mathrm{HCO_3^-}+\mathrm{SiO_2}+\mathrm{H_2O},$$
> where the carbonic acid is dissolved atmospheric CO$_2$. Rivers carry the dissolved ions to the sea, where carbonate precipitates, $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}+2\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\to\mathrm{CaCO_3}+\mathrm{CO_2}+\mathrm{H_2O}$. **Net: one CO$_2$ is removed per Ca** (two consumed in weathering, one returned when the carbonate forms): $\mathrm{CaSiO_3}+\mathrm{CO_2}\to\mathrm{CaCO_3}+\mathrm{SiO_2}$, i.e. **0.38 t of CO$_2$ per tonne of CaSiO$_3$ weathered** ($44.0/116.2$). Metamorphism and volcanism return it over millions of years (the reverse reaction), so *weathering vs outgassing sets atmospheric CO$_2$ and hence temperature on $10^6$-yr time scales* (A&A's statement).
> **The negative feedback.** Weathering kinetics are Arrhenius: rate $\propto\exp(-E_a/RT)$, so a warming of the surface speeds the CO$_2$ sink. The factor for a temperature rise $T_0\to T_1$ is $\exp[(E_a/R)(1/T_0-1/T_1)]$ (`scripts/ls_depth_numbers.py`):
> | $E_a$ | $288\to293$ K ($+5$ K) | $288\to298$ K ($+10$ K, "$Q_{10}$") |
> |---|---|---|
> | 30 kJ/mol | $\times1.24$ | $\times1.52$ |
> | **60 kJ/mol** | $\times1.53$ | $\times2.32$ |
> | 90 kJ/mol | $\times1.90$ | $\times3.53$ |
> A $5$ K warming therefore raises silicate weathering by 25–90 %, drawing CO$_2$ down until the climate cools again: this is a **stabilising feedback**, with relaxation time of order $10^5$–$10^6$ yr (the time for the atmosphere–ocean carbon reservoir to be drained by the weathering flux). Together with the uplift feedback of [[LS10 Folds Flexure and Mountain Building]] (more fresh rock exposed $\Rightarrow$ more weathering $\Rightarrow$ cooling) it is the proposed mechanism by which the Himalayan uplift contributed to Cenozoic cooling.
> *Also in A&A (p.218):* dissolution rates **decline with the age of the mineral surface** ($10^2$–$10^6$ yr experiments), partly because surfaces become occluded and partly because solutes from faster-dissolving minerals saturate the pore water; the lab rate overstates the field rate, which is why weathering is often **supply-limited or transport-limited** in the field, the two regimes of the first section of this chapter.

> [!question] Check yourself (answers below)
> 1. If $E_a=60$ kJ/mol, how much faster is weathering at 25 °C than at 15 °C?
> 2. How many tonnes of CO$_2$ are removed by weathering $10^9$ t of CaSiO$_3$-equivalent rock?
> 3. Why is the carbonate-precipitation step needed to find the *net* CO$_2$ sink?
> 4. A thick regolith is kinetically limited or supply-limited? Which depends strongly on temperature?
> *Answers.* (1) $\times2.32$ (288 to 298 K). (2) $0.38\times10^9=3.8\times10^8$ t. (3) Weathering alone consumes 2 CO$_2$ but the precipitation returns 1, so the net is 1 per Ca. (4) A thick regolith (slow erosion) is *kinetically limited* by mineral dissolution, which is strongly temperature-dependent; a thin regolith on steep, rapidly eroding terrain is *supply-limited* (fresh rock arrives faster than it can react) and nearly temperature-independent.
