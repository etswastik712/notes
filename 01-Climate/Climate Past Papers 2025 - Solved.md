## Mid-semester (26 Sep 2025)
> [!example]- Section A: fill in the blanks
> (a) A geostationary sensor measures the planetary albedo in **visible (shortwave)** wavelengths and emitted radiation in **(thermal) infrared** wavelengths.
> (b) A planet with no atmosphere has a mean surface temperature about equal to its **effective (radiative-equilibrium) emission** temperature, and **large** day–night variations (no heat capacity or transport in air; cf. the Moon, Mercury).
> (c) The orbital period is proportional to the **3/2 power of the semi-major axis** of its orbit (Kepler III, $T^2\propto a^3$), and the planet moves **fastest** when closest to the star (perihelion; Kepler II).
> (d) **High, cold-topped (cirrus)** clouds give a larger greenhouse effect, since their presence reduces the **outgoing longwave radiation** compared with clear sky.
> (e) A sidereal year is about 20 minutes **longer** than a tropical year, mainly because of the **precession of the equinoxes**.

> [!example]- Section B (6 marks each)
> 1. **Two-layer model** (both layers IR-opaque, transparent to solar) ([[CL08 Greenhouse Models]]): (a) TOA: $\sigma T_A^4=\sigma T_e^4$ ⇒ $T_A=T_e$. Layer A: $\sigma T_B^4=2\sigma T_A^4$. Layer B: $\sigma T_s^4+\sigma T_A^4=2\sigma T_B^4$ ⇒ $\sigma T_s^4=3\sigma T_A^4$, i.e. **$T_s=3^{1/4}T_a$**. (b) Surface: $\frac{S(1-\alpha)}4+\sigma T_B^4=\sigma T_s^4$. With $T_e=203$ K: **$T_s=3^{1/4}\times203=$ 267 K**. (c) $T_B=2^{1/4}T_e=241.4$ K, so $T_B-T_A=$ **38.4 K**.
> 2. **Radiative and convective heat transfer** ([[CL09 Radiative-Convective Equilibrium]]): (a) With $N$ opaque layers heated from below, each layer emits up and down. In radiative equilibrium the $n$-th layer from the top has $T_n^4=nT_e^4$ (the n-layer generalisation of B1). The **lower layers sit under more layers of IR "blankets"** and must be hotter to pass the same net flux upward, so T falls with height. (b) That radiative-equilibrium profile is **steeper than the adiabatic lapse rate**, which makes it statically unstable near the surface. Parcels warmed at the surface are buoyant and rise: **convection**. It carries sensible and latent heat upward, relaxing the lapse rate to ~6.5 K/km (moist adiabat) and cooling the surface relative to pure radiative equilibrium.
> 3. **Axial precession and the flip of seasonal contrast** ([[CL17 Orbital Forcing and Glacial Cycles]]): the Sun's and Moon's torques on the equatorial bulge make the spin axis trace a cone (~26 kyr). Together with the slow rotation of the orbit's major axis, the equinoxes move round the orbit: the **precession of the equinoxes**, ~23 kyr (19–23 kyr) relative to perihelion. Today **NH winter is at perihelion**, so NH seasons are mild and SH seasons strong. Half a cycle (~11.5 kyr) later, NH summer is at perihelion and **the hemisphere with the stronger seasonal contrast flips** to the north. The effect scales with eccentricity. See also [[Climate Past Papers 2020-2021 - Solved|2020]] Q2.
> 4. **Declination and solar noon at 45°N** ([[CL10 Global Energy Balance and Meridional Structure]]): (a) The **solar declination** δ is the latitude where the sun is overhead at noon (+23.5° in June, 0 at the equinoxes, −23.5° in December). The **tropics** have the highest annual mean because the sun is always high there (noon zenith angle ≤ ~47°) and day length is close to 12 h all year. Higher latitudes lose more in winter than they gain in summer. (b) Noon altitude $\alpha=90°-|\phi-\delta|$ and noon flux $S\sin\alpha$:
>    | date | δ | altitude | noon flux |
>    |---|---|---|---|
>    | equinox | 0 | 45.0° | 967 W m⁻² |
>    | June solstice | +23.5° | 68.5° | 1273 W m⁻² |
>    | December solstice | −23.5° | 21.5° | 501 W m⁻² |
> 5. **Land against ocean** ([[CL18 The Ocean as the Climate System's Heat Reservoir]], [[CL23 The Ocean's Ledger II - Heat Capacity and Climate Inertia]]): (a) Water has a high **specific heat** (~4× rock). Heat is **mixed** through tens of metres by turbulence, whereas land conducts it only ~1 m. Sunlight **penetrates** water. **Evaporation** absorbs energy without warming. Water moves and carries heat. So the ocean's effective heat capacity is far larger and its temperature changes less over a day or a season. (b) **Sea breeze by day**: land heats faster, so cool marine air flows inland and moderates the daytime maximum. **Land breeze at night**: the sea is warmer, so it keeps coastal nights warmer and lowers the diurnal range. Coasts also get more humidity and clouds, which lower day maxima and raise night minima.

## End-semester (24 Nov 2025)
> [!example]- Q1 (3 + 2 + 2) · Polar ocean profiles ([[CL21 Thermohaline Circulation Water Masses and Sea Ice]], [[CL25 The Ocean's Ledger IV - Salt, Density and Water Masses]])
> (a) Polar profiles:
> - **Temperature**: cold (≈ −1.8 °C) at the surface; often a slightly warmer subsurface layer (Atlantic or Circumpolar Deep Water, ~0–2 °C) at a few hundred metres; nearly uniform below. No strong thermocline.
> - **Salinity**: **low at the surface** (melt water, river input, precipitation), increasing through a **halocline** (~50–200 m), then uniform.
> - **Density**: follows salinity. Light fresh surface water, a **pycnocline = halocline**, dense water below.
>
> (b) **Stable**: density increases with depth, and the stratification is set by **salinity, not temperature** (at near-freezing temperatures the thermal expansion of sea water is small). Factors: sea-ice melt and runoff freshen the surface; cold temperatures make salinity dominate; weak summer stirring.
> (c) Summer: melt and warming strengthen stratification. Autumn and winter: the surface cools to freezing and **sea ice forms**. **Brine rejection** raises the surface salinity and density, the stratification erodes, and **convection** deepens the mixed layer. In polynyas and open-ocean convection areas it can reach the bottom, forming deep and bottom water (Antarctic Bottom Water, NADW precursors).

> [!example]- Q2 (1 + 2 + 2 + 2) · An absorption spectrum (absorptivity against λ, 0.1–50 µm) with near-IR bands near 1–3 µm (A), a low-absorption gap near 8–12 µm (B) and total absorption beyond ~20 µm (C) ([[CL07 Optical Properties of the Atmosphere]])
> (a) **Water vapour (H₂O)**: bands at 0.94, 1.1, 1.38, 1.87, 2.7 and 6.3 µm and the rotational band > 20 µm. Highly variable, **~0–4 % by volume** (≈ 0.25–0.4 % on average).
> (b) Region A (the near-IR, ~0.7–3 µm): H₂O is the one major gas that absorbs **incoming solar** radiation significantly. It deposits ~15–20 % of insolation directly in the troposphere, heating the atmosphere from within (and it also absorbs terrestrial IR). That is its unique role.
> (c) Region B, the **atmospheric window** (8–12 µm, where Earth's emission peaks): the surface radiates directly to space. It controls how efficiently Earth cools. Gases absorbing here (O₃ at 9.6 µm, CFCs, the H₂O continuum) are especially potent greenhouse agents, and satellites observe the surface and cloud tops through it.
> (d) Region C (> 20 µm): **pure rotational transitions**. H₂O has a **permanent dipole**, so rotation alone couples to the field, and the many closely spaced rotational lines merge into a strong, continuous band.

> [!example]- Q3 (7) · Derive $\dfrac{d\mathbf V_I}{dt}=\dfrac{d\mathbf V_R}{dt}+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf V_R-\Omega^2\mathbf r_\perp$ ([[CL12 Rotating Frames and the Coriolis Force]])
> **Frames:** an inertial frame I fixed relative to the stars, and a frame R rotating with constant angular velocity $\boldsymbol\Omega$ (the Earth), sharing the origin at Earth's centre. For any vector $\mathbf A$, $\Big(\dfrac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\Big)_I=\Big(\dfrac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\Big)_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A$: the rotating unit vectors change at $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\hat e$.
> - Apply it to the position $\mathbf r$: $\mathbf V_I=\mathbf V_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r$.
> - Apply it to $\mathbf V_I$: $\dfrac{d\mathbf V_I}{dt}\Big|_I=\dfrac{d}{dt}\Big|_R(\mathbf V_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\mathbf V_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)=\dfrac{d\mathbf V_R}{dt}+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf V_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)$ (Ω constant).
> - Since $\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)=-\Omega^2\mathbf r_\perp$ ($\mathbf r_\perp$ the component of $\mathbf r$ perpendicular to the axis), the result follows.
>
> Read in the rotating frame, the extra terms are the **Coriolis** acceleration and the **centrifugal** acceleration $\Omega^2\mathbf r_\perp$ (directed away from the axis).

> [!example]- Q4 (5 + 2) · The NH Hadley cell ([[CL11 Mean Meridional Circulation and Angular Momentum]], [[CL14 Observed Winds - Jets, Trades and the ITCZ]])
> (a) Air rises in the ITCZ (latent heating), flows poleward aloft, sinks near 30° (subtropical highs) and returns equatorward at the surface: a thermally direct cell. **Upper branch:** moving poleward while (approximately) conserving angular momentum, it gains westerly speed, so the flow is **southwesterly**. This builds the subtropical jet at the poleward edge. **Lower branch:** equatorward surface flow is turned **to the right** by Coriolis (in the NH), i.e. westward, giving the **northeasterly** trades. Friction keeps them weak and balances the westerly torque of the mid-latitudes.
> (b) A **stronger** equator-to-pole temperature gradient makes the upper-level flow baroclinically unstable at **lower latitudes**. In the Held–Hou picture the cell's extent grows with the thermal contrast (∝ (Δθ)^{1/2}), but eddies set the edge where baroclinic instability begins, and that moves equatorward with a stronger gradient. [!verify] The expected answer depends on the course's model: angular-momentum-conserving theory says **wider**, the eddy-limited view says **narrower**. Give the reasoning for the one taught.

> [!example]- Q5 (3 + 4) · Lost at sea ([[CL10 Global Energy Balance and Meridional Structure]], [[CL12 Rotating Frames and the Coriolis Force]])
> (a) Solar noon happens when the watch (Greenwich time) reads 3:00 pm. Local noon is 3 h after Greenwich noon, so the ship is **45° W**. On 21 June δ = +23.5°, and the noon sun altitude is 67° = 90° − |φ − δ|, so |φ − 23.5°| = 23°. Either **φ = 46.5° N** (sun to the south) or φ = 0.5° N (sun to the north). With the sun seen due south (the natural reading for a voyage out of England), **≈ 46.5° N, 45° W**, in the NW Atlantic SE of Newfoundland. [!verify] The question does not state the sun's bearing.
> (b) At 46.5°N, $f=2\Omega\sin\phi=1.06\times10^{-4}$ s⁻¹; $u=50$ km/h $=13.9$ m/s ⇒ **$a=fu=1.47\times10^{-3}$ m s⁻²** to the right of the motion. In 20 min (1200 s): $\tfrac12at^2\approx$ **1.06 km**. The constant-acceleration assumption fails for longer sails: the Coriolis acceleration is always **perpendicular to the (changing) velocity**, so it turns the ship in an **inertial circle** (radius $u/f\approx130$ km, period ~16.5 h), and $f$ changes with latitude.
