## Quiz 1 (18 Aug 2024)
> [!example]- Answers
> 1. Warm humid summers, mild winters: **temperate** (Köppen C, e.g. humid subtropical Cfa).
> 2. At 35°N at the equinox the sun's noon zenith angle is 35°: $1368\cos35°=$ **1120 W m⁻² (b)**.
> 3. A stable equilibrium needs a **negative loop: one positive and one negative coupling (c)**. ([[CL04 Feedback and Multiple Steady States]])
> 4. CO₂ drawdown over geological history: **all of the above (d)**: less volcanic outgassing, more chemical weathering, and dissolution in the ocean all lower it. ([[CL16 Atmospheric Evolution Greenhouse Gases and the Carbon Cycle]])
> 5. Not related to global warming: **the ozone hole (a)** (CFC chemistry).
> 6. Daily cycle: insolation is a half-sine peaking at local noon and zero at night. Absorbed minus emitted stays positive into the afternoon, so **temperature peaks ~2–3 pm** (a lag) and is lowest just after sunrise. Emitted longwave follows temperature ($\sigma T^4$), peaking with it, and is non-zero at night. ([[CL03 Radiation and Planetary Energy Balance]])
> 7. Water near its triple point: (i) **water vapour is the main greenhouse gas** and increases with temperature (feedback); (ii) **phase changes move latent heat** from the surface to the atmosphere and towards the poles; (iii) **clouds and ice** (both possible because all three phases coexist) set much of the albedo, with snow and ice feedbacks. Together they buffer and regulate the radiation balance. ([[CL07 Optical Properties of the Atmosphere]])
> 8. Faint young Sun: $S=0.7\times1368=958$ W m⁻² ⇒ $T_e=[S(1-\alpha)/4\sigma]^{1/4}=$ **233 K** for α = 0.3 (255 K with α = 0). **Methane** was abundant because the atmosphere was **anoxic**: no O₂ to oxidise it, and methanogens produced it. As a strong greenhouse gas it kept the surface above freezing. At high CH₄/CO₂ ratios an organic **haze** forms that cools the surface (anti-greenhouse), a possible regulating feedback. ([[CL16 Atmospheric Evolution Greenhouse Gases and the Carbon Cycle]])

## Mid-semester (21 Sep 2024)
> [!question] Section A Q1 (10 × 1) · The classic figure: downgoing solar radiation (70–75 % transmitted) and upgoing thermal radiation (15–30 % transmitted, emission curves 210–310 K) against wavelength 0.1–70 µm, with the absorption spectrum below (regions A–E). Fill the blanks.

> [!example]- Answers ([[CL07 Optical Properties of the Atmosphere]], [[CL03 Radiation and Planetary Energy Balance]])
> (a) Solar intensities at the **top of the atmosphere** (incoming) and at the surface; terrestrial intensities as **emitted by the surface** (blackbody curves 210–310 K) and what reaches space.
> (b) 0.2–0.3 µm (A) is absorbed by **photodissociation** of **O₂ and O₃** molecules (ozone in the stratosphere).
> (c) **~20–25 %** of visible light is lost through **scattering** (Rayleigh by molecules; aerosols and clouds) of radiation. [!verify] The percentage expected is read off the figure.
> (d) Near-IR absorption (C) comes from the **vibrational** (vibration–rotation) modes of **H₂O** (and CO₂) molecules.
> (e) Terrestrial peak ≈ **10 µm** ⇒ $T=2900/10=$ **290 K**.
> (f) 8–12 µm (D) is the **atmospheric window**; **ozone** absorbs at 9.6–9.8 µm.
> (g) λ > 15 µm (E) is absorbed by the **rotational** modes of **H₂O** (plus CO₂'s 15 µm bending band).
> (h) Air molecules are much **smaller** than the IR **wavelength**: the scattering efficiency ∝ (size/λ)⁴ is negligible.
> (i) O₂ and N₂ are **homonuclear diatomic** molecules with no permanent dipole, and their vibration (symmetric stretch) creates none. They cannot absorb or emit IR (no IR-active modes).

> [!example]- Section B (5 marks each)
> 1. **OLR** ([[CL10 Global Energy Balance and Meridional Structure]], [[CL28 Tutorials II - Earth's Energy Budget from Space (CERES)]]): the total thermal infrared flux leaving the top of the atmosphere (W m⁻²). Components: surface emission through the window; emission by greenhouse gases (H₂O, CO₂, O₃) at their emission levels; emission from **cloud tops**. **Deserts > rainforests**: deserts have clear, dry, subsiding air over a hot surface, so emission comes from warm, low levels. Rainforests have humid air and **deep convective clouds with very cold tops**, so emission comes from cold, high levels.
> 2. **Leaky one-layer, ε = 0.5, α = 0.3** ([[CL08 Greenhouse Models]]): $T_s=T_e\Big(\dfrac2{2-\epsilon}\Big)^{1/4}=254.9\times(4/3)^{1/4}=$ **274 K**. The schematic shows $S(1-\alpha)/4$ down; $\sigma T_s^4$ up; $(1-\epsilon)\sigma T_s^4$ through the layer; $\epsilon\sigma T_a^4$ both up and down. Derivation as in [[Climate Past Papers 2023 - Solved|2023]] MS B2.
> 3. **Hydrostatics and convection** ([[CL06 Hydrostatic Balance and Vertical Structure]], [[CL09 Radiative-Convective Equilibrium]]): (a) $dp/dz=-\rho g$; vertical motion is weak and set by continuity. (b) **Convection** is buoyancy-driven vertical overturning, when a displaced parcel is lighter than its surroundings (lapse rate steeper than adiabatic). Its **vertical scale is capped by the stable stratosphere** (the tropopause). Since convective cells are roughly as wide as they are deep, their **horizontal scale is also limited** to ~1–10 km (individual clouds), organising at most into mesoscale systems.
> 4. **Wien from Planck** ([[CL03 Radiation and Planetary Energy Balance]]): $B_\lambda=\dfrac{c_1}{\lambda^5(e^{c_2/\lambda T}-1)}$; $\partial B_\lambda/\partial\lambda=0$ ⇒ with $x=c_2/\lambda T$: $5(1-e^{-x})=x$ ⇒ $x=4.965$ ⇒ $\lambda_{max}T=c_2/4.965=2.90\times10^3$ µm K. (b) $\epsilon_\lambda=I_\lambda(\text{emitted})/B_\lambda(T)$; $a_\lambda=I_\lambda(\text{absorbed})/I_\lambda(\text{incident})$. **Kirchhoff**: $\epsilon_\lambda=a_\lambda$ in local thermodynamic equilibrium.

## Quiz 2 (6 Nov 2024)
> [!example]- Answers ([[CL12 Rotating Frames and the Coriolis Force]], [[CL13 Horizontal Motion - Geostrophic Balance and Friction]], [[CL14 Observed Winds - Jets, Trades and the ITCZ]])
> 1. The **incorrect** statement about the land breeze: **"it occurs during daytime" (b)**. It blows at night, when the land is cooler and its pressure higher.
> 2. The ground at 40°S moves east at $\Omega R\cos40°=355$ m/s. A parcel with absolute eastward speed 336 m/s moves **westward at ~19 ≈ 20 m/s (a)** relative to the Earth.
> 3. $vr$ = const: $v=20\times50/4=$ **250 m/s (c)** (why real cyclones need friction).
> 4. Geostrophic wind **flows perpendicular to the pressure-gradient force (a)**, i.e. along the isobars.
> 5. Centrifugal force: **(d) it makes the apparent gravity largest at the poles** (zero there, maximal at the equator). (a) and (c) are false (it decreases poleward); (b) is false (its horizontal component points equatorward).
> 6. A **westward** wind in the SH spins *slower* than the Earth, so it has less centrifugal force. The **poleward** horizontal component of gravity is no longer balanced, so the wind turns poleward, i.e. **south** (to its left).
> 7. The annual mean at the pole ≈ $S\sin\varepsilon/\pi\approx$ **173 W m⁻²**, against ~**417** at the equator (ratio ≈ 0.41 < 0.5). The pole gets sun for only half the year and always at low elevation (≤ 23.5°, so the beam is spread out); the equator gets a high sun every day.
> 8. **ITCZ**: the trades converge low-level warm, moist air, which is forced up. Latent heat release makes the air conditionally unstable, giving **deep convection** and towering clouds. **Subtropics**: the **descending branch of the Hadley cell** warms adiabatically and dries, producing a **trade-wind inversion** that caps convection. Hence deserts.

## End-semester (25 Nov 2024)
> [!example]- Section A: fill in the blanks (1 mark each)
> 1. Vertical pressure gradients are much **larger** than horizontal ones; the vertical PGF is balanced by **gravity** (hydrostatic balance).
> 2. On a perfect sphere, a stationary object's linear velocity at the equator is **greater** than at a subtropical latitude, and its effective gravity is **smaller** (larger centrifugal reduction).
> 3. NH surface winds around a high spiral **outward** in the **clockwise** direction.
> 4. Lower OLR over the **western** equatorial ocean basins, regions of **convergence / deep convection** (the warm pool) set up by the trades.
> 5. Moisture-laden **southwesterly** surface winds, and release of **latent heat** over land.
> 6. The Ferrel cell is a thermally **indirect** cell: **rising** motion over the cooler higher latitudes (sinking over the warmer subtropics).
> 7. Sea ice forms when the surface ocean cools below **−1.8 °C** (the freezing point of sea water); it leads to **loss (decrease)** of buoyancy of the surface water through brine rejection.
> 8. **Weakening** of the easterlies during El Niño leads to **higher** than normal SST in the eastern Pacific.
> 9. Sea surface salinity is **high** over the subtropical oceans since evaporation **exceeds** precipitation there.
> 10. The Hadley cell is a **meridional (north–south)** overturning; the Walker cell a **zonal (east–west)** one.

> [!example]- Section B (5 marks each)
> 1. **Subtropical gyres** ([[CL19 Wind-Driven Ocean Circulation]], [[CL21 Thermohaline Circulation Water Masses and Sea Ice]]): (a) As in [[Climate Past Papers 2023 - Solved|2023]] ES B3 (NH version: Ekman to the right, convergence, clockwise). (b) The **western boundary currents** (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio) carry warm water poleward and **release heat** to the atmosphere in winter, which warms NW Europe. The North Atlantic Current carries **warm, salty** water into the subpolar gyre and the Nordic Seas. There it is cooled and made denser (with brine rejection), and sinks: **NADW** formation feeds the thermohaline circulation. The **subpolar gyre's** cyclonic doming (upwelling) weakens the stratification and preconditions deep convection (Labrador Sea).
> 2. **Geostrophy** ([[CL13 Horizontal Motion - Geostrophic Balance and Friction]]): (a) A balance of the PGF and Coriolis: steady, frictionless, straight, Rossby number ≪ 1 (large scale, away from the equator). The flow is **parallel to the isobars**, with low pressure to the left in the NH and speed ∝ isobar spacing. **Surface winds are never geostrophic** because friction in the boundary layer slows them. The weaker Coriolis can no longer balance the PGF, so the wind crosses the isobars towards low pressure. (b) $f_{45}=1.03\times10^{-4}$: **PGF $=fu=1.03\times10^{-3}$ m s⁻²**. At 10°N, $f=2.53\times10^{-5}$, so that PGF would balance a geostrophic wind of **40.7 m/s**, not 10: a 10 m/s wind there is **not geostrophic** (Coriolis is too weak; near the equator geostrophy fails).
> 3. (a) **Angular momentum**: $u=\Omega R\sin^2\phi/\cos\phi=$ **134 m/s** at 30°N (R = 6.36 × 10⁶ m). (b) **Missile at 50°N**: $a=fu=0.112$ m s⁻²; $t=1000$ s; deflection **55.8 km** south (same as the [[Climate Past Papers 2020-2021 - Solved|2020]] key).
> 4. **Ekman spiral and transport** ([[CL19 Wind-Driven Ocean Circulation]], [[CL24 The Ocean's Ledger III - Wind and the Momentum Ledger]]): wind stress drags the surface water, which Coriolis turns **45° to the right** (NH) of the wind. Each layer drags the next with less speed and a further rightward turn, so the current vector **spirals** clockwise and decays exponentially with depth (Ekman depth ~10–100 m). The **depth-integrated (Ekman) transport** is **90° to the right** of the wind (NH; left in the SH), with magnitude $\tau/\rho f$. Its convergence and divergence drive Ekman pumping (gyres) and coastal upwelling.
