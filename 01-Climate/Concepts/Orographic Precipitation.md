# Orographic Precipitation

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** regional monsoon rainfall is strongly influenced by orography; W coast of India, Meghalaya, W coast of Burma (22 Oct p27–28) · 🟡 **extra book:** rain-shadow deserts, Hartmann §6.5.2 · 🔵 **extra:** parcel-lifting rain-rate estimate

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Squeeze a wet sponge: air forced up a mountain is squeezed, expands, cools, and cannot hold its water. The rain falls on the windward slope; the dried air that sinks on the lee side is warm and dry (a rain shadow).

> [!key] Mechanism
> Air forced up a slope expands and cools at the dry adiabatic rate $\Gamma_d=g/c_p\approx9.8$ K km⁻¹ until it is saturated (the **lifting condensation level**, ≈ $125\,\mathrm{m\,K^{-1}}\times(T-T_d)$); above that it cools more slowly (the moist adiabat) and the vapour in excess of saturation condenses and, in part, falls as rain. On the lee side the air descends, warms and dries.

**Numbers.** Monsoon boundary-layer air at 27 °C, 85 % RH (19.5 g kg⁻¹): condensation starts at 340 m; lifted 1 km it has condensed 1.6 g kg⁻¹; spread over a 50 km windward slope with a 1.5 km deep layer at 10 m s⁻¹ this is ≈ 45 mm day⁻¹ (an upper bound with perfect efficiency).

**Examples (taught):** the west coast of India (Western Ghats), north-east India (Meghalaya), the west coast of Myanmar. **Rain shadows:** the Deccan interior, the Gobi, the Patagonian desert ([[CL15 Monsoons]], §2).

Thermodynamics: [[Dry Adiabatic Lapse Rate]], [[Clausius-Clapeyron Relation]], [[Atmospheric Stability]]; see [[Monsoon]].
