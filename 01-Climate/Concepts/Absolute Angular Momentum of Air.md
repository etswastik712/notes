# Absolute Angular Momentum of Air

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span>

> [!syllabus] Scope of this note
> | Scope | What |
> |---|---|

> | 🟡 **Extra · book** | M&P §8.2.1 Eqs. 8-1, 8-2 and the numbers 14, 58, 130 m s⁻¹; role in the subtropical jet and surface westerlies |

> [!key] Definition
> The angular momentum per unit mass about the Earth's axis of a parcel at latitude $\varphi$ with eastward wind $u$ relative to the ground:
> $$A=\Omega a^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi+u\,a\cos\varphi.$$
> The first term is carried by the rotating Earth, the second by the wind. Absent torques (pressure and friction), $A$ is conserved following a parcel.

> [!intuition] The ice skater
> Pull the arms in and you spin faster. A parcel moving poleward has its "arms" (distance $a\cos\varphi$ from the axis) pulled in, so it must gain eastward speed; moving equatorward, it lags and drifts west. In both cases it ends to the **right** of its path in the NH: this *is* the Coriolis effect ([[CL12 Rotating Frames and the Coriolis Force]] §4.3).

**Ring of air from the equator** (frictionless upper branch, $u=0$ at the equator, $A_0=\Omega a^2$): $u(\varphi)=\Omega a\sin^2\varphi/\cos\varphi$ = 14, 58, 134 m s⁻¹ at 10°, 20°, 30° (the book rounds the last to 130); 329 at 45°; $\to\infty$ at the pole. So a single pole-to-pole cell cannot exist ([[CL11 Mean Meridional Circulation and Angular Momentum]]). **Equatorward** from 30° N at rest: $u=\Omega a(\cos^2\varphi_0-1)=-116$ m s⁻¹.

Differentiating gives $\mathrm Du/\mathrm Dt=2\Omega\sin\varphi\,v+uv\tan\varphi/a$: the Coriolis term plus a curvature term.

> [!pitfall] Where the formula applies
> Only to the frictionless upper branch with $u=0$ at the equator; not at the surface, where friction exchanges angular momentum with the ground.

See [[Coriolis Force]], [[Rotating Reference Frames]].
