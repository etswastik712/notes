# Seasons and Earth's Orbit

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!key] Core idea
> The seasons are caused by the **tilt of the spin axis** (23.4° from the orbit normal), not by the Earth–Sun distance. The orbit is a slightly eccentric ellipse ($e=0.0167$): the Earth is at **perihelion** ≈ 3 January (distance −1.7 %, flux +3.4 %) and at **aphelion** ≈ 4 July.

| event | date | what happens |
|---|---|---|
| March equinox | ~20 Mar | Sun over the equator; equal day and night everywhere |
| June solstice | ~21 Jun | north pole tipped towards the Sun: longest day in the north, polar night in the south |
| September equinox | ~22 Sep | equal day and night |
| December solstice | ~21 Dec | south pole tipped towards the Sun |

- Distance varies by < ±2 %; the corresponding flux variation (±3.4 %, ~±45 W m⁻²) is *not negligible* but far smaller than the tilt effect on local seasonal flux. (Southern summer occurs near perihelion; Northern summer near aphelion.)
- Tilt, eccentricity and the date of perihelion all vary slowly (**Milankovitch cycles**, ~$10^4$–$10^5$ yr) and are thought to pace the glacial–interglacial cycles.

> [!extra-beyond]- Extra (beyond): the three cycles
> Obliquity 22.1°–24.5° (period ~41 kyr); precession of the equinoxes (perihelion date; ~19–23 kyr); eccentricity 0–0.058 (~100 and 400 kyr). Computed daily-mean insolation at 65° N on 21 June ranges from 428 to 567 W m⁻² between extreme configurations (present: 478) ([[CL10 Global Energy Balance and Meridional Structure#2.3 The orbit|CL10 §2.3]]). Hays, Imbrie & Shackleton (1976, *Science* 194, 1121).
