# Land-Sea Thermal Circulation

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** the warm/cold column sketch with H and L, low-level inflow and upper outflow (22 Oct p34–36) · 🟡 **extra book:** hydrostatic derivation $\Delta\ln p$, heat capacity numbers (Webster & Fasullo) · 🔵 **extra:** slab-model amplitudes and lags

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Two columns of air with the same weight at the bottom: warm air is *fluffier*, so its column is taller, and at any fixed height above the ground the warm column has more air above it, hence higher pressure. Air aloft slides from the tall column to the short one; the loss at the bottom of the warm column leaves a surface low that draws air in.

> [!key] Mechanism
> 1. Land has little heat capacity (shallow active layer, specific heat ≈ 1300 vs 4218 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water), so it warms and cools more than the ocean: the slab amplitude is $F_0/\sqrt{\lambda^2+(C\omega)^2}$.
> 2. From hydrostatic balance $\partial\ln p/\partial z=-g/RT$: for equal surface pressure, $\Delta\ln p(z_1)=\dfrac{g z_1}{R}\Big(\dfrac1{T_c}-\dfrac1{T_w}\Big)>0$, i.e. **higher pressure aloft over the warm column**.
> 3. Upper-level flow warm → cold; mass continuity makes surface air flow cold → warm (towards the thermal low), and the loop closes with ascent over land and descent over sea.

**Numbers.** $T_c=265$ K, $T_w=275$ K: at 10 km $p_w-p_c=13$ hPa. A surface low of 10 hPa under the warm column only reverses the gradient aloft above $z^*=-R\Delta\ln p(0)/[g(1/T_c-1/T_w)]=2.1$ km.

Day-night analogue: the **sea breeze**; the monsoon is the seasonal, continental-scale version with Coriolis and moisture added ([[Cross-Equatorial Flow]]).
