# Tibetan Plateau and Monsoon Heating

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** topography and low-level winds around the Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau (22 Oct p32–33 3-D map) · 🟡 **extra book:** plateau as an elevated heat source; reversed temperature gradient and easterly shear; role in monsoon onset and in deserts to the west (Webster & Fasullo; Hartmann §6.5.1)

> [!intuition] In one picture
> The plateau is a stove on a table: its surface sits at 4–5 km, so its heating goes straight into the middle troposphere, where it warms a deep layer of air and raises pressure aloft (the upper-level anticyclone) much more efficiently than heating at sea level.

> [!key] Statement (Webster & Fasullo 2003)
> During spring and summer the Tibetan Plateau acts as an **elevated heat source** (sensible plus latent heat). In boreal summer the 500–200 hPa layer over South Asia/Tibet is the warmest on Earth, > 5 K warmer than the equator at the same longitude. A temperature that *increases poleward* gives, through the **thermal-wind** relation, easterly shear: south-westerlies below, easterlies above, i.e. the easterly jet.

Further roles: the plateau and the Himalaya block cold dry air from the north and force the moist monsoon flow to rise (see [[Orographic Precipitation]]); the plateau's position anchors the upper-level anticyclone and the dry belt to the west.

See [[Monsoon]], [[CL15 Monsoons]] §7, [[CL14 Observed Winds - Jets, Trades and the ITCZ]] (jets, thermal wind).
