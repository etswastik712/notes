# Cross-Equatorial Flow

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** how a pressure gradient drives flow across the equator: non-rotating vs rotating force-balance panels, pressure gradient (PG), Coriolis (C), friction (F), lower and upper troposphere (22 Oct p32–40) · 🟡 **extra book:** Somali jet, easterly jet, thermal-wind condition (Webster) · 🔵 **extra:** explicit steady solution

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Push a trolley down a slope with the brake half on. Without steering it just goes downhill. Add steering that gets stronger away from the equator (Coriolis) and it veers: to its right in the north, to its left in the south. At the equator there is no steering, so the flow runs straight along the gradient.

> [!key] Steady boundary-layer solution
> With a meridional pressure-gradient acceleration $G=-\rho^{-1}\partial p/\partial y$ and linear friction $\alpha$:
> $$v=\frac{G\alpha}{\alpha^2+f^2},\qquad u=\frac{G f}{\alpha^2+f^2},\qquad f=2\Omega\sin\varphi .$$
> At the equator $f=0$: $v=G/\alpha$, $u=0$ (a purely meridional flow, *across* the isobars). Poleward of it the flow turns right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern.

For $G=2.9\times10^{-4}$ m s⁻² (10 hPa per 3000 km) and $\alpha^{-1}=0.5$ day: $v_{eq}=12.5$ m s⁻¹; at 10° N $(u,v)=(6.2,5.7)$ m s⁻¹ (a south-westerly); at 10° S $(-6.2,5.7)$ (a south-easterly).

Aloft the gradient is reversed and friction ten times weaker, so the flow is nearly geostrophic and spirals out of the upper anticyclone as the **tropical easterly jet** (40–50 m s⁻¹, Webster). In the western Indian Ocean the East African highlands focus the low-level flow into the **Somali jet** (> 20–25 m s⁻¹).
