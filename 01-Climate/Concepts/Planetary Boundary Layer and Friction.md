# Planetary Boundary Layer and Friction

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** PBL ≈ 1–2 km, friction terms $F_x,F_y$, free atmosphere vs near surface (22 Oct p8, 13–15) · 🟡 **extra book:** three-way balance and angle $\psi$ (W&H §7.2.5) · 🔵 **extra:** Ekman layer solution

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Slide a rug across a rough floor: the layer touching the floor is dragged to a crawl and each layer above is slowed a little less. The lowest 1–2 km of the atmosphere is that rug: the ground drags the wind, and the wind ends up slower and leaning towards low pressure.

> [!key] Definition
> The **planetary boundary layer** is the lowest 1–2 km, where turbulent surface drag $\mathbf F$ matters. Above it (the **free atmosphere**) friction is negligible and the flow is nearly geostrophic. In the PBL the steady balance is **three-way**: $-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\text{Coriolis}+\mathbf F=0$.

With friction opposing the wind, $\tan\psi=\kappa/f$ and $V_s=V_g\cos\psi$ ($\psi$ = angle across the isobars toward low pressure): the surface wind is **slower** than geostrophic and **crosses the isobars toward low pressure**, so surface flow spirals in to lows and out of highs, giving convergence/uplift in lows and divergence/subsidence in highs. Typical $\psi$ is 10–30° (more over rough land).

See [[CL13 Horizontal Motion - Geostrophic Balance and Friction]], [[Cyclones and Anticyclones]], [[Geostrophic Balance]]; trade winds rely on the same friction ([[Trade Winds and Westerlies]]).
