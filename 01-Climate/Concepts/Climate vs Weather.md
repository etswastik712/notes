# Climate vs Weather

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** climate as the 30-year average of weather (Argha L2 sl. 19–20); weather vs climate framing (L1) · 🔵 **extra:** predictability, Lorenz chaos, initial-value vs boundary-value

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Weather is a single roll of a die; climate is the average of a thousand rolls and the shape of the distribution. A loaded die (more greenhouse gas) shifts the average even though no single roll is predictable.

> [!key] Definition
> **Weather** is the instantaneous state of the atmosphere (and ocean surface) at a place and time. **Climate** is the **statistics of weather over a long period**, conventionally a **30-year** average (WMO normal; current reference 1991–2020), including the mean, the spread, the seasonal and daily cycles and the extremes.

- **Why 30 years:** long enough that year-to-year variability averages out ($\sigma/\sqrt N$), short enough to follow slow change.
- **Predictability:** weather forecasting is an **initial-value problem**: small errors double in a day or two, so skill is lost after about two weeks (Lorenz 1963, [[CL01 Earth as a System]]). Climate prediction is a **boundary-value (forced response) problem**: the *statistics* respond to the forcing even when individual days are unpredictable ([[Radiative Forcing]]).
- **Time scale decides:** anything slower than the averaging window is a *trend*, anything faster is *variability*. The record ([[Global Mean Surface Temperature]]) shows both.
- **Extremes** are part of climate: the tails of the distribution move when the mean shifts.
