# Trade Winds and Westerlies

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-book">extra · book</span>

> [!key] Definition
> The low-level zonal-mean winds are **easterly in the tropics** (equatorward of ~30°) and **westerly in mid-latitudes** (poleward of ~30°), but much weaker than the winds aloft (a few m s⁻¹, weakened by surface friction). The low-level tropical easterlies are the **trade winds**: north-easterly in the Northern Hemisphere, south-easterly in the Southern.

- **Why NE?** The return branch of the [[Hadley Cell]] flows toward the equator; Coriolis turns it to its right in the NH (westward component), so equatorward + westward = north-easterly. In the angular-momentum picture the air comes from where the ground moves slowly to where it moves fast, so it **lags** the ground (easterly).
- **They converge** at the [[Intertropical Convergence Zone]] and sit under the **trade inversion**: warm dry subsiding air above cooler, moister near-surface air.
- **Westerlies** at the surface peak near 50°, well poleward of the [[Subtropical Jet]]: explained by eddy momentum transport, not by the axisymmetric Hadley model ([[Ferrel Cell and Polar Cell]]).
- Surface friction is what exchanges angular momentum with the solid Earth: the easterlies *gain* it (ground drags air), the westerlies *lose* it ([[CL11 Mean Meridional Circulation and Angular Momentum]]).

> [!extra-book]- Extra (book)
> The name comes from sailing-ship days, when these winds with the mid-latitude westerlies let ships circle the North Atlantic (M&P p.74, footnote on M. F. Maury). The surface wind stress is a primary driver of the ocean gyres (M&P Ch. 10).

See [[Coriolis Force]], [[CL14 Observed Winds - Jets, Trades and the ITCZ]].
