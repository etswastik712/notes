## Mid-semester (29 Sep 2022)

### Section A (4 marks each)
> [!example]- A1–A4
> 1. **Carbonate buffering**: see [[Climate Past Papers 2020-2021 - Solved|2021]] B(a). Net uptake: $\mathrm{CO_2+CO_3^{2-}+H_2O\to2HCO_3^-}$; DIC ↑, carbonate ↓, pH ↓.
> 2. **15°N and 25°N, June solstice against March equinox** ([[CL10 Global Energy Balance and Meridional Structure]], [[CL17 Orbital Forcing and Glacial Cycles]]). Both latitudes receive **more** in June: a higher noon sun (15°N: 81.6° against 75°; 25°N: 88.4°, almost overhead, against 65°) and **longer days** (12.9 h and 13.6 h, against 12 h everywhere at the equinox). Daily means: 15°N 459 against 421 W m⁻²; 25°N 484 against 395 W m⁻². The June gain is larger at 25°N, near the Tropic, where the sun is overhead at the solstice.
> 3. **Volcanic emissions** ([[CL16 Atmospheric Evolution Greenhouse Gases and the Carbon Cycle]]):
>    - **H₂O**: negligible effect; it rains out within days and is tiny next to the natural cycle.
>    - **CO₂**: a warming that matters only on geological timescales; today's volcanic output is ~1 % of anthropogenic emissions.
>    - **SO₂**: the main short-term effect. Injected into the stratosphere it forms **sulfate aerosols**, which reflect sunlight and cool the climate by a few tenths of a kelvin for 1–3 yr.
>    - **Ash and dust**: local, short-lived shading.
>
>    **Net: short-term cooling; long-term (geological) warming** from the CO₂ source.
> 4. **Precession of the equinoxes** ([[CL17 Orbital Forcing and Glacial Cycles]]): the axis wobbles, so the solstices and equinoxes move around the orbit relative to perihelion (~23 kyr; mechanism in [[Climate Past Papers 2020-2021 - Solved|2020]] Q2). **Climate impact**: it sets which hemisphere has summer at perihelion. That hemisphere gets hotter summers and colder winters (strong seasonality), and the other gets mild seasons. The effect grows with eccentricity. It paces the ~23 kyr monsoon and ice-volume cycles via 65°N summer insolation.

### Section B
> [!example]- B1 (2 + 2 + 1) · Forcing, Greenland, sea ice ([[CL03 Radiation and Planetary Energy Balance]], [[CL26 The Ocean's Ledger V - Mass, the Overturning and the Switch]])
> (a) $T_e\propto S^{1/4}$ ⇒ $\Delta S/S=4\Delta T_e/T_e=4/255$ ⇒ $\Delta S=$ **21.5 W m⁻²** in the solar constant (a forcing of $\Delta S(1-\alpha)/4=3.8$ W m⁻², close to the ~3.7 W m⁻² of doubled CO₂).
> (b) Melt water volume $=2.55\times10^{18}/1000=2.55\times10^{15}$ m³; ocean area $0.71\times4\pi(6371\ \text{km})^2=3.62\times10^{14}$ m² ⇒ rise **≈ 7.0 m**. (The ice density is not needed when the mass is given.)
> (c) **Negligible**: floating sea ice already displaces its own mass of sea water (Archimedes). A tiny rise (~2–3 % of the ice's water-equivalent thickness) comes only because fresh melt water is less dense than sea water.

> [!example]- B2 (2 + 2 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.5) · Daisyworld (white and black, 5–40 °C, optimum 22.5 °C) ([[CL04 Feedback and Multiple Steady States]], [[CL27 Tutorials I - Daisyworld with Land and Ocean]])
> (a) Planetary temperature against coverage: **white coverage ↑ ⇒ T ↓** (higher albedo); **black coverage ↑ ⇒ T ↑**. Two straight-ish lines of opposite slope.
> (b) Black daisies are locally warmer, white daisies locally cooler. (i) **Below the optimum, black grows faster** (its warmth brings it closer to the optimum). (ii) **Above the optimum, white grows faster.**
> (c) Stable equilibria are where the coverage–temperature loop is negative. **White only:** stable where growth rises with T, i.e. **below the optimum** (cooler planet). **Black only:** stable **above the optimum** (warmer planet). **Mixed:** in between, close to the optimum.
> (d) The mixed world's stable temperature is **close to (slightly around) the 22.5 °C optimum**: the two species push it from both sides.
> (e) The relative populations depend on the **solar luminosity** (forcing). [!verify] Or on the daisies' **albedo**: the blank's intended word is unclear. As luminosity rises, the mix shifts from black to white.

> [!example]- B3 (6) · How organic and inorganic carbon in marine sedimentary rocks returns to the atmosphere ([[CL16 Atmospheric Evolution Greenhouse Gases and the Carbon Cycle]])
> **Organic carbon (kerogen)** is uplifted with the rocks and exposed by erosion, then **oxidised**: $\mathrm{CH_2O+O_2\to CO_2+H_2O}$. Uplift and erosion take ~10⁸ yr. Some is also cooked into CO₂ and methane by metamorphism.
> **Carbonate**: carried down by subduction or buried deeply, it undergoes **metamorphic decarbonation**: $\mathrm{CaCO_3+SiO_2\to CaSiO_3+CO_2}$ (the Urey reaction, run backwards). The CO₂ is **degassed by volcanoes** at arcs and ridges, also on ~10⁸ yr.
> Together with silicate weathering (the sink), these close the **long-term carbonate–silicate cycle**, a thermostat on 10⁵–10⁶ yr response times.

> [!example]- B4 (3.5 + 3.5) · Eccentricity and insolation, $\bar I\propto(1-e^2)^{-1/2}$, $R_p/R_a=(1-e)/(1+e)$ ([[CL17 Orbital Forcing and Glacial Cycles]])
> (a) $\dfrac{\bar I(e=0.0607)}{\bar I(e=0.005)}=\sqrt{\dfrac{1-0.005^2}{1-0.0607^2}}=1.00183$ ⇒ **0.18 %** (high precision is needed: $e^2/2$ effects).
> (b) Flux ∝ $1/R^2$: $\dfrac{I_p}{I_a}=\Big(\dfrac{1+e}{1-e}\Big)^2=1.275$. Perihelion receives **27.5 % more** than aphelion (equivalently, aphelion is 21.6 % less than perihelion). The seasonal effect of eccentricity (via precession) is ~100× its annual-mean effect.

## End-semester (25 Nov 2022)

### Section A (4 marks each)
> [!example]- A1 · Hydrostatic balance; a layer's pressure ([[CL06 Hydrostatic Balance and Vertical Structure]])
> (a) $\partial p/\partial z=-\rho g$: the vertical pressure-gradient force balances gravity almost exactly. Implication: vertical accelerations are tiny, so **vertical motions are weak** (cm/s to a few m/s) and are not given by a force balance. They are found from horizontal convergence (continuity).
> (b) Pressure at the top $=7\times10^7/1000=7.0\times10^4$ Pa; the layer adds $\rho gh=1.2\times9.8\times500=5880$ Pa. **$p_{bottom}=7.59\times10^4$ Pa ≈ 759 hPa.**

> [!example]- A2 · Two-layer greenhouse ([[CL08 Greenhouse Models]])
> Top of the atmosphere: $\sigma T_a^4=\frac{(1-\alpha)S}4\equiv\sigma T_e^4$ ⇒ $T_a=T_e$. Top layer: absorbs $\sigma T_b^4$ and emits $2\sigma T_a^4$ ⇒ $T_b=2^{1/4}T_e$. Bottom layer: $\sigma T_s^4+\sigma T_a^4=2\sigma T_b^4$ ⇒ $T_s^4=3T_e^4$ ⇒ **$T_s=3^{1/4}T_e$** (= 335 K for $T_e=255$: an over-estimate; the real atmosphere is not opaque and convection carries heat up).

> [!example]- A3 · The NH subpolar gyre and Ekman transport ([[CL19 Wind-Driven Ocean Circulation]])
> Westerlies on the gyre's southern flank and polar easterlies on its northern flank exert a **cyclonic** (anticlockwise) wind stress curl. **Ekman transport** is 90° to the right of the wind in the NH: southward under the westerlies and northward under the easterlies. Water **diverges** from the gyre centre, which lowers the sea surface (**Ekman suction**, upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water). Geostrophic flow around the low then runs anticlockwise. Its western boundary current, the Labrador Current, is intensified.

> [!example]- A4 · An Earth spinning east to west: balance on a stationary SH parcel, and deflection of a westward-moving parcel ([[CL12 Rotating Frames and the Coriolis Force]])
> At rest the balance is the same whatever the spin direction: the **poleward** horizontal component of gravity (oblate Earth) balances the **equatorward** horizontal centrifugal component. Now the planet spins *westward*, so a **westward-moving** parcel spins *faster* than the ground. It has more centrifugal force, so the equatorward component wins: in the SH it is deflected **north**, i.e. **to the right** of its motion. Reversing Ω reverses the Coriolis deflection (normally to the left in the SH).

> [!example]- A5 · A west wind of 100 km/h at 10 km, 40°N, ρ = 0.37 kg m⁻³ ([[CL13 Horizontal Motion - Geostrophic Balance and Friction]])
> **Geostrophic approximation** (steady, frictionless aloft, straight flow): $fu=-\frac1\rho\partial p/\partial y$. $f=2\Omega\sin40°=9.37\times10^{-5}$ s⁻¹, $u=27.8$ m/s: pressure-gradient force per mass $=2.6\times10^{-3}$ m s⁻². $|\partial p/\partial y|=\rho fu=$ **9.6 × 10⁻⁴ Pa m⁻¹** (≈ 1 hPa per 100 km). Pressure **decreases northward**: low pressure to the left of a westerly in the NH; the gradient force points north and Coriolis points south.

### Section B (5 marks each)
> [!example]- B1 · Equatorial Pacific mean state and the Bjerknes feedback ([[CL20 ENSO and the Walker Circulation]])
> **Mean state:**
> - easterly trades;
> - a **warm pool** in the west (SST ~29–30 °C, deep convection, rain) and a **cold tongue** in the east (equatorial and coastal upwelling);
> - the thermocline **deep in the west, shallow in the east**, and sea level ~40 cm higher in the west;
> - the **Walker circulation**: rising air over the warm pool, eastward flow aloft, sinking over the east Pacific.
>
> **If the trades weaken:** upwelling falls and warm water sloshes east (Kelvin waves), so the eastern thermocline deepens and eastern SST warms. The east–west SST and pressure gradients shrink, the Walker circulation and the trades weaken further: a **positive (Bjerknes) feedback** that drives El Niño.

> [!example]- B2 · Hydrostatic pressure profiles ([[CL06 Hydrostatic Balance and Vertical Structure]])
> $dp/dz=-\rho g=-\dfrac{pg}{RT}$ ($R$ the specific gas constant of air).
> (i) Isothermal at $T_s$: $p=p_se^{-gz/RT_s}$, scale height $H=RT_s/g\approx8.4$ km.
> (ii) $T=T_s-\Gamma z$: $\dfrac{dp}p=-\dfrac{g}{R}\dfrac{dz}{T_s-\Gamma z}$ ⇒ $p=p_s\Big(1-\dfrac{\Gamma z}{T_s}\Big)^{g/R\Gamma}$ (which tends to (i) as Γ → 0).

> [!example]- B3 · Seasonal variability; the Asian monsoon ([[CL15 Monsoons]], [[CL14 Observed Winds - Jets, Trades and the ITCZ]])
> (a) **Temperature** swings most at **mid and high latitudes** (especially over continents), where the seasonal range of insolation (sun angle and day length) is largest. **Precipitation** swings most in the **monsoon tropics**. Tropical rain is controlled by moisture convergence, which flips sign as the ITCZ and the circulation migrate. A small change in heating relocates where deep convection occurs, so rainfall goes from almost nothing to the yearly maximum.
> (b) In **winter**, a cold continent holds the Siberian high, so dry **northeasterly** winds blow off land. In **spring** the land (and the Tibetan Plateau) heats, and a thermal low forms over NW India and Pakistan. The pressure gradient **reverses** to point from ocean to land. Cross-equatorial flow from the SH is turned by Coriolis into the **southwesterly** monsoon, bringing moisture. **Latent heat release** over land deepens the low and the upper-level anticyclone (Tibetan high, tropical easterly jet), sustaining the circulation June–September. The reverse happens in autumn.

> [!example]- B4 · Radiation distribution (1–2 lines each) ([[CL10 Global Energy Balance and Meridional Structure]])
> (a) A lower sun angle spreads the beam over a larger area (∝ cos of zenith angle), and the path through the atmosphere is longer.
> (b) 24 h of daylight in June: the daily mean at 90°N is **544** W m⁻², against **493** at 30°N, though the sun is low.
> (c) The noon sun at the equator is highest at the **equinoxes** and lower at the solstices (declination ±23.5°). The Earth–Sun distance also varies (±3.4 % flux), so a semiannual cycle appears.
> (d) **Largest at the poles** (0 to > 500 W m⁻²); **smallest at the equator**.
> (e) The polar atmosphere is kept warm by **poleward heat transport** (atmosphere and ocean). Even with no sunlight the air (~200–250 K) radiates: $\sigma(205\ \text{K})^4\approx100$ W m⁻².
