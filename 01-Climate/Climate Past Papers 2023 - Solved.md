## Mid-semester (27 Sep 2023)
> [!example]- Section A (3 marks each)
> 1. **Inverse square** ([[CL03 Radiation and Planetary Energy Balance]]): Venus $1368/0.72^2=$ **2640 W m⁻²**; Mars $1368/1.52^2=$ **592 W m⁻²**.
> 2. **Angular momentum in the Hadley cell** ([[CL11 Mean Meridional Circulation and Angular Momentum]]): $(\Omega R\cos\phi+u)R\cos\phi=\Omega R^2$ ⇒ $u=\Omega R\dfrac{\sin^2\phi}{\cos\phi}=7.29\times10^{-5}\times6.38\times10^6\times\dfrac{0.25}{0.866}=$ **134 m/s** westerly at 30°N, far stronger than the observed subtropical jet (~30–40 m/s): eddies and friction remove momentum.
> 3. **Diurnal cycle**: Earth's rotation (day and night). **Seasonal cycle**: the axial tilt (with a small orbital-distance part). The seasonal cycle **grows towards higher latitudes**: the annual range of noon sun angle and of day length (0–24 h near the poles) increases with latitude. In the tropics insolation stays high all year.
> 4. **Momentum equation in the rotating frame** ([[CL12 Rotating Frames and the Coriolis Force]]): $\dfrac{D\mathbf v}{Dt}=-\dfrac1\rho\nabla p-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v+\mathbf g^*+\mathbf F_r$. The terms: acceleration relative to the Earth; the pressure-gradient force; the **Coriolis** force (deflects moving air, does no work); **effective gravity** (true gravity plus centrifugal, normal to the geoid); friction.
> 5. **Zonal-mean OLR** ([[CL10 Global Energy Balance and Meridional Structure]], [[CL28 Tutorials II - Earth's Energy Budget from Space (CERES)]]): **maxima in the subtropics** (~270–290 W m⁻²: warm surface, dry subsiding clear air); **a dip at the equator** (~230–250: cold-topped deep convective clouds and a humid column); a **decrease to the poles** (~150–200: cold surface and air). It varies much less with latitude than absorbed solar radiation does, so the tropics have a net surplus and the poles a deficit, which drives poleward transport.

> [!example]- Section B (5 marks each)
> 1. **An Earth spinning east to west**: see [[Climate Past Papers 2022 - Solved|2022]] ES A4. At rest, the horizontal gravity component (poleward) balances the horizontal centrifugal component (equatorward). An **eastward** wind now moves *against* the spin: it rotates slower than the ground, its centrifugal force is less, and gravity's poleward component wins. It is deflected **poleward**, to the **left** in the NH (opposite of the real Earth).
> 2. **Leaky one-layer model** ([[CL08 Greenhouse Models]]): the layer has emissivity = absorptivity ε. TOA: $\sigma T_e^4=(1-\epsilon)\sigma T_s^4+\epsilon\sigma T_a^4$. Layer: $\epsilon\sigma T_s^4=2\epsilon\sigma T_a^4$. Hence $T_s^4=\dfrac{2}{2-\epsilon}T_e^4$. With $T_e=254.9$ K (albedo 0.3, $S=1368$): $\epsilon=2-2(T_e/300)^4=$ **0.957**. [!verify] The question does not give the albedo; 0.3 is assumed.
> 3. **Thermal structure** ([[CL06 Hydrostatic Balance and Vertical Structure]], [[CL09 Radiative-Convective Equilibrium]]):
>    - **Troposphere** (0–~12 km): $T$ falls ~6.5 K/km. It is heated from below by the surface, and convection keeps the lapse rate near the moist adiabat.
>    - **Stratosphere** (to ~50 km): $T$ rises because **ozone absorbs UV**. It is stably stratified.
>    - **Mesosphere** (to ~85 km): $T$ falls. There is little ozone heating and CO₂ radiates heat away.
>    - **Thermosphere**: $T$ rises steeply as O₂ and N₂ absorb extreme UV and X-rays; the density is so low that each absorption heats a lot.

## End-semester (21 Nov 2023)
> [!example]- Section A (4 marks each)
> 1. **Pressure gradients in the Hadley cell** ([[CL11 Mean Meridional Circulation and Angular Momentum]]):
>    - Heating at the equator expands the air column (hypsometric equation: thickness ∝ T). Aloft, at a fixed height, pressure is **higher over the equator** than over the subtropics, so the upper air flows **poleward**.
>    - That outflow removes mass from the equatorial column, so **surface pressure falls** at the equator (the equatorial trough). Air piling up at ~30° builds the **subtropical high**.
>    - At the surface the gradient therefore points the other way and drives the trade winds **equatorward**.
> 2. **Tropical cyclone feedback** (wind-induced surface heat exchange): stronger winds evaporate more water from the warm sea. Convection in the eyewall releases that latent heat, which warms the core and lowers the central pressure; the larger pressure gradient strengthens the winds. **Warmer climate**: higher SST raises the potential intensity, and air holds ~7 %/K more vapour (Clausius–Clapeyron), so more fuel and more intense storms (even if the total number does not rise). ([[CL04 Feedback and Multiple Steady States]])
> 3. **Why a sea-breeze model is not enough for the Indian monsoon** ([[CL15 Monsoons]]):
>    - A sea breeze is a local, **diurnal** circulation driven by *sensible* heating contrasts over a few tens of km.
>    - The monsoon is **subcontinental and seasonal**. It involves cross-equatorial flow turned by **Coriolis** (the southwesterlies), the **northward migration of the ITCZ and tropical convergence zone**, and **latent heat** release (moist static energy) that keeps heating the atmosphere over land even after the surface has cooled under clouds and rain.
>    - It is helped by the elevated heating of the Tibetan Plateau.
>    - A sea breeze cannot explain the **onset timing, the 4-month length of the rainy season**, the active–break cycles, or the withdrawal. Land–sea temperature contrast alone would reverse within weeks once rain cools the land.

> [!example]- Section B (6 marks each)
> 1. **Orbital parameters and insolation** ([[CL17 Orbital Forcing and Glacial Cycles]]):
>    - **Eccentricity** (~100 and 400 kyr) changes the **annual global mean** insolation only slightly (∝ $(1-e^2)^{-1/2}$, ≤ 0.2 %). It changes the perihelion–aphelion contrast by up to ~27 %, and so **modulates the amplitude of precession**.
>    - **Obliquity** (41 kyr, 22.1–24.5°) changes the **seasonal contrast and latitudinal distribution**: higher tilt gives hotter summers and more annual insolation at high latitudes, less at low ones. It acts the same way in both hemispheres.
>    - **Precession** (~19–23 kyr) changes **in which season perihelion falls**: anti-phased seasonal contrast between the hemispheres, with no annual-mean change.
>
>    The ~100 kyr cycles follow from how these change **high-northern-latitude summer insolation** (65°N), amplified by ice-albedo and CO₂ feedbacks.
> 2. **Air–sea CO₂ exchange** ([[CL16 Atmospheric Evolution Greenhouse Gases and the Carbon Cycle]], [[CL29 The Ocean's Ledger VI - Slow Swings and the Ocean's Memory for Carbon]]). Three factors: (i) the **pCO₂ difference** between ocean and air, (ii) the **gas-transfer velocity** (wind speed, sea state), (iii) **solubility**, which falls with temperature and salinity. Biological drawdown and upwelling set the ocean's pCO₂.
>    - **Equatorial** oceans: upwelling of cold, CO₂-rich deep water that warms at the surface (solubility drops), so they are a **source** (outgassing; the equatorial Pacific is the largest natural source).
>    - **Subtropical** gyres: warm and stratified, roughly neutral. They are seasonally a source in summer, and their poleward-cooling flanks are **sinks**.
>    - **Polar and subpolar** oceans: cold water dissolves CO₂, biological production is high in spring, and deep-water formation carries the carbon down, so they are a strong **sink** (North Atlantic). Southern Ocean upwelling regions can be sources in places.
> 3. **The SH subtropical gyre** ([[CL19 Wind-Driven Ocean Circulation]], [[CL24 The Ocean's Ledger III - Wind and the Momentum Ledger]]):
>    - **Formation (3):** trades (easterlies) near 15°S and westerlies near 45°S. Ekman transport is **to the left** in the SH, so it converges toward ~30°S. Sea level rises there (Ekman pumping, downwelling), and geostrophic flow runs **anticlockwise** around the mound, which is anticyclonic for the SH. Friction and the β-effect close it.
>    - **(1):** the currents are broad, slow and **spread out on the eastern side** (Benguela, Humboldt). The fundamental cause is the **β-effect**, the increase of $|f|$ with latitude.
>    - **(2):** the **western** boundary (Brazil, Agulhas and East Australian currents) is **faster and deeper**: (i) the vorticity balance needs a narrow, fast current whose frictional shear offsets the wind's and planetary vorticity input (see [[Climate Past Papers 2020-2021 - Solved|2020]] Q3); (ii) mass conservation: the whole interior Sverdrup transport returns through a narrow band, so it must be fast (and deep).
