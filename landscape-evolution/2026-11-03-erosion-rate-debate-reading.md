# Erosion-rate debate: Herman et al., Schildgen et al., glacial erosion time scales

## Covered
This post is a reading list on one question: did global erosion rates in mountains increase in the late Cenozoic as climate cooled? No lecture content came with it. The summary below uses only the post and the one paper that could be opened.

- Paper 1 (named in the post, not linked): Herman and others, a global compilation of thermochronological data used to infer recent variation in erosion rate. The Schildgen et al. reference list gives it as Herman, F. et al., "Worldwide acceleration of mountain erosion under a cooling climate", Nature 504, 423-426 (2013). The post does not name the paper, so this identification is an inference. I did not read it.
- Paper 2 (the opposing view), Schildgen, van der Beek, Sinclair and Thiede, "Spatial correlation bias in late-Cenozoic erosion histories derived from thermochronology", Nature 559, 89-93 (2018). Abstract:
  - A nearly twofold increase in erosion rate in mountainous landscapes over late-Cenozoic times had been inferred from a global thermochronology compilation (the Herman et al. result).
  - The authors re-examined the 30 locations with reported accelerated late-Cenozoic erosion. In 23 of them the reported increase is a spatial correlation bias: data with disparate exhumation histories are combined, so spatial erosion-rate variations are converted into temporal increases.
  - In 4 locations the increase can be explained by changes in tectonic boundary conditions. In 3 locations a climatically induced acceleration is recorded, driven by localized glacial valley incision.
  - Conclusion: thermochronology data currently have insufficient resolution to assess whether late-Cenozoic climate change affected erosion rates on a global scale. They suggest a synthesis of local findings that include location-specific information.
  - Background in the abstract: sedimentation-rate records suggested a global increase, but other studies argue that rates stayed steady and that the sedimentary increases come from preservation biases, depositional hiatuses and varying measurement intervals. Others argued that the thermochronology result has biases from how the data are averaged and from erosion hiatuses in glaciated landscapes.
- Paper 3 (Science Advances, doi 10.1126/sciadv.1600204): on time-scale-dependent estimates of glacial erosion. The page could not be opened (Forbidden). The Schildgen reference list cites a paper with the matching ID: Ganti, V. et al., "Time scale bias in erosion rates of glaciated landscapes", Sci. Adv. 2, e1600204 (2016). I take it to be this one, but I have not read it, so no content is recorded here.

Exam-relevant ideas (all from the Schildgen abstract): a measured erosion rate depends on the interval and the sampling over which it is averaged; spatial variation can look like temporal change; and the thermochronology resolution limits what can be concluded.
