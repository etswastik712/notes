# Geomorphic Features of the Deccan Traps Terrain (2026-10-24)

Slides by Dr. Shilpa Patil (Dept. of ECS, IISER Pune), posted on Classroom with an announcement. (Classroom: Geomorphic Features of the Deccan Traps Terrain.pdf)

## Covered

**Terminology**
- *Large Igneous Province (LIP)*: the Deccan Volcanic Province is one of the largest in the world; the slides call it the primary cause of the Cretaceous-Paleogene mass extinction.
- *Deccan*: from Sanskrit Dakṣiṇa ("south"), via Prakrit Dakkhiṇa, to Deccan/Dekan; the Deccan Plateau is the plateau of southern/peninsular India.
- *Trap*: from Swedish *trapp*, related to *trappa* ("stair"). Chain given: successive lava flows → horizontal basalt layers → differential weathering/erosion → step-like slopes → "traps".
- *Tholeiitic*: the dominant basalt type (about 90% of province volume).
- *Intertrappean beds*: thin sedimentary layers (1 m to over 20 m thick) sandwiched between successive basalt flows.
- *Boles* (red boles, green boles): interflow horizons. Two origins given: (a) paleosol profiles formed by in-situ physical and chemical weathering of the flow tops of lower flows; (b) ash-fall deposits.
- *Etchplanation* and *pediplanation*: the two planation processes named below.

**The province (slide 2, 5; after Kale et al.)**
- Highest exposed continuous stack: 1642 m at Mt. Kalsubai. Onland composite stratigraphic thickness about 3000 m. (The slides also give Kalsubai as 1646-1660 m, base to top, and 1,646 m as peak elevation, so the figure varies between slides.)
- Onland erupted volume about 6,17,700 km³ (Indian digit grouping, i.e. 617,700 km³). Two areas are shown, about 380,000 km² and about 500,000 km²; the extracted text doesn't say which label belongs to which.
- Composition by volume: massive, overwhelmingly tholeiitic continental flood basalts (about 90% of the province); the rest is tholeiitic to silicic volcanics (rhyolites, trachytes, pyroclastic tuffs), subaqueous marine pillow basalts, alkaline igneous complexes, mafic dyke swarms and minor localized continental flood basalts. Volumes shown on the figure: 1,93,000 km³ and 4,39,000 km³ (labels not recoverable from the text).

**Three major geomorphic features**
1. *Konkan Coastal Belt (KCB)*: narrow low-lying coastal strip, under 100 km wide, elevation generally under 100 m asl. Drained by short, high-gradient westerly rivers with rapid headward incision into basalt. Underlain by the Panvel Flexure and north-south fault zones tied to early Cenozoic rifting.
2. *Western Ghats Escarpment (WGE)*: continuous ridge crest rising 500-1700 m, about 1,200 km long, separating the coastal lowlands from the interior Deccan Upland. Exposes stacked subhorizontal basalt flows in multi-tiered cliff faces. It is the major drainage divide, the primary continental divide of Peninsular India. Origin: post-Cretaceous rifting and scarp retreat.
3. *Deccan Plateau and Malwa Plateau* (interior; the Balaghat Range is also marked on the cross-section with Pune).

**Four controls over about 66 million years** (summary table, slide 10)

| Control | Resulting feature |
|---|---|
| 1. Effusion rate and flow type | Sheet flows → flat plateaus/mesas; hummocky flows → undulating tumuli fields |
| 2. Lithology and differential resistance | Alternating hard/soft layers → step-like "trap" terraces and escarpments |
| 3. Structural/tectonic uplift | Regional eastward tilt → asymmetric continental drainage divide |
| 4. Monsoonal climate and erosion | Deep river gorges, laterite duricrusts, rapid scarp retreat along the Ghats |

**1. Volcanological and eruptive style (primary control)**
- Low effusion rates → compound, hummocky pāhoehoe flow fields with inflated tumuli, lava lobes, inflation clefts and knobby micro-relief. Lobes: length a few metres to 100s of metres, thickness under tens of metres (example: Bhuleshwar Ghat section, SE of Pune).
- High effusion rates → extensive flat sheet flows and ʻaʻā flows forming broad, high plateaus and steep cliffs. Areal extent 10-100+ km²; crust type inflated vesicular; emplacement by endogenous transfer (examples: Tamhini Ghat west of Pune; Gadaghat section, NH3, Madhya Pradesh).
- Effusion-rate table (volumetric rate, flow type and landform):
  - Low, under 10 m³/s: compound hummocky pāhoehoe (tumuli, toes, inflation clefts).
  - Moderate, 10-1,000 m³/s: channelized flows, transitional pāhoehoe-to-ʻaʻā fields.
  - Extreme (flood level), 1,000-100,000+ m³/s: massive sheet flows (flat plateaus, sheer escarpments).
- Internal structure: thermal contraction on cooling produces columnar jointing (prismatic basalt columns). This controls how cliffs collapse, how scarp retreat occurs and how vertical rock faces are maintained. Examples: entablature columnar joints at Singhania, MP; multitiered colonnade joints.
- Flow-lobe structure (after Sheth 2020): a lobe has a crust (upper vesicular zone UVZ), a massive core, and a base with a lower vesicular zone (LVZ).

**2. Lithology and differential resistance**
- The signature step-like topography comes from differential weathering of alternating hard (massive core) and soft (interflow horizons such as intertrappeans, red boles, green boles, flow-top breccia FTB) layers. Mangi Tungi is shown as an example.

**3. Structural/tectonic uplift**
- Regional eastward tilt: post-eruptive crustal warping tilted Peninsular India east, giving an asymmetric trans-peninsular divide. Godavari, Krishna and Bhima rise near the Western Ghats crest and flow east over 700 km to the Bay of Bengal (after Kale et al. 2017).
- Segmented Quaternary block faulting (figures from Kale et al. 2021).
- Fluvial incision: bedrock gorges, entrenched meanders, knickpoints and potholes. Examples: River Mula (SE of Bota, N of Pune); a natural arch about 4 m high and 12 m wide at the head of a knickpoint on a tributary of the Kukdi at Ane Ghat; a network of potholes in a linear fracture-controlled channel of the Kukadi near Nighoj. Mechanism stated for the constricted fracture-bound trench: the river squeezes from a wide valley into a narrow rock trench, which increases hydraulic head, kinetic energy and flow velocity during monsoon floods.

**4. Monsoonal climate and erosion**
- Tropical monsoonal weathering produces planation surfaces (palaeoplains, erosion surfaces): multi-tiered, elevated flat tablelands at about 1,330, 1,225, 1,000, 920, 700 and 600 m above mean sea level.
- Development is governed by: (1) long-term tropical weathering (etchplanation); (2) passive-margin scarp retreat (pediplanation); (3) lithological differential erosion; (4) later Quaternary neotectonic block displacement.
- Laterite cappings (after Ollier and Sheth 2008) are the other signature of tropical weathering.

**Sources cited on the slides** (for further reading): Kale et al. 2017, 2021, 2026; Sheth 2020; Ollier and Sheth 2008; Kapur et al. 2018; Wilson Mantilla et al. 2022.
