## Mid-semester (23 Sep 2024)

### Q1 (2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 3) · Faults of the Himalaya and Tibet
> [!question] A map of the faults around the Himalaya and Tibet (India moving north at 40–50 mm/yr, with dates of major earthquakes) and a cross-section through the HFT, MBT, MCT and the STD above the Main Himalayan Thrust. The MHT is "presently locked" for ~100 km north of the HFT, and its creeping segment lies further north. (a) Explain the major strike-slip faults around Tibet. (b) Explain the N–S normal faults in the middle of the plateau. (c) How do the activation ages of the MCT, MBT and HFT vary, and why? (d) Where do you expect high uplift rates, and what is the evidence? (e) Why is the plateau ~5 km high? (f) From the convergence rate, the locked width and a recurrence of a few hundred years, estimate the friction coefficient $\mu$ of the MHT ($E\approx70$ GPa, $\rho\approx3\times10^3$ kg m⁻³, $g\approx10$ m s⁻²).

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS09 Faults and Earthquakes]], [[LS10 Folds Flexure and Mountain Building]], [[LS07 Isostasy]], [[LS08 Plate Tectonics Strain Stress and Convection]])
> (a) **Lateral extrusion of Tibet.** India indents Asia like a rigid punch. Part of the convergence is taken up not by thickening but by squeezing Tibetan crust out **eastward** along large strike-slip faults: the Altyn Tagh and Kunlun (left-lateral) and the Karakoram and Red River (right-lateral, at times).
> (b) **E–W extension of a high plateau.** The plateau stores so much gravitational potential energy that its crust cannot support more thickening. N–S compression continues, but the plateau spreads east–west under its own weight, so **N–S striking rifts (normal faults)** open. This is gravitational collapse and spreading, helped by the oblique, radial convergence along the arc.
> (c) **They young southward: MCT (~20–23 Ma) > MBT (~10–5 Ma) > HFT (< 2 Ma, active).** The thrust wedge grows by **in-sequence forward propagation**. As the wedge thickens it must keep its critical taper (LS10), so deformation steps to a new thrust in the foreland, the older thrusts are carried piggyback and become inactive, and the front moves south onto the Indian plate.
> (d) Uplift is **high above the ramp of the MHT**, where the flat steepens ~100 km north of the HFT, at the front of the Higher Himalaya (the locked–creeping transition in the lower panel). Rock riding up the ramp is uplifted at $v\sin(\text{ramp dip})$. Evidence:
> - an abrupt rise in mean elevation and relief (the physiographic transition);
> - steep river channels with knickpoints and high channel-steepness indices;
> - young thermochronological cooling ages, i.e. fast exhumation;
> - a band of microseismicity and of GPS-measured uplift at the edge of the locked zone;
> - the monsoon rainfall peak.
>
> There is also uplift at the HFT itself: uplifted Siwalik river terraces record ~cm/yr growth of frontal folds. ([[Strath Terrace]], [[Knickpoint]])
> (e) **Isostasy of a doubled crust.** The crust under Tibet is ~70 km thick versus ~35 km normally. The extra 35 km of crust floats higher by $35(1-\rho_c/\rho_m)=35(1-2800/3300)\approx\mathbf{5.3}$ **km**. It cannot rise much higher: the crust's strength limits it, and the E–W normal faults of (b) show the plateau is already at the limit.
> (f) Strain builds up in the locked zone between earthquakes.
> - Convergence across the Himalaya is $v\approx20$ mm/yr (the 40–50 mm/yr on the map is India–Eurasia; take either).
> - Recurrence $T\approx300$–500 yr, so the slip deficit is $d=vT\approx10$–14 m.
> - Locked width $W\approx100$ km gives strain $d/W\approx10^{-4}$.
> - Stress drop $\Delta\tau\approx E\,d/W=70\ \text{GPa}\times10^{-4}\approx7$–9 MPa.
> - Normal stress at the mean depth of the locked MHT (~10 km): $\rho gz=3\times10^3\times10\times10^4=300$ MPa.
>
> If the fault slips when the shear stress reaches $\mu\sigma_n$, then $\mu\approx\Delta\tau/\sigma_n\approx\mathbf{0.02\text{–}0.03}$. **The MHT is extremely weak** compared with Byerlee friction (0.6–0.85, [[LS06 Maximum Topography]]). This points to near-lithostatic pore pressure (fluids released by the underthrust plate) or weak clay and gouge on the décollement. [!verify] This treats the stress drop as the whole frictional strength; any residual shear stress would raise $\mu$.

### Q2 (2 + 1 + 2 + 1) · A dense liquid over a light one
> [!question] (a) Two horizontal layers of immiscible liquids, the upper one denser. Can this be in equilibrium? Plot $p(z)$. (b) Force balance on a small square at the interface. (c) Repeat for a slightly tilted interface: what does it say about stability? (d) How is this related to the evenly spaced volcanoes of the South Georgia / South Sandwich Islands?

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS07 Isostasy]], [[Landscapes Past Papers 2021 - Solved]] Q3)
> (a) **Yes, an equilibrium exists**: with a horizontal interface, pressure is a function of depth only and every parcel's weight is balanced. $p(z)$ is two straight lines, **steeper in the upper (denser) layer**, slopes $\rho_1g>\rho_2g$. But the equilibrium is **unstable**.
> (b) A square straddling a horizontal interface: the pressures on its left and right faces are equal at every depth, so there is no net horizontal force. Vertically, the pressure difference between top and bottom balances the weight of each part. It stays put.
> (c) Tilt the interface by a small angle. Along a horizontal line through the tilted zone, one side has more dense fluid above it than the other, so the pressure differs across the square: $\Delta p=(\rho_1-\rho_2)g\,\Delta h$. This **horizontal pressure gradient** pushes dense fluid down the tilt, so the tilt *grows*. Heavy over light is **Rayleigh–Taylor unstable** (light over heavy, the gradient flattens the tilt: stable). Any small undulation grows fastest at a wavelength ~2–3 times the layer thickness (set by viscosity and layer depths).
> (d) Beneath an arc, a **buoyant layer** (partial melt or hydrated, low-density mantle wedge material) lies under denser overlying mantle or lithosphere. It is Rayleigh–Taylor unstable and breaks up into **diapirs at the fastest-growing wavelength**. Each diapir feeds a volcano, so the volcanoes come out **regularly spaced** along the arc (tens of km apart, about the instability wavelength).

### Q3 (2 + 2 + 2) · Soil production and water chemistry
> [!question] (a) If the regolith production rate at the bedrock varies with depth as $p(z)=p_0e^{-z/\lambda}$ and the surface erosion rate is $\epsilon$, what is the steady soil thickness? (b) Feedbacks that make production fall as the soil thickens? (c) Why is river water ~100 ppm in dissolved salts and groundwater several thousand ppm?

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS11 Weathering and Regolith]], [[LS12 Hillslope Transport and the Diffusion Equation]], [[LS25 Water in the Landscape]])
> (a) Steady state: production at the soil base = erosion, $p_0e^{-h/\lambda}=\epsilon$ ⇒ $\boxed{h^*=\lambda\ln(p_0/\epsilon)}$ if $p_0>\epsilon$. If $\epsilon>p_0$ the soil is stripped: bare bedrock. This is stable: a thicker soil produces less and thins back.
> (b) The soil shields the bedrock:
> - it damps **temperature swings** (less frost cracking and thermal stress);
> - **roots and burrowing** animals rarely reach deep, so biotic fracturing falls off;
> - infiltrating water reaches the base already **near chemical saturation** and with less CO₂ (acid), so dissolution slows;
> - deeper water flow is slower, so solutes are removed less.
>
> Each effect makes $p$ fall with depth.
> (c) **Contact time.** River water is mostly recent rain and quick runoff (days to months in contact with rock), so it picks up little. Groundwater moves slowly through pores for years to millennia, with a large rock surface per volume of water, so it approaches **equilibrium with the minerals**. In dry regions evaporation concentrates it further, and deep groundwater may contain old (connate) brines. Rivers are diluted by direct runoff; their base flow is groundwater.

### Q4 (2 + 2 + 2) · The top 5000 mountain peaks
> [!question] The top 5000 peaks: latitude against longitude (a map), elevation against latitude, and elevation against longitude. Describe the patterns and explain them.

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS06 Maximum Topography]], [[LS14 Glaciers Mass Balance and Ice Flow]], [[LS10 Folds Flexure and Mountain Building]])
> - **The map:** the peaks cluster along **active convergent belts**: Himalaya–Tibet–Karakoram (~25–45°N, 70–100°E), the **Andes** (a N–S line near 70°W, ~10°N–50°S), Alaska and the Cordillera, and a few Alpine and African peaks. There are none on stable cratons or passive margins. High peaks need active uplift; once it stops, erosion removes them.
> - **Elevation against latitude:** the maximum is near **30°N** (the Himalaya, ~8–8.8 km). The tropical and subtropical Andes reach 6–7 km. **Maximum elevation falls towards the poles** (~4–5 km by 60°). One reason is the **glacial buzzsaw**: glaciers erode efficiently above the snowline (ELA), which falls with latitude, so peaks cannot stay far above it. Another is that the major collision zones happen to lie at low to mid latitudes.
> - **Elevation against longitude:** two dominant spikes, the Andes (~70°W) and the Himalaya (~85°E). The Himalayan spike is the tallest, and its top (~8.8 km) is close to the **strength limit** of LS06 (~10 km for 100 MPa). No peak exceeds it.

### Q5 (2) · All stresses in a square
> [!question] Compressive stresses $\sigma_1$ (vertical) and $\sigma_1+\delta\sigma$ (horizontal). On a plane at angle $\theta$, $\sigma=\sigma_1+\tfrac12\delta\sigma(1+\cos2\theta)$ [the sheet's $\sigma_1+\tfrac12\delta\sigma\cos2\theta$ omits the mean term] and $\tau=-\tfrac12\delta\sigma\sin2\theta$. Plot all possible $(\sigma,\tau)$.

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS06 Maximum Topography]] eq. (16), Mohr circle)
> Eliminate $2\theta$: $\big(\sigma-(\sigma_1+\tfrac12\delta\sigma)\big)^2+\tau^2=(\tfrac12\delta\sigma)^2$. This is the **Mohr circle**: centre $(\sigma_1+\delta\sigma/2,\,0)$, radius $\delta\sigma/2$. It passes through $(\sigma_1,0)$ and $(\sigma_1+\delta\sigma,0)$, the planes with no shear. The maximum shear $\delta\sigma/2$ acts on planes at 45°. Going once round the circle corresponds to $\theta$ running through 180°. [!verify] As printed, the normal-stress formula's centre is $\sigma_1$; with principal stresses $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_1+\delta\sigma$, the mean stress $\sigma_1+\delta\sigma/2$ must be the centre (the student's annotation "$\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta$" is the step that recovers it).

## End-semester (22 Nov 2024)

### Q1 (1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2) · The Western Ghats escarpment
> [!question] (a) From a DEM of peninsular India, how would you identify the Western Ghats (WG)? (b) Why is the WG called a Great Escarpment, and are there similar ranges elsewhere? (c) The elevation profiles $z(x)$ of the west-flowing rivers draining the escarpment have inverse power-law forms. Explain. (d) Plot such a profile. (e) Catchment-averaged ¹⁰Be erosion rates rise linearly with catchment slope: $E=(15\pm7.8)+(123\pm27)S$ m/Myr. Explain. (f) What does this relation say about the evolution of the distance between the escarpment and the coast?

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS18 Deccan Traps Terrain]], [[LS28 Deccan Field Guide Lava Architecture and Sahyadri Stratigraphy]], [[LS23 Bedrock Channels and River Incision]], [[LS10 Folds Flexure and Mountain Building]], [[Cosmogenic Erosion Rate]], [[Stream Power Law]])
> (a) Compute slope and local relief (e.g. in a 5 km window) and trace the drainage divide. The WG is a **continuous belt of steep slopes and ~1 km local relief, parallel to the west coast and ~40–80 km inland**. It separates a low coastal plain from a high, gently east-tilted plateau, and the main **drainage divide runs along its crest**, i.e. the divide is strongly asymmetric. Swath profiles perpendicular to the coast show a step, not a ridge.
> (b) It is a **passive-margin escarpment**: a single, continuous, seaward-facing scarp ~1 km high and > 1500 km long, formed after continental rifting (the Seychelles–India split, ~65 Ma). It stands well inland of the coast and is not a fold or thrust mountain belt. Similar great escarpments: the **Drakensberg** (South Africa), southeastern **Australia**, the **Serra do Mar** (SE Brazil), **Namibia**, and the Red Sea margins (Yemen, Saudi Arabia).
> (c) Combine **steady-state stream power** with Hack's law. Erosion $E=KA^mS^n=U$ gives $S=(U/K)^{1/n}A^{-\theta}$ with $\theta=m/n$. With $A\propto x^{h}$ ($h\approx1.7$, $x$ = distance from the divide), $S\propto x^{-\theta h}$. For $\theta h>1$ this integrates to $z-z_0\propto x^{1-\theta h}$, **an inverse power law**: very steep near the head, flattening quickly. Short west-coast rivers with large concavity and closely spaced base levels (the sea is ~50 km away) fit this.
> (d) Sketch: elevation ~1000–1200 m at the divide; an almost vertical upper reach (waterfalls, knickpoints at the scarp, [[Knickpoint]]); a rapid flattening; a long low gradient over the coastal plain to sea level. Strongly **concave**, like $1/x$.
> (e) At low to moderate slopes, the erosion rate **∝ slope** follows from slope-dependent transport. Linear hillslope diffusion gives a flux $q=KS$, and stream power with $n\approx1$ gives $E\propto S$ at fixed area. The 10–30 % mean slopes here are below the threshold (~0.6) at which landslides would make $E$ rise steeply (nonlinear). The intercept (~15 m/Myr) is the background lowering of the flat plateau (chemical weathering, slow soil creep).
> (f) The scarp face (mean slope ~0.3) erodes vertically at $E\approx15+123\times0.3\approx52$ m/Myr. A face of slope $S$ eroding vertically at $E$ retreats horizontally at $E/S\approx\mathbf{170}$ **m/Myr ≈ 0.17 km/Myr**. The plateau behind it (S ≈ 0.02) lowers at only ~17 m/Myr, so the scarp **persists and retreats slowly, and the escarpment–coast distance grows**, by ~10 km since ~65 Ma at today's rates. The WG now lies ~50–80 km inland, so either the escarpment began well inland (e.g. at a rift-flank or flexural shoulder, LS10) or it retreated faster early on. Today it is nearly static.

### Q2 (1 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 1) · From the group presentations
> [!question] (a) What makes some parts of Majuli river island more prone to bank erosion? (b) How would you find places in the Himalaya where permafrost may exist, and which landform signals degrading permafrost? (c) Arctic coasts: block collapse of the shoreline (photo, earth.org), increasingly common. Explain the rapid retreat. (d) How do submarine channels form off the mouths of big rivers like the Ganga and Indus, and why do the submarine gorges narrow downstream? (e) Mechanisms of river-network reorganisation in and around the Himalaya, and their effect on relief and elevation? (f) How would you identify a palaeo-river bed in an arid region from satellite data?

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS16 Fluvial Landscapes]], [[LS22 Floods Planform and River Profiles]], [[LS19 Glacial and Aeolian Landforms]], [[LS27 Coasts Beaches Deltas and Rocky Shores]], [[LS24 Sediment Transport Mechanics]], [[LS23 Bedrock Channels and River Incision]])
> (a) Majuli sits in the **braided Brahmaputra**: [[Braided Rivers]]. Banks erode fastest where:
> - the banks are **non-cohesive sand and silt** (no clay, little vegetation);
> - mid-channel bars **deflect the main flow (thalweg) against the bank**: outer bends, anabranch confluences and channel-switching zones;
> - the river aggrades and its floods are large;
> - the water level **drops fast after a flood**, so pore pressure in the bank makes it fail;
> - embankments and spurs upstream redirect the flow.
>
> (b) Permafrost needs a mean annual ground temperature below 0 °C, i.e. mean annual air temperature below about −1 to −2 °C. Map it with:
> - air temperature from station or reanalysis data, extrapolated with the lapse rate (~6.5 K/km) to find the elevation band, roughly > 4500–5000 m;
> - north-facing, shaded slopes;
> - inventories of **rock glaciers** (active rock glaciers indicate permafrost);
> - InSAR creep.
>
> **Degrading permafrost:** **thermokarst** (thaw lakes, subsidence, retrogressive thaw slumps), and accelerating or destabilising rock glaciers.
> (c) **Thermo-abrasion of ice-rich permafrost bluffs.** Warm sea water and waves melt a **thermo-erosional notch** at the base of the cliff, and the overhanging block of frozen ground then **collapses as one piece** (as in the photo). The falling blocks thaw quickly and are carried away. Retreat is accelerating because **sea ice now lasts a shorter season** (more open water, longer fetch, more storm waves reaching the coast), and because warmer air and water thaw the ice-rich sediment and sea level is rising. ([[Rocky Coast Erosion]], [[Coastline Instability]])
> (d) At the shelf edge, sediment-laden **turbidity currents** (and [[Hyperpycnal Flow|hyperpycnal]] river plumes in flood) erode **submarine canyons**, such as the Ganga's *Swatch of No Ground* (heading close to the delta, which helps). Lowstands of sea level, when rivers reached the shelf edge, cut them deeper. Downstream, on the fan, the currents **lose water and sediment by overspilling their levees (flow stripping)** and by deposition. Their discharge *falls* with distance, the reverse of a river, so the channels become narrower and shallower until they end in lobes.
> (e) Mechanisms:
> - **river capture** (piracy) by headward erosion of a steeper, lower river;
> - **divide migration** driven by uneven erosion or rainfall across the divide;
> - tectonic **uplift and growing folds deflecting rivers** (versus antecedent rivers that cut through);
> - damming by landslides or glaciers, and lake overflow.
>
> Effects: the capturing river gains area and its base level drops, so knickpoints and a wave of incision travel upstream: higher local relief and erosion. The beheaded basin loses discharge, aggrades and its relief decays. Capture also redistributes the sediment flux, so mean elevations adjust isostatically. Analysis: χ maps ([[Chi Transform]]) show where divides are moving.
> (f) Look for **sinuous, branching linear features** that do not match the present drainage:
> - optical/IR images: linear vegetation, moisture or tone anomalies along buried channels (e.g. the Ghaggar–Hakra palaeochannels of Rajasthan in Landsat);
> - **L-band radar**, which penetrates dry sand and reveals buried channels (as with the shuttle radar over the eastern Sahara);
> - subtle ridges (inverted channels) or troughs in a DEM;
> - thermal-inertia anomalies;
> - lines of wells and settlements.

### Q3 (1 + 2 + 2 + 2) · Flexure of the Indian plate
> [!question] (a) Why is the lithosphere a good thin elastic plate? (b) A thin plate, initially horizontal, loaded at its edge $x=0$, deflects as $w(x)=-ce^{-x/\alpha}\cos(x/\alpha)$ for $x>0$. Plot $w$ against $x/\alpha$. (c) Appropriate $c$ and $\alpha$ for the Indian plate loaded by the Himalaya? (d) Which large-scale features of the subcontinent fit this picture?

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS10 Folds Flexure and Mountain Building]], [[LS07 Isostasy]])
> (a) Its elastic thickness (~10–70 km) is much smaller than the horizontal scale of the loads and of the bending (hundreds of km). Strains are small. The plate floats on a fluid-like asthenosphere that provides a buoyant restoring force. These are exactly the thin-plate assumptions: stresses vary linearly across the plate and shear across it is negligible.
> (b) This is the **broken-plate** (free-end) solution. $w(0)=-c$ (deepest, at the load). It crosses **zero at $x/\alpha=\pi/2$** and reaches a **forebulge** at $x/\alpha=3\pi/4$ of height $+0.067c$ (larger than the 0.043 of the continuous plate, [[Landscapes Past Papers 2021 - Solved]] Q5). It is essentially flat beyond $x/\alpha\approx4$.
> (c) $c$ is the **depth of the foredeep at the mountain front**: the Gangetic basin basement there is **~5–6 km** deep. $\alpha$ follows from the forebulge distance $3\pi\alpha/4$. With the bulge ~200–300 km south of the front, $\alpha\approx\mathbf{80\text{–}100}$ **km**. That is consistent with LS10's sediment-filled value (82 km for $T_e=25$ km), and with the 100 km quoted in 2021.
> (d) The **Indo-Gangetic foreland basin**: a sediment-filled trough parallel to the Himalaya, deepest at the front and wedge-shaped southward. A **peripheral bulge** further south. [!verify] Usually taken as the Bundelkhand–Vindhyan uplands and the buried Delhi–Haridwar and Faizabad ridges; general knowledge. The north-flowing rivers that drain these uplands (Chambal, Betwa, Ken, Son) also fit.

### Q4 (2 + 1) · Hack's rule
> [!question] (a) Justify Hack's rule for alluvial rivers, slope × distance ≈ constant along the profile. (b) Reconcile it with the fact that riverbed sediment fines downstream.

> [!example]- Solution ([[LS22 Floods Planform and River Profiles]], [[LS24 Sediment Transport Mechanics]], [[LS21 River Hydraulics and Hydraulic Geometry]], [[Shields Criterion]], [[Hydraulic Geometry]])
> (a) An alluvial river is **graded**: at bankfull flow its bed is near the threshold or transport state set by the **Shields number**, $\tau^*=\rho ghS/[(\rho_s-\rho)gD]\approx$ const. So $S\propto D/h$. Depth grows with discharge ($h\propto Q^{0.4}$, hydraulic geometry), discharge with area ($Q\propto A$), and area with length (Hack's law $A\propto L^{1.7}$). Then $S\propto D\,L^{-0.7}$. Alternatively, a stream-power or transport-limited concavity $\theta\approx0.6$ gives $S\propto A^{-0.6}\propto L^{-1}$. Since $\theta h\approx0.6\times1.7\approx1$, $S\cdot L\approx$ const, a **logarithmic profile** $z=z_0-k\ln L$. Hack's own empirical form was $S\propto(D/A)^{0.6}$, i.e. $S\propto D^{0.6}/L$.
> (b) Hack's form carries the grain size: $SL\propto D^{0.6}$. "SL = const" holds **along a reach of uniform grain size**. Downstream fining (abrasion, selective transport) lowers $D$, so the slope must fall **faster** than $1/L$. Profiles are more concave than logarithmic, and the gradient index $SL$ steps down where the bed changes from gravel to sand. Read the other way, a jump in $SL$ along a river flags a change in lithology, grain size or uplift ([[Knickpoint]]).

### Q5 (2) · Argon in biotite
> [!question] Biotite contains trace ⁴⁰K, which decays to ⁴⁰Ar. Argon diffuses rapidly out of the mineral above ~300 °C but is trapped below. What controls the ⁴⁰Ar content of biotite in surface rocks, and how does it help understand landscape evolution?

> [!example]- Solution ([[LSX1 Clocks in Rocks]], [[Closure Temperature]], [[Radioactive Decay Law]])
> ⁴⁰Ar accumulates only after the rock **cooled below the closure temperature (~300 °C)**. Its amount depends on:
> - the K content;
> - the **time since cooling** through closure (the decay clock: ⁴⁰Ar/⁴⁰K grows as $e^{\lambda t}-1$);
> - any reheating that resets it (metamorphism, intrusions);
> - excess or inherited argon;
> - argon loss by weathering or alteration.
>
> **Landscapes:** a rock cools as erosion brings it towards the surface. With a geothermal gradient of 25–30 °C/km, the 300 °C isotherm lies ~10–12 km down, so a biotite cooling age $t$ gives a long-term **exhumation rate ≈ 10–12 km / $t$**. **Young** Ar–Ar biotite ages (a few Myr) in the Higher Himalaya mean exhumation of several mm/yr. Old ages on cratons mean slow erosion. Maps of ages show where erosion has been concentrated (e.g. above the MHT ramp, mid-sem Q1d). Combined with lower-temperature clocks (apatite fission track and (U-Th)/He) they give a cooling path.
