## Frame 3: Where water sinks  [fr_11]
NOTES: Beat 1 (≈4 min here, Smooth). The data scientist's hat. Yesterday's map of surface density (WOCE annual mean; navy denser than 27.4). Tracers (oxygen, CFCs, radiocarbon) show that deep water forms today only at high latitudes in the North Atlantic and in the Southern Ocean (Hartmann p. 224; Stewart p. 211: "Bottom water is produced only in these two regions").
- North Atlantic: the Gulf Stream carries warm, salty water north into the Norwegian and Greenland Seas, where very cold air cools it until it is dense enough to sink (Hartmann p. 224). The Labrador Sea also makes deep water (Stewart Fig 13.1 names it).
- Around Antarctica, especially the Weddell and Ross Seas: brine from sea ice, yesterday's slide (Hartmann pp. 224-225).
- Convection happens in patches a few km across; the sinking that matters for the mass budget is spread over larger areas (Stewart p. 219). Don't go further than that.

## Frame 4: How much  [fr_21]
NOTES: Hold: how much. Hartmann p. 230: deep-water formation averages 1.5-2 × 10¹⁰ kg/s in the North Atlantic, about 15-20 Sv, and about 1 × 10¹⁰ kg/s (≈ 10 Sv) around Antarctica. A sverdrup (Sv) is a million cubic metres a second; the ACC on Wednesday was ~125 Sv, and the Gulf Stream off Florida about 30 Sv (Hartmann pp. 225, 229).
- Don't repeat Hartmann's "more than a factor of ten smaller than the western boundary currents" (p. 230): his own boundary-current figure is ~60 Sv, so his numbers give a factor of 3-4. Say "smaller than the Gulf Stream, but it fills the whole deep ocean".

## Frame 12: Wednesday's wind  [fr_54]
NOTES: Hold: what pulls it up. The westerlies blow east (out of the page). South of the equator Ekman transport is to the left of the wind: north. Poleward of the jet more water leaves than arrives, so water comes up from below; equatorward of it, it is pushed down (Hartmann pp. 225-227). This is the teal belt on Wednesday's map. The same wind physics drives the slow ocean's return: that is the tie between O4 and O6.
- If asked about eddies: they matter a lot here and partly cancel the wind's overturning (Hartmann pp. 227, 231). One sentence.

## Frame 14: How slow?  [fr_60]
NOTES: Beat 4 (≈5 min, Smooth). Week 2's ruler: τ = C/λ, with ~140 years for 4,000 m of water, if it were all mixed at once. It isn't: the deep water is replaced only as fast as it forms and returns. Hartmann p. 225: the time to replace the deep ocean is "on the order of 1000 years", the turnover time. Stewart p. 222: 1,000 years from the North Atlantic to the North Pacific means a mean flow of about 1 mm/s.
- This is the slow brake on warming from the O3 notes, and why the Southern Ocean warms more slowly than the rest of the globe: water rising there hasn't been at the surface for centuries (Hartmann p. 227).
  TEXT: How slow? About 1,000 years for the round trip
  TEXT: 140 years if all 4 km were stirred at once. But mixing down is slow: the deep water is only replaced as fast as it sinks and comes back.

## Frame 15: The oldest water  [fr_66]
NOTES: Hold: where is the oldest water? Not at the bottom near Antarctica, but at middle depths in the North Pacific, the farthest from both places where deep water forms (Hartmann p. 225). The oldest water isn't at the bottom, so deep water must slowly rise elsewhere, as the mass budget requires (same page). It also matches the oxygen: the deep North Pacific has 10-15% oxygen saturation where the North Atlantic has ~85% (Hartmann p. 222).

## Frame 22: Hysteresis  [fr_94]
NOTES: Hold: walk the path. 1: freshen slowly along the top; the flow weakens a little. 2: past the edge there's no "on" state left, and it drops. 3: take the fresh water away again: it stays off. 4: only when the north is saltier than where it started does it come back on. Stewart p. 218 (after Rahmstorf 1995): "Surface waters must become saltier than average for the first state to return." That is hysteresis: where you are depends on how you got there.
  TEXT: Physicist’s hat · mass budget
  TEXT: Two stable states
  TEXT: For the same fresh water, the overturning can be on or off.
  TEXT: Past the edge it drops. Undo the fresh water and it stays off: hysteresis.

## Frame 23: Has it happened?  [fr_97]
NOTES: Hold: how sensitive, and has it happened? Stewart p. 215: in Rahmstorf's (1995) model, ±0.1 Sv of extra fresh water into the North Atlantic switched a 14 Sv circulation on or off. The ice-core record shows abrupt changes many times in the last ice age: ~11,500 years ago Greenland warmed by ~8 °C in 40 years (Stewart p. 216, after Alley 2000). Heinrich events: surges of icebergs whose melt water is thought to have shut down deep-water formation, chilling the North Atlantic (Stewart pp. 216-218, Fig 13.2). The causes are still argued over (Stewart p. 218).
