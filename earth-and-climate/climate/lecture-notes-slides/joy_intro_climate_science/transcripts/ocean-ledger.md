## Frame 9: Jupiter has its own heat  [fr_34]
NOTES: STOP 4 · planets out of balance (Snappy between the three).
- Jupiter emits about twice what it absorbs (Argha: ~7 W/m² absorbed, ~14 emitted). Its interior is still cooling from its formation, 4.5 billion years ago.
- Gas giants are massive enough to keep primordial heat for billions of years. So "out of balance" is possible, if the planet is cooling.
  FIGURES: t_c8681bc5e0a1.svg

## Frame 10: A lava world  [fr_36]
NOTES: - Rocky planets are small: their heat of formation leaks out early (a magma-ocean phase lasts millions of years, not billions). To stay out of balance they need a source that keeps going.
- 55 Cancri e: S ≈ 2,400 (S = starlight received relative to Earth's; a = 0.0154 AU, L = 0.58 L☉, check). Starlight alone can melt rock; its surface is likely a magma ocean.
- Tidal friction: if its orbit is even slightly eccentric, tides can heat it from inside (as on Io). The measured eccentricity is small (e ≈ 0.05 ± 0.03, consistent with zero; e < 0.015 from the eclipse depth, Bolmont et al. 2013), so how much tides add is debated (e.g. arXiv 2601.23235, 2601.07080). If they matter, it emits more than it absorbs, like Jupiter.
- So a rocky planet out of balance is more likely a lava world than an ocean world.
  FIGURES: t_6d8b686e9161.svg

## Frame 16: The 0-D trap  [fr_54]
NOTES: STOP 7 · the 0-D trap (≈3 min, Smooth).
- Habitable-zone planets around small, cool stars (M dwarfs: Proxima b, TRAPPIST-1 e, f, g; bottom rows of the diagram) are probably tidally locked: one side always lit.
- The global mean can look fine while the day-night contrast is huge; then transport decides the climate. Kite et al. (2011) give ways such a planet in the zone can become uninhabitable.
- With a dynamic ocean there is still no snowball jump (Checlair et al. 2019; callback to Argha's snowball).
- Planet values (check): Proxima b S = 0.65, T★ 3050 K (Anglada-Escudé et al. 2016); TRAPPIST-1 e, f, g S = 0.646, 0.373, 0.252, T★ 2566 K (Agol et al. 2021).

## Frame 20: Runaway  [fr_71]
NOTES: Hold: the map recolours to the warming 20 years of imbalance WOULD give 10 m of water (ρc = 1025 × 3990 J m⁻³ K⁻¹).
- Equator: +1,100 K (✓), hot enough to melt rock. Solid line: where the water would pass 1,400 K (basalt melts at ~1,400 K).
- Poles: the deficit takes all the heat out of 10 m of water in ~3 years (✓). Dashed line: poleward of it, the water would lose all its heat within the 20 years. Nothing can cool below the ~3 K of the cosmic microwave background (2.7 K), so "toward 3 K" is the limit. (In reality its emission would drop as it cooled; this is accounting, not a forecast.)
- Framing: this is ACCOUNTING. The observed imbalance already includes every feedback at today's temperatures, and it persisted while temperatures stayed put. Don't ask "what would the temperature be without transport?" (that's the Planck counterfactual, in the lab: +17 K at the equator, −50 K at the poles in emission temperature).

## Frame 27: Does it matter that Earth is round?  [fr_99]
NOTES: Then momentum (Smooth pan).
- Moving air keeps its angular momentum about Earth's axis, M = (Ωr + u) r, with r the distance to the axis.
- On a cylinder spinning about its axis, moving "poleward" (along the axis) keeps r the same: nothing changes.
- On a sphere, r = a cos φ shrinks as you go poleward. Keeping M, air that starts at rest at the equator reaches u = Ωa sin²φ / cos φ: ~134 m/s eastward at 30° (Suhas; Hartmann §6.2). The profound consequence: one loop can't reach the pole.
  TEXT: Does it matter that Earth is round?
  TEXT: Net radiation
  TEXT: Carried poleward
  TEXT: Moving air keeps its angular momentum: its spin about Earth’s axis, which grows with its distance r from the axis. On a cylinder, going poleward keeps r the same: nothing changes.
