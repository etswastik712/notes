## Frame 2: The salt ledger  [fr_6]
NOTES: Beat 1 (≈3 min here, Smooth). The accountant's hat.
- Salinity: grams of dissolved salt in a kilogram of seawater. The ocean's mean is about 34.7 (Hartmann p. 196; Stewart p. 80; WOCE ✓ 34.72). Hartmann writes ‰ and Stewart uses the practical salinity scale with no unit; "g/kg" is close enough for us.
- Salt stays: evaporation and freezing take water and leave the salt; rain, rivers and melting ice add water (Stewart p. 78). Over the timescales here no salt is added or removed (rivers bring dissolved salt in only over millions of years).
- The accountant's sum (ours): a 50 m mixed layer at S = 35 that loses 1 m of fresh water a year (E − P ≈ 2.7 mm/day, like the subtropics) gets saltier by 35 × 1/50 = 0.7 g/kg a year. It doesn't keep rising, so currents and mixing must carry salt away as fast as evaporation concentrates it. That's the ledger balancing.

## Frame 9: A freshwater lid  [fr_57]
NOTES: (≈2 min, Smooth.) The physicist's hat. WOCE annual-mean profiles, box means over 10-20°N. The Bay's surface is ~3 g/kg fresher and so lighter: σ 20.7 against 23.4 at the surface (σ = density − 1000 kg/m³, defined here; ✓). Fresh water floats on salty water, and light water on heavy is hard to stir (O3: the stratification that stops the wind's stirring). So the Bay's surface layer stays thin and warm.
- Aside if asked (⚠ not in a course reading): this lid is one reason the Bay's cyclones find warm water all the way down to the bottom of the lid.

## Frame 11: Warm water  [fr_69]
NOTES: Hold: warm water (25 °C, S = 35, TEOS-10 ✓). ρα = 0.30 kg/m³ per K; ρβ = 0.74 per g/kg. So +1 kg/m³ is cooling by 3.3 K or adding 1.4 g/kg: one g/kg is worth ~2.4 K. Ocean temperatures span 30 K and surface salinities only a few g/kg, so in warm water temperature does most of the work.

## Frame 13: No 4 degree maximum  [fr_75]
NOTES: Hold: fresh water is densest at 4 °C, which is why lakes freeze from the top and keep 4 °C water at the bottom. Seawater saltier than 24.7 has no such maximum: it keeps getting denser all the way down to freezing (Hartmann p. 197), at -1.92 °C for S = 35 (TEOS-10 ✓). So the coldest seawater is also the densest.

## Frame 14: Sea ice leaves its salt behind  [fr_78]
NOTES: (≈2 min, Smooth.) Brine rejection. When sea ice forms, salt is pushed out of the ice crystals as brine, which makes the water under the ice saltier and denser. Around Antarctica, especially the Weddell Sea, cold offshore winds push the ice away and new ice keeps forming in the open water (polynyas): the source of Antarctic Bottom Water (Hartmann pp. 224-225).
  TEXT: Physicist’s hat · salt budget
  TEXT: Ice leaves the salt
  TEXT: Sea ice is almost fresh. Its salt stays in the water below as brine.
  TEXT: Cold and salty: the densest water in the ocean, made around Antarctica.

## Frame 15: Why sinking is rare  [fr_84]
NOTES: Beat 5 (≈3 min, Smooth). Hartmann p. 225: 75% of the ocean has potential density above 27.4, but only 4% of the surface water is that dense. Our check from WOCE (annual means, TEOS-10): 78% of the volume; 1.4% of the surface in the annual mean. Hartmann's 4% includes winter, when the dense patches are biggest.
- So deep water forms in only a few places, at a few times. Everywhere else the ocean is light water on heavy: the stratification that stopped the wind's stirring in O3.

## Frame 17: Fingerprints  [fr_96]
NOTES: Beat 6 (≈4 min, Smooth; if short of time, merge into the section). Below the mixed layer, temperature and salinity change only by slow mixing, so water keeps the T and S it had when it last touched the surface (Stewart §13.4, p. 223; Hartmann p. 221). A T-S diagram tells you where water came from.
- One WOCE profile, 30°S 25°W (1° box). The kinks: a salinity minimum at ~900 m, a deep salinity maximum at ~3,000 m, and the coldest water at the bottom (4,750 m). Three sources mixing (Stewart Fig 13.9 is the same idea for the South Atlantic). Don't name them yet: the section does that.
