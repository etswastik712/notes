This handout goes with the same app as part 1, "Daisyworld with an Ocean", and uses its presets 4 to 7. Part 1
built a bare planet: land, a mixed layer and a deep ocean, pushed by random weather. Here, daisies grow on the
land and grazers eat them. Open the app next to this handout.
        Part 1 explains what life adds to the model, draws the feedbacks as a causal loop diagram, and says what
        every control does.
        Part 2 walks through six runs. For each one it says what to set, asks you to predict what will happen, and
        then explains what you see. Try to answer each "Before you look" question before you read on.
        Part 3 sums up what to take away, and says what the model leaves out.
        Take it further is a spreadsheet exercise for after the tutorial.
You need no maths for this handout.

   The point in two sentences. On their own, daisies steady the land's temperature: they form loops that push
   back against any change. Add grazers and the ocean's slow memory, and the push-back comes too late,
   so the planet overshoots into a boom–bust cycle; add weather, and the ocean's memory sets the size of
   each bust, so a planet driven by nothing slow swings over decades and centuries.


Part 1. Life, its feedbacks, and the controls
1.1 What life adds, in words
Everything from part 1 is still here: land, a mixed layer 75 m deep over 70% of the planet, a deep ocean, the sun
and the weather. Life adds three things, all on the land.
        White daisies. They reflect more sunlight than bare ground, so a patch of white daisies is cooler than the
        land around it, by about 3–4 K.
        Black daisies. They absorb more sunlight than bare ground, so their patches are warmer than the land
        around them.
        Both colours grow best when their own patch is at 22.5 °C, more slowly either side, and not at all below 5
        °C or above 40 °C. They can only spread onto bare ground, and they die at a steady rate. So white daisies
        do best when the land is warm, and black daisies when it is cool.
        Grazers. They eat daisies of both colours, but prefer black ones. They multiply when food is plentiful and
        starve when it runs out. They eat fastest when the land is at 25 °C and more slowly when it is colder or
        warmer. The land usually sits near 19–22 °C, below their best, so in this model warmer land means
        hungrier grazers.
Life touches the climate in only one way: who covers the land sets how much sunlight the land reflects. The
climate touches life in only one way: temperature sets how fast each daisy grows and how fast the grazers eat.
Two differences from part 1. The life presets run with Day and seasons unticked: the sun is steady, and the
weather is a fresh random push every quarter-day that doesn't last. They were tuned that way, so leave the box
unticked for presets 4 to 7. To compare a life run with a bare planet fairly, load preset 3 and untick Day and
seasons.

 The causal loop diagram for presets 4 to 7, drawn for this handout. The "How it works" tab shows the same loop as a cycle
                                  of six stages; this diagram shows the links that make it.

How to read it.
        An arrow from A to B means "a change in A changes B".
        + at the arrowhead means they move the same way: more A, more B; less A, less B.
        − means they move opposite ways: more A, less B.
        Follow arrows round until you come back to where you started, and you have a loop. Count its − signs. An
        odd number makes a balancing loop (B): it pushes back against a change. An even number, or none,
        makes a reinforcing loop (R): it makes a change grow.
        ‖ marks a delay: B responds to A only after a while.
        Dashed boxes are outside drivers. They push on the planet, but nothing on the planet pushes back on
        them.
The signs hold near the land's usual temperatures, about 18–22 °C. Far from there, some can flip: on very hot
land, for example, even white daisies grow worse as it warms.
The links, one loop at a time.
        B1, the white-daisy thermostat. Warmer land → white patches move toward their best temperature, so
        white daisies spread (+) → more sunlight is reflected, and the land cools (−). One − sign: balancing.
        B2, the black-daisy thermostat. Warmer land → black patches, already warmer than the land, get too hot,
        so black daisies shrink (−) → less sunlight is absorbed, and the land cools (+ from black daisies to
        temperature: fewer black daisies, cooler land). One − sign: balancing. Turn it round and it says what part 2
        will show: cold land favours black daisies.
        B3 and B4, food. More daisies → more grazers (+, food). More grazers → fewer daisies (−, eating). One −
        each: balancing. But it is slow: grazers take months to multiply, and months to starve. The thick line says
        that grazers prefer black daisies.

        Appetite. Warmer land → hungrier grazers (+). This is how the climate reaches the grazers.
        Land and ocean. Heat flows between the land and the ocean, both ways (+ and +). The land holds little
        heat and the ocean a lot, so the ocean changes slowly and the land is dragged toward the ocean's
        temperature, years late (‖). This pair is not a runaway loop: heat always flows from warm to cold. Read it
        as "the land is tied to a slow ocean".
        Outside drivers. The sun warms the land (and the ocean, not drawn). The weather pushes on the land
        and the ocean together, at the same moment.
Try it yourself. Find the path Land temperature → Grazers → White daisies → Land temperature. Count its signs.
Is it balancing or reinforcing? When would it matter most?
It has two − signs, so it is reinforcing. Hungrier grazers strip white daisies too, the land darkens, warms, and the
grazers eat faster still. During a boom, this is what makes the boom run away until the food is gone.
Why balancing loops can make cycles. A balancing loop pushes back, so you might expect it to hold the planet
steady, and with no delay it does (Run 1 and Run 3). With a long delay it overshoots. Think of a shower with a
long pipe: you turn it hotter, nothing happens, so you turn it more, and by the time the hot water arrives it is
scalding. You turn it back, too far, and so on. The ocean is the long pipe. Grazers strip the land, the planet
brightens, but the land cools only as fast as the ocean lets it, and by the time it is cold the daisies have already
changed again. This is Run 2.

From a sun of 0.95 to 1.10, bare land warms by 17 K; land with daisies stays between 19 and 25 °C.
Why. The two thermostats. When the land warms, white daisies gain and black lose, both of which cool it; when
it cools, the reverse. From year to year the daisies make little difference, but a drift that lasts decades gives them
time to answer, so they take the slow swings out. Under a dim sun the land is covered in black daisies, and
under a bright one in white. Past 1.10, white daisies already cover as much land as they can, so the thermostat
has nothing left to give, and the land warms again.

Run 2. Add grazers, but no weather
Set: preset 6, Compare: grazers, no weather. Set Show to 50 years.
Before you look: nothing outside the planet changes: the sun is steady and there is no weather. All the loops in
the diagram are balancing. What will the grazers do over 50 years?




What to look for. A cycle that repeats for ever, one big boom about every 8 years (the turns alternate a little,
about 7 and 9 years). The grazers swing 24 times, from about 0.016 to 0.39, and the land between 18 °C and 21
°C. Every bust is the same size.
Follow one turn with the planet box, counting years after a grazer peak:
     1. Boom (0 years). Grazers peak and strip the land, black daisies first. Bare ground is darker than white
        daisies, so the planet is at its warmest about 0.7 years later.
     2. Bust (0.7 years). The food is gone; the grazers starve.

     3. White bloom (1.1 years). Free of grazers, white daisies cover up to 64% of the land. The planet is at its
        brightest.
     4. Slow cooling. The land is coldest at 3.6 years, and the mixed layer at 4.5 years: 2.5 to 3.5 years after the
        brightest moment. This delay is the ocean's memory.
     5. Cold suits black daisies, not grazers. Black daisies peak at 5.6 years, at up to 15% cover. Grazers eat
        less in the cold.
     6. Rewarming and the next boom (about 8 years). Black daisies darken the land, the planet warms, and the
        grazers have warmth and their favourite food: boom.
Why. No step needs anything from outside. Each balancing loop answers the last change, but through the
ocean, years late, so each answer overshoots. Put your finger on the diagram and follow the turn: grazers →
black daisies (B4), food runs out, white daisies take the land (B3), the land cools through the ocean's delay (‖),
black daisies spread (B2), the land warms, and the grazers' appetite returns.

Run 3. Take the ocean away
Set: stay on preset 6, tick Keep this run, then move Ocean fraction to 0%.
Before you look: the same daisies and grazers, the same steady sun, no weather, and now no ocean. Will the
cycle be faster, slower, or something else?




What to look for. No cycle at all. After the hidden years, everything sits still: grazers at 0.19, white daisies at
29%, black at 26%, land at 18.3 °C.
Now load preset 5, Compare: grazers, all land: the same all-land planet, with weather. The grazers boom and
bust about every 2 years, and the busts are small: in a typical one the grazers fall less than 3 times.
Why. Without the ocean, there is no long delay. The land answers within weeks, so the loops push back in time
and the planet settles. A kick from the weather sets it ringing for a while, like a bell, but it dies down, so the
busts stay small and fast. The ocean's delay is what keeps the cycle going. For a check, try 30% ocean on
preset 6: a cycle comes back, about every 3 years.

Run 4. Add weather: busts of every size
Set: preset 4, Add life. Show 300 years.
Before you look: the weather is random and forgets within a quarter-day. In Run 2 every bust was the same. Will
weather make the busts all a bit bigger, all a bit smaller, or different from each other? What will it do to the
century bar?




What to look for. Look at the grazer chart in the screenshot of section 1.4: the circles have every size. In a
typical bust the grazers fall 8–17 times; in the smallest tenth only about 3 times; in the largest tenth 200 to 330
times. Big busts come every few decades.
Now the bars. The century bar is 0.11–0.28 K: two to four times the bare planet's with the same weather number
(0.05–0.08 K), and roughly ten times that of the daisies without grazers in Run 1. The decade bar sits far above
the dashed line.
Find three busts of different sizes. Tap each circle and read the line under the charts to see how far the grazers
fell. Then move along the temperature chart to the three years just before each bust, and read the mixed layer in
the planet box. What do you notice?
Why. The weather's pushes are fast and small, but the ocean adds them up, as in part 1. So some years the
ocean goes into a bust colder than usual. A colder ocean keeps the land cold for longer after the white bloom:
the grazers starve for longer, and the bust goes deeper. Across many busts, the colder the ocean in the three
years before, the deeper the bust (a correlation of about −0.8; −1 would be a perfect match). The loop sets
when busts come; the ocean's memory of the weather sets how big each one is. Daisies alone took slow swings
out (Run 1); daisies, grazers and an ocean together put far more back in.

What to look for. Two kinds of cycle. When the grazers eat well at the land's usual temperature (best
temperature 22 °C, or a wide tolerance), they cycle quickly with their food, about once a year, and the land's
temperature hardly moves: the grazers and daisies are cycling without the climate. When the cold holds them
back hard (best temperature 28 °C, or a narrow tolerance), the slow, ocean-paced cycle returns, and each bust
nearly wipes them out. At 30 °C they never recover, and the planet is Run 1's daisies-only planet. The taste
control changes which colour the grazers clear away: with no preference the land stays mostly bare, and if they
eat only white daisies, black ones take over and the land is hot.
Why. In the diagram, these controls set the strength of the appetite link and the thickness of the B3 and B4 lines.
The slow cycle needs the climate in the loop. Take the climate out, and the food loops alone give the fast cycle
you saw on all land in Run 3. Make the climate's grip too strong, and the grazers can't come back.

Part 3. What to take away
     1. Daisies alone are a thermostat. Two balancing loops, through the colour of the land, hold its temperature
        near the daisies' favourite over a wide range of sunlight, and they take out the slow swings.
     2. A delay turns a balancing loop into a cycle. The ocean's slow response makes the grazer–daisy–climate
        loop overshoot, so it cycles for ever with nothing outside cycling. Take the ocean away and it settles.
     3. The ocean sets the beat. A deeper mixed layer gives a longer cycle.
     4. The ocean's memory sets the size of each bust. Weather is fast and small; the ocean adds it up, and a
        cold ocean going into a bust makes it deep. So busts come in every size, and the planet swings over
        decades and centuries.
     5. The cycle needs the climate in the loop. Grazers that don't care about temperature cycle fast with their
        food alone; grazers held back too hard die out.
What the model leaves out. Places: there is one land and one ocean, so no daisy can move somewhere cooler.
Evolution: daisies and grazers never adapt. Earth's living things do change its climate (forests darken snowy
land, plants move water into the air), but these links are much weaker than Daisyworld's, which is built to make
the link strong. And no day, seasons or lasting weather in the life presets.
Questions to take to the discussion.
        Find a balancing loop in the real climate system. Does it have a long delay anywhere? Could it overshoot?
        In Run 1, daisies made the slow swings smaller. In Run 4, daisies and grazers made them larger. Which
        parts of the diagram make the difference?
        Before the biggest bust you found, was the ocean warmer or colder than usual? Could you have forecast
        that bust a few years ahead? What would you need to know?

        columns from here on.
               Python: run = pd.read_csv(<the _run.csv file>) , df = pd.read_csv(<the _busts.csv
               file>) , then for each of the four columns df[c] = df[c] - run[c].mean() .
     3. The average bust. Make a pivot table with whole_years_from_bust as rows, and the average of the four
        anomaly columns, grazers and grazer_appetite_per_day as values. Chart it.
               Python: df.groupby("whole_years_from_bust").mean(numeric_only=True) .
     4. Big against small. Filter bust_drop_factor above 30 (big busts), then below 5 (small busts), and repeat
        the pivot each time.
     5. What predicts the size? For each bust_id , average the mixed-layer anomaly over
         whole_years_from_bust −3 to −1. Scatter-plot it against bust_drop_factor , with a log scale on that axis.
