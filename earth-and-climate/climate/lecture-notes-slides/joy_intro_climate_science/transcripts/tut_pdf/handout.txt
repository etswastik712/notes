   The point in two sentences. The Earth takes in more sunlight than it gives back as infrared radiation in the
   tropics, and gives back more than it takes in near the poles, so the atmosphere and ocean must carry
   energy poleward. Overall the planet is gaining a little energy every year, and most of that ends up in the
   ocean.


1. What CERES is
CERES stands for Clouds and the Earth's Radiant Energy System. It is a set of identical instruments that NASA
has flown on several satellites: Terra, Aqua and, more recently, NOAA-20. Each instrument is a broadband
scanning radiometer. It sweeps across the Earth below and measures how much radiation comes up from each
spot. On Terra and Aqua it has three channels: a shortwave channel for reflected sunlight, a total channel for all
wavelengths, and a narrow infrared "window" channel. The longwave, the infrared emitted by the Earth, is the
total minus the shortwave. The newer instrument on NOAA-20 measures the longwave in a channel of its own.
"Broadband" is the important word. Most satellite instruments measure a few narrow colours, which is good for
spotting clouds or measuring temperature. CERES instead catches all the energy, at every wavelength, because
energy is what the budget is about.
Why do we need satellites at all? The top of the atmosphere is the only place where the Earth's energy budget is
simple: energy can cross it only as radiation. Sunlight comes in, and reflected sunlight and infrared go out. No
ground station can see that boundary. A satellite looks down on it from above, and a few satellites together see
every part of the globe at many times of day.
The app uses monthly mean maps. Each map gives, for every box of the latitude–longitude grid, the average
flux over one calendar month, counting day and night. The source data are on a 1° grid. The app averages them
onto a 2° grid (a grid of 90 × 180 cells), weighting each 1° box by its area, to keep the download small. The
record runs from January 2010 to January 2025, which is 181 months.

2. What EBAF is, and its honest caveats
The product in the app is CERES EBAF, which stands for Energy Balanced and Filled.
"Filled" refers to the clear-sky maps. To know what clouds do, we need the fluxes for clear skies (see below),
but in many places and months CERES never sees a cloud-free scene, and a clear-sky map built from CERES
alone has holes. EBAF fills them, using the clear parts of scenes seen by a finer-resolution imager (MODIS) and

radiative-transfer calculations. Separately, the CERES satellites pass over each place only a few times a day, and
the hours in between are filled in using geostationary weather satellites.
"Energy Balanced" needs more care. Each CERES instrument is very stable, so it detects changes very well, but
its absolute calibration is uncertain by a few W m⁻². That is more than the planet's whole imbalance. So the EBAF
team adjusts the shortwave and the longwave fluxes, each within its own uncertainty, so that the long-term
global mean of the net flux matches the rate at which the ocean is measured to be gaining heat. In practice this
means:
        the size of the global-mean imbalance is anchored to ocean measurements, not measured by CERES
        alone;
        the patterns (where the Earth gains and loses), and the changes from month to month and year to year,
        come from CERES itself.
Surface fluxes are computed, not measured. No satellite can measure the radiation at the ground directly,
because the atmosphere is in the way. The surface fields in EBAF come from a radiative-transfer model: a
program that calculates how radiation passes through the atmosphere. The model is fed clouds and aerosols
seen by satellite instruments, and air temperature and humidity from a weather-forecast reanalysis. Its results
are then nudged so that, at the top of the atmosphere, they agree with what CERES measured. The surface
numbers are therefore less certain than the top-of-atmosphere numbers, and anything computed from them (the
"column" in view 3) inherits that uncertainty.
Clear-sky fluxes and the cloud radiative effect. To ask what clouds do, we need to know what the fluxes would
be without the clouds. EBAF provides clear-sky fluxes. The app uses EBAF's clear-sky for the total region (the
variables called clr_t ). For each grid box and month, this is the flux that the whole box would have if its clouds
were removed. Over the clear parts of a box it comes from measurements. Over the cloudy parts it comes from
the radiative-transfer model with the clouds taken out. This is the same definition that climate models use, so the
two can be compared.
The cloud radiative effect (CRE), called "Cloud effect" in the app, is then

   cloud effect = (net flux, all skies) − (net flux, clear skies)


computed separately for the shortwave, the longwave and their sum. With the app's sign convention, a negative
cloud effect means the clouds make the Earth (or the surface) lose energy compared with clear skies; a positive
one means they make it gain energy.

3. Why it's used
        It is the benchmark for climate models. A climate model has to get the top-of-atmosphere budget right,
        both the global numbers and the maps. CERES is what modellers compare against.
        Clouds. How clouds will change as the planet warms is the largest source of uncertainty in how much
        warming a given amount of CO₂ will cause. Clouds both reflect sunlight and trap infrared. CERES
        measures both effects, everywhere, every month.
        Monitoring Earth's energy imbalance. CERES shows how the imbalance changes from year to year, and
        whether it is growing.
        The link to the ocean. Most of the energy the Earth gains ends up in the ocean, because water stores far
        more heat than air or land. The ocean is where the imbalance shows up as warming. EBAF's anchoring to
        ocean heat measurements is this same link, run in the other direction.

The app has five views, chosen with the numbered tabs at the top. Each view opens with a question in bold at
the top. The question is there for you to think about; the app never answers it. The settings are your own: change
any of them and look again.
Sign convention. Everywhere in the app, positive means energy going into the thing named: into the Earth at
the top of the atmosphere (TOA), into the surface at the surface, and into the atmosphere for the column.
Outgoing longwave (OLR) and incoming or absorbed sunlight are plain amounts, always positive; the field's note
under the field menu says which is a gain and which a loss.
Mean and anomaly. A mean is the average of a field over the months you choose. An anomaly is the difference
from the 2010–2024 average of the same calendar months. A January anomaly is measured against the average
January, so the seasonal cycle drops out and what is left is how unusual that month was.
Seasons. DJF is December–January–February, MAM is March–May, JJA is June–August, SON is September–
November. "DJF 2015–16" means December 2015 plus January and February 2016. In view 4, the Jul–Jun year
runs from July of one year to June of the next, which matches the way El Niño events grow and decay. In views
1–3 the period is always the 2010–2024 average: either the Annual mean or one average month (for example,
the average of all fifteen Julys).
On every map you can hover (or tap, on a phone) to read the value, latitude and longitude of a cell. The colour
bar gives the units. Diverging colour scales (blue–white–red) are centred on zero. For net radiation, the cloud
effect and the column, blue means energy lost and red energy gained. On anomaly and A − B maps, red means
higher than the average (or than B) and blue lower; for outgoing longwave, higher means more energy leaving,
so red there is a loss. Sequential scales (dark purple to yellow) are used for plain amounts such as OLR. Grey
cells have no value (for example, albedo during the polar night).
Every time series is shaded red during El Niño spells and blue during La Niña spells, from the Niño-3.4 index
(section 6): a spell is at least 5 months with the 3-month mean index at or beyond ±0.5 °C.

Exercise 1. The pattern of the net budget at the top of the atmosphere
Before you look: where on Earth do you expect a net gain of energy by radiation, and where a net loss? Is the
boundary between them at the same latitude in both hemispheres? Would a hot desert gain or lose?
In the app: view 1, Top of atmosphere, with its defaults: Field Net radiation (TOA), Period Annual mean. Hover
over the Sahara (23°N, 11°E) and over the western Pacific warm pool (1°N, 151°E). Then switch the Field to
Absorbed sunlight and to Outgoing longwave, and compare.




 Annual-mean net radiation, 2010–2024, with the zonal means of absorbed sunlight and outgoing longwave. The readout is
                                                    for the Sahara.

What to look for
      A red band of gain across the tropics and blue loss toward both poles. The largest zonal-mean gain is
      +72 W m⁻², at 1°S. The zonal mean near the poles is −126 W m⁻² at 89°N and −91 W m⁻² at 89°S.
      In the zonal-mean panel, absorbed sunlight (ASR) and outgoing longwave (OLR) cross near 37°S and
      36°N. Equatorward of the crossings the Earth gains; poleward of them it loses.
      ASR changes enormously with latitude: about 322 W m⁻² at the equator against 68 W m⁻² at 89°N. OLR
      changes much less: about 251 W m⁻² at the equator against 195 W m⁻² at 89°N.
      The Sahara loses energy (−28 W m⁻²), even though it is hot and sunny. It absorbs 255 W m⁻² of sunlight
      but emits 283 W m⁻². Its bright sand gives it an albedo of 0.34. The warm pool, under heavy cloud, gains
      +85 W m⁻² (ASR 315, OLR 230 W m⁻², albedo 0.24).

Why
Sunlight arrives almost head-on in the tropics and at a glancing angle near the poles, so the tropics absorb far
more of it. Outgoing longwave depends on temperature, and the tropics are only somewhat warmer than the
poles, so it varies much less with latitude. The two curves must cross somewhere, and in our climate they cross
near 37°S and 36°N.
A region that gains energy every year should keep warming, and one that loses should keep cooling. They don't,
so the atmosphere and the ocean must carry energy from the tropics toward the poles. Adding up the gains
equatorward of each latitude, the TOA map implies that the atmosphere and ocean together carry about 5.8 PW
(petawatts, 10¹⁵ W) northward across 36°N, and about 5.7 PW southward across 36°S. Those are the latitudes
where the gains stop and the losses begin, so that is where the poleward flow is largest. Weather systems in the
atmosphere and currents in the ocean do this carrying.
The Sahara shows that the budget is not just about temperature. Its sand is bright, so it reflects much of the
sunlight. Its air is dry and almost cloudless, so its hot surface radiates freely to space. Its atmosphere is kept
warm by air that sinks over it and is warmed by compression. That air has brought energy from the rainy tropics.

Exercise 2. Seasons
Before you look: the Earth's tilt moves the Sun north and south through the year. Where will the band of energy
gain be in January and in July? In midsummer the Sun never sets at the pole. Could the summer pole take in
more energy than it gives off?
In the app: view 1, Field Net radiation (TOA). Click Play the months and watch, then pause. Set Period to
January, then to July. In each case, find the latitudes where the zonal-mean absorbed sunlight (purple) is above
the outgoing longwave (orange). Then set Field to Incoming sunlight for June and compare the North Pole with
the equator.

What to look for
      In January the zonal mean shows a gain from 67°S to 13°N. In July it runs from 7°S all the way to 81°N.
      The band of gain follows the Sun into the summer hemisphere and spreads far toward the summer pole.
      The winter pole loses heavily: −171 W m⁻² at 89°N in January and −113 W m⁻² at 89°S in July.
      Near the summer pole the Earth is close to balance. At 89°N the net is −23 W m⁻² in June and −11 W m⁻²
      in July. At 89°S it is −21 W m⁻² in December.
      Incoming sunlight in June is 517 W m⁻² at 89°N, but only 386 W m⁻² at the equator. In December it is 549
      W m⁻² at 89°S against 412 W m⁻² at the equator.
Why
The incoming sunlight on these maps is a 24-hour average. At the summer solstice the polar Sun is low, but it
shines all day and all night. Added up over the day, the pole receives more sunlight than the equator, where it is
dark half the time.

The pole does not absorb most of that sunlight, though. Snow, sea ice and summer clouds make it bright: in
June the albedo at 89°N is 0.61, so it absorbs 201 W m⁻². That is close to what the pole emits, and the result is a
small loss at the pole itself and a gain over most of the summer high latitudes, up to 81°N in July. Antarctica is
brighter still (an albedo of 0.70 at 89°S in December, absorbing 166 W m⁻²), so the southern summer gain stops
at 67°S, over the ocean around the ice sheet.
In winter the polar night gets no sunlight at all, but the air and surface keep radiating. The energy carried in from
lower latitudes keeps them from getting colder and colder. That is why the poleward carrying of energy from
exercise 1 is strongest in winter.

Exercise 3. The planetary imbalance
Before you look: if the Earth were in balance, the global-mean net radiation would be zero. Is it? How large is any
imbalance compared with the sunlight the Earth absorbs? Has it changed since 2010?
In the app: view 1 with its defaults. Read the Global mean in the control panel, then switch the Field to Absorbed
sunlight and Outgoing longwave and read their global means too. Look at the chart below the map and its
readout. For a closer look, use view 5 (Free explore): Field Net radiation (TOA), Time series Anomaly.




    Global-mean net radiation, month by month. The thick line is the 12-month running mean; the dashed line is the trend.

What to look for
      The global mean is +1.26 W m⁻²: absorbed sunlight 241.95 minus outgoing longwave 240.69 W m⁻². The
      difference is only 0.5% of the absorbed sunlight.
      The monthly values swing through the year, from about −9 W m⁻² in the average June to +9 W m⁻² in the
      average February, far more than the mean. The 12-month running mean stays positive throughout.
      The readout gives a trend of +0.61 W m⁻² per decade. The 2010–2014 average is +0.93 W m⁻²; the 2020–
      2024 average is +1.50 W m⁻².
      Among calendar years, 2010 had the smallest imbalance (+0.24 W m⁻²) and 2023 the largest (+2.06 W
      m⁻²).
Why
The Earth is gaining energy. Over the whole planet, +1.26 W m⁻² adds up to about 2.0 × 10²² J every year:
multiply by the Earth's surface area, 5.1 × 10¹⁴ m², and by the number of seconds in a year. Most of this energy
goes into the ocean, which is why the ocean heat content keeps rising. Remember the caveat from section 2: the
size of the mean imbalance is anchored to the ocean heat measurements, but the change since 2010 comes
from CERES itself.
The imbalance exists because greenhouse gases keep accumulating. Each increase makes the atmosphere a
little more opaque to infrared, so the Earth emits a little less than it absorbs until it warms enough to catch up.
The trend shows the gap has grown. Part of that comes from the Earth absorbing more sunlight, as low clouds,
snow and sea ice have decreased and some kinds of air pollution have been cleaned up.
The seasonal swing has nothing to do with this. The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, and the
hemispheres differ (the Southern Hemisphere is mostly ocean). The trend is therefore fitted allowing for a
different average for each calendar month, so the seasonal cycle cannot tilt it. In view 5 the same trend reads

Exercise 4. Clouds cool the planet
Before you look: clouds reflect sunlight back to space, and they also absorb infrared from below and emit less
of it to space from their cold tops. Which effect wins, globally? Where do you expect clouds to cool most

The clouds off Peru, Namibia and California are stratocumulus: low, flat sheets of cloud over cold ocean water,
under air that sinks from above. They are bright and cover most of the sky, so they reflect a great deal of
sunlight. Their tops are low and nearly as warm as the sea (279 K off Peru), so they hide very little infrared. The
shortwave wins and they cool strongly.
Over the warm pool, the clouds are deep convective towers and the anvils that spread from them. They also
reflect a lot of sunlight (a shortwave effect of −59 W m⁻², though less than the −84 W m⁻² off Peru), but their
tops are high and cold (239 K, about −34 °C), so they trap a lot of infrared. The two effects nearly cancel.
Over Greenland and Antarctica the surface is snow, already as bright as a cloud, so clouds add little reflection;
but they still trap infrared, so there they warm.
Across the whole globe, the cooling wins, and by a lot: the cloud effect is many times the planet's imbalance.
This is why a small change in clouds as the climate warms matters so much.

      covers 83% of the sky there, with tops at 239 K (−34 °C).

        Sahara: 283 W m⁻² with clouds, 293 W m⁻² without them. The difference is only +10 W m⁻², because
        cloud covers just 17% of the sky.
        With the clouds removed, the two differ by only about 13 W m⁻². With the clouds, the warm pool sends out

Why
A surface or a cloud emits about σT⁴, where σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant and T its temperature in kelvin.
A cloud top at 239 K emits about 186 W m⁻², far less than the warm sea below it. Where deep clouds cover most
of the sky, as over the warm pool, the planet "shows" space mostly its cold cloud tops, and the outgoing
longwave drops.
Even without clouds, the warm pool emits less than the Sahara, although its surface is just as warm. Its air is full
of water vapour, a greenhouse gas, which absorbs the surface's infrared and re-emits it from higher, colder
levels. The Sahara's air is dry, so more of its hot surface's infrared escapes directly.
This is why weather forecasters use low outgoing longwave to find tropical rain: low OLR in the tropics means
high, cold cloud tops, and those come from deep, raining convection. You will use this in exercise 7.

      Sahara's surface gains only +42 W m⁻².
Why
The atmosphere really does lose about 110 W m⁻² by radiation. With nothing else heating it, the whole
atmosphere would cool by about 0.9 °C every day. It doesn't, so something other than radiation must heat it, by
the same amount. The surface tells you where to look: it gains +111.6 W m⁻² by radiation, yet it doesn't keep
warming either. The surface hands its excess energy to the atmosphere, without radiation, in two ways.
     1. Latent heat. Water evaporates from the surface, mostly from the ocean. Evaporation takes energy: it is
        why sweat cools you. The water vapour carries that energy up into the atmosphere. When the vapour
        condenses into cloud droplets and falls as rain, the energy is released into the air.
     2. Sensible heat. Air touching a warmer surface is heated by it, and turbulence carries the warmed air
        upward.
Estimates of the global means, from many observations put together, are roughly 80 W m⁻² of latent heat and
roughly 20 W m⁻² of sensible heat. Trenberth and colleagues (2009) gave 80 and 17 W m⁻²; Stephens and
colleagues (2012) gave 88 and 24 W m⁻². Their sums, 97 and 112 W m⁻², bracket the column's radiative loss of
110 W m⁻². These estimates, and the computed surface fluxes in EBAF, are each uncertain by several W m⁻², so
this is as close an agreement as we can expect.
Rain is the latent heat you can see. Condensing enough water to give 1 mm of rain a day releases about 29 W
m⁻². So 80 W m⁻² of latent heating means that, averaged over the globe, about 2.8 mm of rain falls each day.
This is how the ocean hands energy to the atmosphere: sunlight warms the sea surface, the sea evaporates, and
the energy is released in the air, in clouds and rain.

But the map has a twist. If the rain balanced the radiation in each place, the rainiest places should show the
biggest radiative loss. Over the oceans they show the opposite. The warm pool loses only −81 W m⁻²: its high,
thick clouds trap infrared inside the column (the column's cloud effect there is +40 W m⁻²). The biggest losses
are over dry subtropical oceans like the one off Peru (−157 W m⁻²), where it hardly ever rains. (Dry land is
different. The Sahara's ground gets very hot and loses −130 W m⁻² as infrared, much of which the air above
absorbs, and the hot ground also heats the air directly as sensible heat. A cool ocean surface can do neither, so
the Sahara's column loses only −69 W m⁻².) So rain cannot balance the budget place by place. The atmosphere
also moves energy sideways. In the rain belts, condensation releases far more heat than the column loses by
radiation, and the air rises. That air spreads toward the subtropics and sinks there, warming by compression as it
sinks. The sinking warms the dry columns that lose the most. The water vapour that feeds the rain belts was
mostly evaporated over the warm subtropical oceans under the trade winds, and the trade winds carried it
toward the equator. (Off Peru the sea also gains a lot by radiation, +151 W m⁻², but much of that warms cold
water that wells up from below along the coast, rather than evaporating it: another place where the ocean stores
and moves the energy.) This circulation, rising in the deep tropics and sinking in the subtropics, is the Hadley
circulation. Its east–west cousin along the equatorial Pacific, the Walker circulation, is the one El Niño disturbs
(exercise 7).

Why
Deep convection, the tall rain clouds of exercise 5, sits over the warmest water. Normally that is the western
Pacific warm pool. During El Niño the warm water and the convection shift east to the central Pacific, and during
La Niña they are pushed further west than usual. So El Niño minus La Niña gives more high, cold cloud in the
central Pacific (much lower OLR) and less over the maritime continent (higher OLR). The Walker circulation
weakens and shifts with them.
The net radiation hardly changes, for the reason you found in exercise 4: deep convective clouds reflect about
as much sunlight as the infrared they trap. Moving the clouds east moves both effects together. El Niño
rearranges the clouds and the rain far more than it changes the energy gained at the top of the atmosphere.
Its effect on the energy budget is mainly at the surface and inside the ocean and atmosphere: the ocean
releases heat to the atmosphere in the central and eastern Pacific.
No two El Niños are alike. In 2023–24 the warming peaked further west, and the cloud changes followed it.

Why
During El Niño the ocean releases heat into the atmosphere over the central and eastern Pacific. The
atmosphere warms, and a warmer atmosphere emits more infrared to space: the global OLR rises, peaking a
couple of months after Niño-3.4 does. On its own, that would make the Earth lose energy during El Niño. But the
absorbed sunlight changes too, because the clouds rearrange (exercise 7), and in these years it rose with the
OLR. The two largely cancel. Only about half a year after the Niño-3.4 peak does the global net show a small
loss (the correlation of −0.23). La Niña does the reverse: the ocean stores heat and the planet emits a little less.

So the answer is: a little, and late. El Niño mainly moves energy between the ocean and the atmosphere, and
from place to place, rather than adding energy to the planet or removing it. Months of El Niño and La Niña make
the global-mean surface temperature jump about, because heat moves out of or into the ocean. But the long-
term imbalance of exercise 3 is set by the greenhouse gases and the planet's reflectivity, and El Niño only adds
wiggles to it. 2023 shows how much else matters. An El Niño year would be expected to lose energy, yet in 2023
the Earth gained more energy than in any other year of the record, mostly because it absorbed more sunlight.
