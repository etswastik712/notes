   The point in two sentences. The ocean does two things to a planet: it makes it darker, and it makes it
   slower. Neither one gives the planet control over its temperature: more ocean means the daisies have less
   to work with, and the planet takes longer to respond to any change.


Part 1. The model and the app
1.1 Daisyworld in one paragraph
Daisyworld is a thought experiment by James Lovelock and Andrew Watson (1983). An imaginary planet circles a
sun that slowly brightens, as our own sun has over billions of years. The only life is two kinds of daisy, black and
white. Black daisies absorb sunlight and warm their surroundings; white daisies reflect it and cool theirs. Neither
daisy "aims" to control anything. Each simply grows where it is comfortable. Yet together they hold the planet
near the temperature they like over a wide range of sun, while a planet without them heats steadily. The model
was built to show that life and climate can steer each other without anyone planning it. It is now a standard way
to teach feedbacks.

1.2 What this version adds: an ocean
This app gives the planet an ocean, and the ocean does two jobs.
     1. It takes away the daisies' room, and it is dark. Daisies can't grow on water, so they can only change the
        colour of the land. The ocean reflects little sunlight, less than anything on land, and its colour never
        changes. The more ocean, the less of the planet's colour the daisies control.
     2. It stores a lot of heat. Warming water takes far more energy than warming land and air. The more ocean,
        the more slowly the planet warms or cools when the sun changes.
Those two jobs give the two questions this handout explores: how much control can the daisies have, and how
fast does the planet respond?

1.3 What is in the model, in words
        One temperature for the whole planet. There are no places, no map and no seasons. Land and ocean
        share the same temperature.
        The sun. Its brightness is called L. L = 1 is the reference sun of the original model. The planet absorbs part
        of the sunlight and radiates heat to space. Its temperature settles where the two balance.
        The surface. It is made of four kinds of surface. Each has its own albedo, the fraction of sunlight it
        reflects:

         Surface                     Albedo                       Effect
         White daisies               0.75                         reflect most sunlight: cool the planet
         Bare ground                 0.50                         in between
         Black daisies               0.25                         absorb most sunlight: warm the planet
         Ocean                       0.10 (adjustable)            darker than anything on land, and fixed

        The daisies. They live only on land and spread into bare ground. They grow best at 22.5 °C and cannot
        grow below 5 °C or above 40 °C. A fixed share of them dies every year. They need years to spread or to
        die back.
        Patches. This is the trick that makes Daisyworld work. A black patch is warmer than the planet's average
        and a white patch is cooler, by up to about 10 °C. Under a dim sun, the black patches are the ones warm
        enough to grow in. Black daisies spread, the planet darkens and it warms. Under a bright sun, the white
        patches are the comfortable ones. White daisies spread, the planet brightens and it cools. That is the
        whole regulating mechanism.
        The heat store. This is how much heat the planet must take in to warm by one degree. Land and air store
        little, so an all-land planet responds in weeks. The ocean's top layer, the mixed layer (50 m deep by
        default), stores about 20 times more, so the planet responds in months. Deeper settings stand in for slow
        mixing into the deep ocean, and the response takes years.
        The bare planet. A twin planet runs alongside, with the same land, ocean and sun but no daisies. It is the
        control experiment. Any difference between the two is the daisies' doing.

Why it happens
The balance. With the sun steady, the planet settles where the sunlight it absorbs equals the heat it radiates:

   S · L · (1 − A) = σ T⁴


Here S = 917 W/m², A is the planet's albedo and σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. A higher albedo means a
cooler planet. The daisies' only lever on the climate is A.
The albedo of an all-land planet is the area-weighted average of its surfaces:

   A = a_w · 0.75 + a_b · 0.25 + x · 0.5


Here a_w and a_b are the fractions of land covered by white and black daisies, and x = 1 − a_w − a_b is bare
ground.
The patches. Each colour of patch has its own temperature:

   T_white = T + 20 · (A − 0.75)
   T_black = T + 20 · (A − 0.25)


The 20 K (called q′) sets how little heat the patches share with each other. On a planet of albedo 0.5, a black
patch sits 5 °C above the planet's temperature and a white patch 5 °C below it. Under a dim sun, the black
patches reach the growing range first. Black daisies spread, A falls and T rises, which helps more black daisies
grow. That is the jump at L ≈ 0.75. As the sun brightens, the white patches become the comfortable ones. White
daisies spread, A rises and the planet cools. The two colours pull in opposite directions and hold T where both
can grow.
The growth. Each kind of daisy grows into bare ground at a rate set by its own patch's temperature, and dies at a
fixed rate:

   da_i/dt = a_i · [ x · β(T_i) − γ ]
   β(T) = 1 − 0.003265 · (22.5 − T)²             (zero below 5 °C and above 40 °C)


Here γ = 0.3 per year. Daisies can never cover more than 70% of the land: when growth balances death, x · β =
γ, and β is at most 1, so bare ground x stays at 0.3 or more. At L ≈ 1.5 the white daisies are near that limit. The
dial has reached its end stop. A little more sun warms the white patches beyond what the daisies can stand.
Some die, the albedo falls, the planet warms, and more die. This runaway ends on the bare planet's line.

Why it happens
With a fraction f of the planet as land and 1 − f as ocean, the albedo becomes:

   A = f · (a_w · 0.75 + a_b · 0.25 + x · 0.5) + (1 − f) · 0.1

This one line is the whole story of leverage. On land, daisies can move the albedo anywhere between 0.25 (all
black) and 0.75 (all white), a swing of 0.5. On the whole planet that swing is 0.5 × f: 0.5 with all land, 0.35 with
30% ocean and only 0.15 with 70% ocean. The ocean's share is fixed, and it is dark. More ocean gives the
daisies a smaller dial, and it sets the planet warmer to begin with.

What to look for
        Before the change, black daisies cover 59% of the land and hold the daisy planet at 25.0 °C, while the
        bare planet sits at 8.7 °C.
        The bare planet warms by 7.0 °C. It gets two-thirds of the way in 22 days and is flat after a few months.
        The daisy planet does just what the bare planet does for the first two months: it jumps by 7 °C. Then, over
        years, the white daisies spread (from 8% to 22% of the land) and the black daisies thin out (from 59% to
        45%). The planet comes back, and settles after about 18 years at 1.4 °C cooler than before.
Why it happens
The energy budget, with storage. When the planet is out of balance, the difference goes into (or comes out of)
its heat store, C:

   C · dT/dt = S · L · (1 − A) − σ T⁴


Absorbed minus emitted equals the rate at which heat is stored. At the jump, the absorbed sunlight rises by 46
W/m² at once, but the emitted heat catches up only as the planet warms. The planet in this model has no
greenhouse: it radiates straight from its surface.


Daisyworld, land and ocean                                                                                             12 / 19
The heat store is shared between land and ocean:

   C = f · C_land + (1 − f) · ρc · h


Here C_land = 10⁷ J m⁻² K⁻¹ stands for land plus the air above it, about 2.5 m of water's worth. ρc = 4.1 × 10⁶ J
m⁻³ K⁻¹ is for sea water, and h is the mixed-layer depth. At 50 m, the ocean stores about 20 times as much heat
per square metre as land.
The response time. After a small sudden change, a planet goes about two-thirds of the way to its new
temperature in

   τ = C / λ,            λ = 4σT³ ≈ 5.4 W m⁻² K⁻¹


Here λ is how much more heat the planet emits for each degree it warms. For all land, τ ≈ 21 days, and the run
measures 22 days. In lecture, λ was the Planck feedback for Earth, 3.8 W m⁻² K⁻¹, which includes the
greenhouse effect. This model has no greenhouse, so λ is larger and τ is shorter: 50 m of water gives about 1.2
years here against 1.7 years in lecture.
Heat capacity doesn't change where the planet ends up. Set dT/dt = 0 and C drops out. It changes only how
fast the planet gets there.
The spike. The temperature responds in weeks and the daisies in years. So for the first months the daisies are
effectively frozen, and the daisy planet warms just like the bare one. Fast physics plus slow biology gives an
overshoot. Ending cooler under a brighter sun is not a bug. Run 1 showed it already: in the middle of its
regulated range, the daisy planet cools slightly as the sun brightens.

What to look for. The bare planet now warms by 7.3 °C, two-thirds of the way after about 4 months (127 days)
instead of 22 days. The daisy planet peaks later (after about a year) and lower (+6.5 °C). It then settles at −1.3

Why: an accountant's view. The sun jumps, and the planet suddenly absorbs 46 W/m² more than it emits, the
same surplus on both planets. That surplus has to fill the store until the planet is warm enough to emit it away.
Both bare planets end up about 7 °C warmer, but the energy needed to get there is C × ΔT: about 7 × 10⁷ J/m²
for all land against 5 × 10⁸ J/m² with 30% ocean. The bigger store takes longer to fill.

Most of what happens after a sudden change comes from how these clocks compare. When the temperature
clock is much faster than the daisy clock, you get a spike (Run 6). When the two get closer, the spike shrinks
(Run 8). When the daisy clock runs the show, the planet responds as slowly as the biology (Run 9).

Part 3. What to take away
3.1 Six points
     1. Leverage. Daisies can only change the colour of land. With 70% ocean they still grow, but they barely
        move the planet's temperature. Surviving is not the same as regulating (Runs 2 and 4).
     2. A dark ocean shifts everything. The ocean reflects little sunlight, so an ocean planet is warmer under the
        same sun, and life's window moves to a dimmer sun (Run 3).
     3. Heat capacity sets the response time. After a sudden change the bare planet always ends up about 7–8
        °C warmer, but it takes about 3 weeks with all land, 4 months with 30% ocean and 8 months with 70%
        ocean. A deeper mixed layer makes it years. τ = C/λ: the bigger the store, the longer it takes to fill (Runs
        6–8).
     4. A transient is a time out of balance. While the planet catches up, it absorbs more than it emits, and the
        difference goes into storage. Earth today is in such a transient: it absorbs about 1 W/m² more than it emits,
        and more than 90% of the excess goes into the ocean.
     5. Fast physics plus slow biology gives an overshoot. The temperature jumps before the daisies can react,
        and the daisies pull it back over years. The more slowly the temperature rises (a deeper ocean), the more
        time the daisies have and the smaller the peak (Runs 6 and 8).
     6. The planet's response time isn't only C/λ. A slow feedback can stretch it: with 70% ocean the heat
        arrives within a year, but the planet keeps warming for decades as the daisies die back (Run 9). And if the
        sun changes faster than the planet can follow, the planet lags behind (Run 10).

3.2 What the model leaves out
A model is useful partly because you know where it breaks.
        One temperature. On Earth, land and ocean have their own temperatures and their own response times:
        the yearly range of temperature is tens of degrees inside continents and only a few degrees over the open
        ocean. Here, adding ocean slows the land down too, because they share one T. This is the biggest
        simplification.
        No transport. On Earth, no place is in energy balance on its own, and the atmosphere and ocean currents
        carry heat from where there is a surplus to where there is a deficit. A one-box model has no places, so it
        can't move anything.
        No sea ice. The real ocean's albedo jumps from about 0.06 to about 0.6 when it freezes, a strong
        feedback. Here the ocean stays dark at every temperature.
        One slab. The real ocean exchanges heat with its deep layers over decades to centuries, so it responds in
        two stages, fast and then very slow.
        No greenhouse, no clouds, no weather. These keep the model simple.
        Daisies are a parable. Real vegetation does change albedo (dark forest against snow), but on Earth that
        effect is small next to clouds, ice and the ocean.
