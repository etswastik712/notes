This handout goes with the app "Daisyworld with an Ocean". Open the app next to it. The app runs in any
browser, including on a phone, and needs no installation.
        Part 1 explains what the model is and how to use the app.
        Part 2 walks through six runs. For each one it says what to set, asks you to predict what will happen, and
        then explains what you see. Try to answer each "Before you look" question before you read on.
        Part 3 sums up what to take away, and says what the model leaves out.
        An extension at the end adds daisies and grazers, if you have time.
You need no maths beyond the lecture's slab equation.

   The point in two sentences. Weather pushes on every surface of a planet, and the surface responds
   according to how much heat it can lean on. A deep, stirred ocean takes out the fast swings and turns
   weather into slow wandering, but it doesn't change the size of the slowest swings: depth sets the
   timescale, not the size of the slow swings.


Part 1. The model and the app
1.1 What is in the model, in words
The first three presets are a bare planet: no life, just physics.
        Land. One temperature for all the land. It stores little heat, so it responds within weeks.
        A mixed layer. One temperature for the stirred top of the ocean, 75 m deep, covering 70% of the planet. It
        has about 30 times the land's heat capacity.
        A deep ocean. One temperature for all the water below. It holds 13 times as much heat as the mixed layer
        and exchanges heat with it slowly.
        The sun. Sunlight rises and sets every day, and through the year swings 30% above and below its
        average, highest on 21 June (the Day and seasons box, ticked in presets 1 to 3).
        The weather. A random push of energy, in or out, that lasts a few days, like a weather system passing. A
        day's push has a spread of 30 W/m². Nothing in the weather swings slowly. The same push falls on land
        and ocean.
        Heat loss to space. A warmer surface radiates more. Every part of the model that touches the air feels
        this pull back toward balance.
There are no places, no map and no currents. No water moves sideways. All the ocean does here is store heat,
and mixing up and down decides how much of it the surface can use.

1.2 The lecture's equation, and the app's numbers
Each part of the planet obeys the slab equation from the lecture:
C dT′/dt = F′(t) − λT′
Here C is the heat capacity, F′ is the weather and λ says how fast a warmer surface loses heat. The response
time is τ = C/λ.
The app's numbers differ a little from the lecture's, for good reasons:

Part 2. Six runs to explore
Run 1. Land only
Set: preset 1, Land only.
Before you look: the weather is random, and forgets within a few days. What should a planet of bare land do?
Sketch the temperature you expect over 300 years. If you average over a whole year, then 10 years, how fast
should the wiggles shrink?




What to look for. First press Monthly and show 10 years: the seasons are huge, from about −5 °C in February to
44 °C in August.




Daisyworld with an Ocean                                                                                   4 / 12
Then go back to Yearly averages: the temperature jitters with no pattern you can see. Each bar is smaller than
the last: about 3.2 K for monthly values, 1.8 K for yearly averages, 0.6 K over 10 years and about 0.2 K over 100
years.
Why. Land forgets within about two months. After that, each push has faded, so averaging over longer periods
mostly cancels pushes against each other. The bars fall close to the dashed line. The seasons are big for the
same reason: land holds so little heat that it nearly catches up with the sun each season.

Run 2. Add a mixed layer
Set: tick Keep this run, then preset 2, Add a mixed layer. Same weather.
Before you look: you are about to give 70% of the planet 30 times the heat capacity. What happens to the
seasons? Will the planet vary more, less or the same from month to month? From century to century? Write
down your answers.

The fast bars shrink a lot and the century bar doesn't change. Now look at the yearly bar next to the monthly
one: with the mixed layer, it hardly drops. On Monthly, the seasons shrink too: the mixed layer swings only about
3 K through the year, warmest in September, nearly three months after the sun, and the land next to it about 9 K.




Daisyworld with an Ocean                                                                                     6 / 12
Why. A month of warm weather warms the mixed layer only a little, but it hasn't forgotten that warmth when the
next month comes. It adds up the kicks. So its temperature barely registers one month, then wanders over years.
Over a century, what matters is only how strongly heat is lost to space, and that didn't change. A season is a
slow push too, but not slow enough for a layer that takes about 5 years to respond: it never catches up, so its
seasons are small and late.
The ocean doesn't calm the planet evenly. It moves variation from the fast end to the slow end.

Run 3. Sort it by timescale
Set: stay on the same two runs and press Spectrum.
Before you look: the curve for the land-only run and the curve for the mixed layer should be far apart
somewhere and together somewhere else. On which side of the chart will each happen?




Daisyworld with an Ocean                                                                                  7 / 12
What to look for. On the left, at periods of months to a couple of years, the mixed-layer curve sits about 100
times below land's (each gridline is a factor of 10). On the right, past about 30 to 50 years, the two curves lie on
top of each other.
Why. For fast swings, the push doesn't have time to be balanced by heat loss, so C alone sets the response. For
slow swings, the surface has time to reach balance, F′ ≈ λT′, and C drops out of the equation.
Two details. The dotted lines mark each part's response time τ. A curve bends at a period of about 2π × τ,
because a swing of period P varies at a rate ω = 2π/P, so the bends sit somewhat to the right of the dotted lines.
And this chart shows the temperature of the whole planet, which is 30% land. The mixed layer on its own would
be several hundred times below land.

Run 4. Change the depth
Set: preset 2, then move Mixed-layer depth to 25 m. Tick Keep this run, then move it to 150 m.
Before you look: a mixed layer six times deeper. What happens to the month bar? What happens to the century
bar?
What to look for.

 Spread of the planet's temperature                     25 m                75 m                 150 m
 over 1 year                                            1.08–1.14 K         0.67–0.74 K          0.48–0.54 K
 over 10 years                                          0.51–0.62 K         0.45–0.54 K          0.37–0.45 K
 over 100 years                                         0.14–0.28 K         0.14–0.26 K          0.13–0.24 K

Why. This is the main result. Depth sets the timescale, not the size of the slow swings. A deeper layer holds on
for longer, so the fast swings shrink and the wander gets slower. The size of the slowest swings stays put,
because λ sets it, and λ doesn't care how deep the water is.

Think about it: depth here means how far the stirring reaches. The monsoon stirs the Arabian Sea from about 20
m in April to about 60 m in July. What does that do to the sea's memory during the year?

Run 5. Add a deep ocean
Set: preset 2, tick Keep this run, then preset 3, Add a deep ocean.
Before you look: a deep ocean holds 13 times more heat than the mixed layer. Do you expect more slow variation
or less?




What to look for. The century bar falls, from 0.14–0.26 K to 0.12–0.22 K. On the spectrum, power at 100 to 1,000
years falls by about a third.
Why. Many people expect a slower part to add slow variation. Here it soaks up heat instead: when the mixed
layer wanders warm, some of that warmth leaks down, and the leak acts like extra heat loss on long timescales.
The deep ocean calms the slowest swings a little.

Run 6. Brighten the sun
Set: preset 3. Move Weather size to 0, then press Brighten the sun. Note what happens on Yearly averages,
which hide the seasons. Then untick Deep ocean and do it again.
Before you look: the sun brightens by 5% and stays there. How long until the planet stops warming? A year?
Ten? Does the deep ocean change your answer?

What to look for.
 Share of the final warming (5.8 K) reached after              1 year    10 years     100 years   500 years
 With a deep ocean                                             35%       80%          87%         98%
 Without a deep ocean                                          37%       95%          100%        100%

The planet warms in two stages: most of it within 10 years, and the last fifth over centuries.
Why. The mixed layer catches up within a few of its response times. But it keeps leaking heat into the deep
ocean, which takes centuries to fill. This is the shape of the warming we are living through: more than 91% of
Earth's extra heat is going into the ocean, and the surface hasn't finished responding to today's imbalance.

Part 3. What to take away
     1. How much heat a surface can lean on depends on how heat gets below it. Land has only conduction, so
        its slab is always thin. The ocean is stirred, so its slab is tens to hundreds of metres.
     2. The ocean adds up weather. Fast, random pushes become slow wandering, with nothing slow driving it.
     3. Depth sets the timescale, not the size of the slow swings.
     4. A deep ocean adds a slower stage. It calms the slowest swings a little, and it makes warming arrive in
        two stages.
     5. All of this happens with no water moving sideways. Mixing up and down is enough.
What the model leaves out. Currents, so heat can't be carried anywhere. Geography: its day and seasons are
one place's, every day lasts 12 hours, and its land is one block sized for the year's swing, so it swings only about
1 K between day and night (real bare ground, warmed only in its top 10 cm or so by a day, swings far more). Any
change of mixed-layer depth through the year. Feedbacks that change with the ocean's temperature, such as
those behind El Niño, which is why real ocean swings can be stronger than this model's.
Questions to take to the discussion.
        Whose temperature next month would you rather forecast, Delhi's or the Arabian Sea's? Why, and what
        would make you less sure?
        The Arabian Sea cools from about 30 °C in May to about 27 °C in August, while the sun is still high. What
        in the lectures could explain that, and what in this model couldn't?

Extension. Add life
Set: preset 4, Add life, then Compare: grazers, no weather.
Daisies now set how much sunlight the land reflects, and grazers eat the daisies. The life presets run without day
and seasons, with weather that forgets from one quarter-day to the next, as they were designed. For a fair
comparison, load preset 3 and untick Day and seasons: its century bar is 0.05–0.08 K. Now load preset 4 and
look at the century bar: it is two or three times as tall (0.11–0.28 K). With no weather at all, the same planet cycles
every 8 years. Nothing outside is cycling: the ocean's delay makes the daisies and grazers overshoot, and the
weather then decides how deep each bust goes. Find three busts of different sizes in the grazer chart and read
the ocean's temperature just before each. What do you notice?
