# Tutorial Questions and Answers (Tutorials 3 and 4)

## Covered
Problem sheets for acids and bases (Tutorial 3) and main group chemistry (Tutorial 4), with handwritten answer sheets from the class. Answers below summarise what the answer sheets say.

**Tutorial 3: acids and bases** (Classroom: Tutorial 3 Questions / answers)
1. Define Brønsted (H⁺ donor / acceptor), Lewis (e⁻-pair acceptor / donor; BF3, NH3), Lux–Flood (O²⁻ acceptor = acid, donor = base; CaO + SiO2 → CaSiO3). Practises: [[ch2113-principles-of-inorganic-chemistry-august-20/2026-09-03-acids-and-bases-lecture-1]].
2. Hard and soft acids and bases. Answer: hard acids are highly charged, small cations with high polarizing power; soft acids are less charged, large, less polarizing. Hard bases: small, low polarizability, highly electronegative; soft bases: large, highly polarizable, less electronegative.
3. Why Arrhenius cannot explain NH3 basicity: NH3 contains and donates no OH⁻.
4. Conjugate acid and base (HCl + H2O → H3O⁺ + Cl⁻; NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻).
5. Conjugate bases of Si(OH)4: $[\mathrm{SiO(OH)_3}]^-$, $[\mathrm{SiO_2(OH)_2}]^{2-}$, $[\mathrm{SiO_3(OH)}]^{3-}$, $[\mathrm{SiO_4}]^{4-}$.
6. Blast furnace: CaO (O²⁻ donor, Lux–Flood base) + SiO2 (acceptor, Lux–Flood acid) → CaSiO3.
7. Fluorosulfonic vs aminosulfonic acid: stronger −I effect of F gives a more stable conjugate base, hence higher acidity (F > NH2).
8. BBr3 vs BF3: in BF3, 2p–2p back-donation (pπ–pπ) from F to B reduces B's electron deficiency; in BBr3 2p–3p back-donation is weak, so B stays electron-deficient. Back-donation order: 2p–2p > 2p–3p > 2p–4p.
9. SO2: Lewis acid (S electron-poor), e.g. SO2 + OH⁻ → HSO3⁻, SO2 + H2O → H2SO3; weak Lewis base, donating via O to a strong acid like SbF5.
10. SiF4 + 2HF → [SiF6]²⁻ + 2H⁺: SiF4 accepts F⁻ (acid), HF donates F⁻ (base). Me3Si–I + AgBr → Me3Si–Br + AgI: a metathesis (the answer sheet labels Si as acid and AgBr as base).
11. Ambidentate [NCS]⁻: N- or S-donor; hard acid centres prefer N, soft prefer S; gives linkage isomers, e.g. $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_5(NCS)}]^{2+}$ (κN) and $[\mathrm{Co(NH_3)_5(SCN)}]^{2+}$ (κS).
12. Madelung energy and London dispersion: hard–hard interactions (small size, short distance, high charge density) are governed by Madelung (electrostatic) energy; soft–soft interactions (large, polarizable, significant covalency) are stabilized by London dispersion, which grows with size and polarizability.

**Tutorial 4: main group chemistry**
1. Products: Mg3B2 + H3PO4(aq) gives boron hydrides (B4H10, B5H9, B6H10); NaBH4 with I2 / H3PO4 / BF3 gives B2H6; BF3 with NaH / LiH / LiAlH4 gives B2H6.
2. MO explanation of the 3c–2e⁻ bonds in B2H6 (12 electrons; 8 in the terminal B–H bonds; 4 left for the two bridges). Practises: [[ch2113-principles-of-inorganic-chemistry-august-20/2026-09-16-main-group-chemistry-cages-and-clusters]].
3. B2H6 + NMe3 → 2 BH3·NMe3 (symmetric cleavage); 3 B2H6 + 6 NH3 → 3 [BH2(NH3)2][BH4], then heat → borazine + H2 (asymmetric cleavage first).
4. Skeletal electron pairs and class. The answer sheet gives: [B12H12]²⁻ closo, 13 pairs; B10H14 nido, 12 pairs; B5H11 arachno, 8 pairs; B5H13 hypho, 9 pairs; Na4Sn9 as [Sn9]⁴⁻: $g = 40$, $g - 2n = 22 = 2n + 4$, nido. (The sheet also lists super-hypercloso $n-1$ and hypercloso $n$.)
5. Borazine: 3 B2H6 + 6 NH3 → 3 [BH2(NH3)2][BH4] → 2 B3N3H6 + 12 H2.
6. B3N3H6 + 3 Br2 (0 °C) → B-tribromo-N-tribromoborazine (addition product). Cr(CO)6 + benzene → Cr(C6H6)(CO)3 + 3 CO; with borazine the analogous tricarbonyl complex forms, with a puckered borazine.
7. Polyborazylenes: heating borazine at 80 °C expels H2 and couples units para-wise by new B–N bonds; low-temperature dehydropolymerization gives a cyclo-linear polymer ($M_w \approx$ 7600); pyrolysis at 1200 °C gives white BN. (The sheet writes "Pyrolysis at 1200 °C".)
8. Dewar model for cyclotriphosphazenes: the $d_{xz}$ and $d_{yz}$ orbitals of P hybridize to give two orbitals directed at the neighbouring N atoms; this allows three-centre π bonds about each N (the "island" model), with delocalization over three-atom segments and a node at each P because the two hybrid orbitals are orthogonal.
9. P3N3Cl6 with NaF/CH3CN gives P3N3F6; excess CF3CH2ONa gives P3N3(OCH2CF3)6 (nucleophilic substitution); the RMgX product is drawn on the sheet as a substituted ring with P–R.
10. Polyphosphazenes: hybrid inorganic–organic polymers with a P–N–P–N backbone and two organic side groups on each P, linear formula $(\mathrm{N{=}PR^1R^2})_n$; made by heating (NPCl2)3 at 210–250 °C then substituting Cl (RONa, RNH2, R2NH, RM).
