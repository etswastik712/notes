# Acids and Bases: Lecture 1

## Covered
- The acid–base definitions are not fundamental theories like VBT and MOT. They are different, convenient viewpoints, not right or wrong. (Classroom: Acids and Bases Lecture 1)
- **Arrhenius**: an acid raises $[\mathrm{H^+}]$ ($[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]$) in water; a base raises $[\mathrm{OH^-}]$. About 1 in $10^7$ water molecules is hydronium (and an equal number hydroxide). It explains neutralization, salt hydrolysis and acid/base strength.
  - Limitations: it fails for CO2, SO2, SO3 (acidic, no H); for NH3, CaO, MgO (basic, no OH); and for salt solutions that are not neutral (HCl + NH3 is slightly acidic, CH3COOH + NaOH is basic). It led to the wrong formula NH4OH (the actual base is NH3). It cannot explain the acidity of AlCl3 in water.
- **Brønsted–Lowry**: acids are proton donors, bases proton acceptors. It handles any protic solvent (liquid NH3, H2SO4). Water is amphoteric.
  - Only this theory describes CH3COOH + NH3 ⇌ CH3COO⁻ + NH4⁺, which makes no hydronium.
  - Proton transfer is fast in both directions (dynamic equilibrium). The conjugate base is what remains after a proton is lost; the conjugate acid is formed when a proton is gained. A conjugate acid is just another acid.
  - H3O⁺ is hydrogen-bonded (H9O4⁺; gas-phase H⁺(H2O)21).
  - Limit: only useful for protic systems.
- **Lux–Flood**: oxide-ion transfer, for non-protic systems (molten oxides, geochemistry, molten-salt electrochemistry). A base is a basic anhydride (O²⁻ donor); an acid is an acid anhydride (O²⁻ acceptor). Examples: MgO + CO2 ⇌ MgCO3; CaO + SiO2 ⇌ CaSiO3; NO3⁻ (base) + S2O7²⁻ (acid) ⇌ NO2⁺ + 2 SO4²⁻.
- **Lewis**: the most widely used (simple, broad, especially in organic chemistry). It includes reactions where no ions form, and covers H⁺ and O²⁻ transfer, solvent interactions, acid–base adducts and all coordination compounds.
- **Solvent system**: an acid increases the concentration of the solvent's characteristic cation; a base increases the characteristic anion.
- **Generalized concept**: acidity is positive character of a species, decreased by reaction with a base; basicity is negative character, decreased by reaction with an acid. Two advantages: it incorporates all the definitions, and it gives a criterion for correlating strength with electron density and structure.
