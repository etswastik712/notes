# Lecture Notes: 1 and 2

## Covered
**Lewis theory** (Classroom: Lecture 1a_Lewis_structures_2026.pdf)
- Qualitative models (Lewis, VSEPR) are pictorial; semi-quantitative models are CFT, VB and MO theory. The true molecular structure comes from an interplay of both kinds.
- A covalent bond is a shared electron pair (A—B, A=B, A≡B). Unshared valence-electron pairs are lone pairs: they do not bond directly but influence shape and chemical properties.
- Octet rule: each atom shares electrons with its neighbours to reach a total of 8 valence electrons.
- Steps for a Lewis structure:
  1. Total the valence electrons; add one electron per negative charge, remove one per positive charge.
  2. Write the atoms in their bonded arrangement; the central atom is usually the less electronegative element (exceptions, e.g. H2O and NH3).
  3. Put one electron pair between each bonded pair, then add lone pairs or multiple bonds until every atom has an octet.
  4. The net charge of a polyatomic ion belongs to the ion as a whole.
- Examples set: $[\mathrm{BF_4}]^-$, N2, CO, $[\mathrm{ClO_4}]^-$, $[\mathrm{PO_4}]^{3-}$, $[\mathrm{SO_4}]^{2-}$.
- Resonance: one Lewis structure is often inadequate (O3 has two identical O–O bonds). Resonance averages the bond characteristics over the molecule, and the hybrid has lower energy than any single contributing structure. Exercise: resonance structures of $[\mathrm{NO_3}]^-$.
- Formal charge: the charge an atom would have if every shared pair were shared equally. $f = V - L - \tfrac{1}{2}P$, where $V$ is the number of valence electrons, $L$ the number of lone-pair electrons and $P$ the number of shared electrons.
- The lowest-energy structure has (1) the lowest formal charges on the atoms and (2) the more electronegative element carrying any formal negative charge and the less electronegative one any formal positive charge. Worked example: NO2F (formal charges calculated for each candidate structure, preferred structure has N = +1, O = −1, other O = 0, F = 0).
- Oxidation number: derived by exaggerating the ionic character of a bond (formal charge exaggerates the covalent character). The more electronegative atom takes the bonding electrons; $[\mathrm{NO_3}]^-$ is treated as $[\mathrm{N^{5+}}][\mathrm{O^{2-}}]_3$, so N is N(V).

**VSEPR** (Classroom: Lecture notes_VSEPR_2026.pdf)
- Limits of the octet rule: incomplete octet (LiCl, BeH2, BCl3); odd-electron molecules (NO, NO2); expanded octet (PCl5, SF6, H2SO4). It also cannot account for noble-gas compounds, molecular shape, or relative stabilities.
- Primary assumption: regions of enhanced electron density (bonding pairs, lone pairs, multiple bonds) sit as far apart as possible to minimise repulsion.
- Basic shapes by number of regions: 2 linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral, 5 trigonal bipyramidal (TBP), 6 octahedral, 7 pentagonal bipyramidal.
- Steps: count the valence electrons of the central and bonding peripheral atoms; get the number of pairs and the basic shape; place σ bonds, lone pairs and multiple bonds. A multiple bond counts as one region, along its σ bond (POCl3, COCl2).
- Worked patterns: POCl3 has 10 valence electrons = 5 pairs, minus 1 pair for the double bond, so 4 regions, tetrahedral. $\mathrm{SO_3^{2-}}$ has 14 electrons = 7 pairs, minus 3 pairs for the S–O bonds/oxide, so 4 regions, tetrahedral basic shape, trigonal pyramidal actual shape.
- Repulsion order: lp/lp > lp/bp > bp/bp. Lone pairs are on average closer to the nucleus, so they repel more and need larger sites (equatorial in TBP; trans to each other if all sites are equal).
- SF4: 10 electrons = 5 pairs, TBP basic shape; the lone pair is equatorial because that site has two 90° interactions against three for an axial site; axial bonds are distorted.
- Angles: CH4 109.5° (no lone pair), NH3 107° (1 lone pair), H2O 104.5° (2 lone pairs). In COF2 the double bond is shorter, so =O needs more angular volume (larger angle). SF4 axial F–S–F is about 179°/178.5°/170° and equatorial about 103°/110°/97° across the three structures drawn (the slide gives the numbers without labels).
- Summary rules: electron pairs minimise repulsion; lp/lp > lp/bp > bp/bp; lone pairs make bond angles smaller than predicted; lone pairs choose larger sites; double bonds occupy more space; electronegative substituents occupy less space than electropositive ones (Bent's rule); lone pairs are less stereochemically influential for heavy elements.
- Examples of stereochemically inert lone pairs: octahedral $[\mathrm{SeF_6}]^{2-}$ and $[\mathrm{TeCl_6}]^{2-}$; $[\mathrm{XeF_5}]^-$ is pentagonal planar with two axial lone pairs. Explanation on the slide: the lone pair sits in a non-directional s orbital.
