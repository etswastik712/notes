# Study material 12: Large-scale circulation pattern

## Covered
Source: Hartmann, *Global Physical Climatology*, Ch. 6 (pp. 180–188 of the PDF: end of §6.4 and §6.5 "Large-scale circulation patterns and climate"). (Classroom: Study material 12)

- **Angular momentum flow (Fig. 6.17):** westerly angular momentum goes from Earth to the atmosphere in the tropical easterlies (surface trades), is carried poleward and downward by eddies, and returns to Earth in the mid-latitude surface westerlies (January winds and mean meridional streamfunction).
- **Zonal asymmetry:** east–west variations of wind and temperature matter for regional climate. In January the Northern Hemisphere subtropical jet has two local maxima downstream of the Tibetan Plateau and the Rocky Mountains, over the Pacific and Atlantic. The time-mean wind maxima coincide with maxima of transient eddy activity and eddy heat and moisture fluxes, which define the **storm tracks**. In July the NH mid-latitude maximum is weaker and farther poleward. In the Southern Hemisphere the mid-latitude jet near 50° S exists in all seasons, while the subtropical jet is strongest in July (winter) over the Western Pacific and Australia, where strong convection over Indonesia drives a localized Hadley circulation. SH transient-eddy heat flux is concentrated in the Atlantic and Indian sectors and is weakest in the Pacific; the seasonal change of transient eddy heat flux is much larger in the NH (Fig. 6.18).
- **Surface pressure (Fig. 6.19, 1000 hPa height as proxy; global mean ≈ 16 m removed):** near the surface air spirals inward to lows (so must rise by mass conservation) and outward from highs (subsidence above the boundary layer, suppressed convection). Pressure near 30° is higher than at the equator, consistent with trade winds blowing toward the ITCZ, where pressure is low and deep convection with latent heat release occurs.
- **Seasonal land–sea contrast:** January has the Aleutian and Icelandic lows at the northern margins of the Pacific and Atlantic and a high over Asia. In July the contrast reverses: highest pressures over the oceans (centred near 40° N, vs ≈ 30° N in January) and lowest over land; the dominant NH summer low is centred over Asia near 30° N and is associated with the Asian summer monsoon. Cause: seasonal insolation changes together with the different responses of land and ocean. The ocean response is smaller because energy goes into a deep layer of large heat capacity and because evaporation consumes much of the input; land has little heat storage and is often too dry for evaporation to balance the summer insolation increase. Lows occupy regions where the atmosphere is heated, highs where it is cooled; land is warmer than the adjacent ocean in summer and colder in winter.
- **6.5.1 Monsoonal climates:** regular seasonal changes of wind speed and direction (Arabic word for "season"), with shifts between very dry and very rainy regimes. In summer the Tibetan Plateau is heated and transfers much of it to the atmosphere; the resulting low surface pressure draws a low-level flow of warm, moist air from the ocean onto land, giving intense rain over India and the Himalayan slopes (especially where the winds meet the western Ghats and Himalayas); latent heating drives the upward motion balancing low-level convergence. In winter the flow reverses (air from land to the Indian Ocean) and India has a wintertime drought. The wind shift is strongest in the western Indian Ocean and drives a strong ocean response (SST). Onset is abrupt, typically around mid-June, and onset date, duration and intensity vary year to year (floods or droughts) (Figs 6.20–6.21).
- **6.5.2 Desert climates:** precipitation significantly less than potential evapotranspiration. Causes: (i) persistent subsidence of the Hadley circulation over roughly 10°–40° latitude in both hemispheres; (ii) localized subsidence on the downwind side of mountain ranges (North American deserts east of the Cascades/Sierra/Rockies, Monte and Patagonian deserts east of the Andes); (iii) cold coastal SSTs (Peru and Chile coast: subsidence, easterly flow over the Andes, and cold SST, which makes the air stable to moist convection); (iv) blockage from humid air, with rain-out on the windward side and very low relative humidity on the lee side (Gobi, central Asia). The Sahara and Arabian deserts combine large land mass, subtropical subsidence and strong insolation; with high temperature and albedo they have **net radiation loss in the annual average**, compensated by import of high-energy air at upper levels, which subsides while radiatively cooling and exports moisture at low levels. Examples: Ouallen (22.8° N) July mean 38 °C, January 16 °C, ≈ 22 mm yr$^{-1}$; Antofagasta (23.5° S) 13–20 °C, ≈ 2 mm yr$^{-1}$. Deserts have a large diurnal temperature range (rapid night cooling under dry, cloudless sky), largest at altitude where the greenhouse effect is weak.
- **6.5.3 Wet climates:** precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration for much of the year. Tropical wet climates follow from the circulation bringing warm moist air to the equator (ITCZ). Three OLR minima along the equator: South America, Africa, Indonesian–western Pacific region. Rain zones move with the seasons and tend to lie in the summer hemisphere. The Maritime Continent (equatorial location, shallow seas, small land masses, high SST, strong diurnal land–sea breezes) is the largest region of intense precipitation and a strong thermal forcing for the large-scale circulation; in SH summer its convection extends southeast as the **South Pacific convergence zone** (SPCZ, to ≈ 30° S, 140° W); in NH summer it extends northwest into the Bay of Bengal and joins the Asian summer monsoon. The Amazon Basin (>2 m rain per year) is fed by north-easterly trade winds carrying water vapour; the Andes block the flow, and latent heating plus rainfall recycling sustain the convection and the Amazon River's discharge; away from the equator rainfall is seasonal and greatest in the summer hemisphere, so opposite sides of the equator have rainy seasons six months apart (Fig. 6.23).
