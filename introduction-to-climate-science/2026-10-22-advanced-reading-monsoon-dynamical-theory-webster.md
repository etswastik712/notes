# Advanced reading: Monsoon: Dynamical Theory (Webster & Fasullo)

## Covered
Source: P. J. Webster and J. Fasullo, "Monsoon: Dynamical Theory", *Encyclopedia of Atmospheric Sciences* (2nd ed., vol. 6, 2003 reprint), pp. 151ff. Labelled by the instructor as **advanced reading**, for those who want to explore the topic further. The first ~35,000 of ~97,000 characters were read (Elements of a monsoon, Differential heating, Mixing and storage of heat, Generation of pressure gradient forces, and the start of the moist processes section); the rest is not yet filed. (Classroom: announcement 2026-10-22)

- **Monsoon definition:** in summer, lower-tropospheric winds flow toward the heated continents from the colder winter-hemisphere ocean; in the upper troposphere the flow is reversed. Rain falls mainly in summer around the solstice, near a **monsoon trough** of low pressure, generally well poleward of the oceanic ITCZ (South Asian monsoon rain falls at the latitudes of the great deserts). Most rain comes from synoptic disturbances grouped in 10–30 day **active periods**; the intervening mini-droughts are **breaks**.
- **Systems:** Asian–Australian, West African and American monsoons (colocated continent pairs, or continents straddling the equator). The northern American monsoon is weak with no discernible cross-equatorial flow. Rain over equator-spanning continents is not strictly monsoonal: it has double maxima at the equinoxes, while monsoon climates have a single solstitial maximum.
- **Ingredients of the pressure gradients:** differential land–ocean heating (heat capacity), different vertical heat transfer and storage, modification by moist processes, generation of meridional pressure-gradient forces, and ocean heat transport, all considered relative to planetary rotation, geography and topography.
- **Heat capacity (Halley):** specific heat of water 4218 J kg$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$ vs ≈ 1300 for dry land (factor ≈ 4; wet soil about 30 % higher than dry), so land warms nearly 4× more for the same heating per unit mass. Halley (late 17th century) proposed this heating contrast as the monsoon driver and recognised the annual solar cycle's role in the seasonal reversal; the paper argues his theory must be extended.
- **Slab heating:** with surface flux $F$ and no flux out of the bottom of a layer of thickness $\Delta z$: $\frac{dT}{dt}=-\frac{1}{\rho C_p}\frac{dF}{dz}=\frac{1}{\rho C_p}\frac{F_{z=0}}{\Delta z}$ [1]. Surface balance $F_{z=0}=I_{net}-H_s-H_e$ [2] (sensible and latent turbulent fluxes), with $I_{net}=S(1-a)+\varepsilon\sigma T_g^4-\sigma T_a^4$ [3] (as printed).
- **Why land heats faster:** land's small $\Delta z$ (soil opaque to radiation; the active annual-cycle layer is only ~1 m because conduction is inefficient) and small heat capacity outweigh its larger albedo (20–40 % vs ≈ 10 % for ocean). Over an immobile ocean the effective depth is the solar e-folding depth (~10 m), which would give far too large a temperature range. Forcing $F\propto\sin\omega t$ gives $T\propto-\cos\omega t$, lagging the forcing by a quarter period.
- **Ocean mixing and storage:** wind stirring and convective overturning deepen the mixed layer (50–100 m in the tropics) so heat is spread over a depth far larger than the solar e-folding depth; SST changes are smaller and lag the solar cycle. In summer net surface heating is positive and wind mixing stores heat; in winter cooled surface water is mixed down and replaced by warmer subsurface water. Fresh layers from rain can stabilise the surface and reduce stirring. Land temperature follows the solstices, though because of moist processes it peaks before the onset of summer rains.
- **Pressure gradient reversal with height:** eliminating density between the equation of state and hydrostatic balance gives $\partial p/\partial z=-gp/(RT)$ [4]; pressure decreases with height more slowly over a warm column. Integrating: $\Delta\ln p(z_1)=\frac gR z_1\left(\frac1{T_c}-\frac1{T_w}\right)+\Delta\ln p(0)$ [5] ($T_w$, $T_c$ mean temperatures of the warm and cold columns). With equal surface pressures, $\Delta\ln p(z_1)>0$ needs $T_w>T_c$: upper flow from summer to winter hemisphere, with a low-level return flow.
- **Threshold:** observed surface pressure over the winter subtropics is higher than over the heated continent, by up to ~20 hPa, and $\Delta\ln p(0)$ becomes more negative as heating increases, so $T_w>T_c$ is not sufficient. The condition becomes $T_w>\dfrac{gz_1T_c}{gz_1+R\,\Delta\ln p(0)\,T_c}$ [6]. Hence there may be a threshold in the temperature gradient, which may explain the sudden onset of the South Asian monsoon in late May or early June, with deep convection and heavy rain.
- **Dry monsoon:** in steady state surface heating balances radiative loss to space; vertical extent is set by the heat input and stratification, and the longitudinal extent by the time a parcel takes to radiate away excess heat (radiative e-folding ≈ 20 days).
- **Moist processes (start):** latent heat release over the summer continent or adjacent seas gives strong mid-tropospheric heating. Moisture is evaporated from the ocean and carried by the low-level flow ($B_q=\int_0^\infty q\vec V\,dz$ [7], dominated by the boundary layer; accumulation zones south of Asia in boreal summer and north of Australia in boreal winter). Boundary-layer air moves over increasing SST, warms and moistens. Spring heating drives a low-level pressure gradient and moist advection; once enough vapour is imported, latent heat release warms the column and produces the reversed upper-level gradient, after which the monsoon accelerates: surface winds exceed 10 m s$^{-1}$ and North Indian Ocean SST drops 1–2 °C.
