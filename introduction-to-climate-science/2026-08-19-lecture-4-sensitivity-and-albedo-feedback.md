# Lecture 4: Sensitivity & (albedo) Feedback

## Covered
Student-written notes (made with Gemini Notebook, reviewed by Yugansh and Anjali) on slides 41–48. Textbook: Kump, Kasting & Crane, *The Earth System*.

**Parameter sensitivity (slides 41–42)**
- Start from $\dfrac{S}{4}(1-\alpha)=\sigma T_s^4$. Take logs: $\ln S+\ln(1-\alpha)-\ln 4=\ln\sigma+4\ln T$. Differentiate:
$$\frac{\Delta S}{S}-\frac{\Delta\alpha}{1-\alpha}=4\,\frac{\Delta T_s}{T_s}.$$
- Constant $S$: $-\dfrac{\Delta\alpha}{1-\alpha}\approx4\dfrac{\Delta T_s}{T_s}$. A change $\Delta\alpha=0.07$ gives $\Delta T_s\sim6$ K (as stated in the notes).
- Constant $\alpha$: $4\dfrac{\Delta T_s}{T_s}\approx\dfrac{\Delta S}{S}$. A 10 % change in the solar constant ($\Delta S\sim136$ W/m$^2$) means ~24 W/m$^2$ net (averaged over the sphere, allowing for albedo), giving a shift of ~6 K.
- Radiative forcing = change in the planet's energy balance caused by a specific factor. Sunspot cycle: only ~1 W/m$^2$. Anthropogenic forcing (greenhouse gases and land use): ~3 W/m$^2$, well above natural solar variability.
- Slide 41 bar chart (forcing since 1750): CO$_2$ is the dominant warming driver, then CH$_4$, N$_2$O, halogenated gases. Cooling: aerosols (direct and via clouds) and land-use changes that raise albedo. "Total anthropogenic" ≈ +3 W/m$^2$ against "Solar" 0.06 W/m$^2$.
- Slide 42: (a) observed warming ≈ 1.1 °C relative to 1850–1900; (b) total human influence accounts for nearly 100 % of observed warming, solar/volcanic drivers and internal variability (e.g. ENSO) are near zero over this period; (c) well-mixed greenhouse gases alone would give ~1.5 °C, offset by ~−0.5 °C from sulfur dioxide/aerosols (the "masking effect").
- Textbook: Ch. 15 "Radiative Forcing"; Ch. 3 Box "A Closer Look: Planetary Energy Balance".

**Planck feedback, no atmosphere, constant albedo (slide 43)**
- With a forcing $F$: $\dfrac{S(1-\alpha)}{4}+F=\sigma T_s^4$. Differentiate in $F$ with $S,\alpha$ fixed: $1=4\sigma T_s^3\,\dfrac{dT_s}{dF}$, so
$$\Lambda_P\equiv\frac{dT_s}{dF}=\frac{1}{4\sigma T_s^3}.$$
- Negative (stabilising) feedback: warming increases emission $\propto T^4$, which cools the planet.
- At 255 K, $\Lambda_P\approx0.28$ K (W m$^{-2}$)$^{-1}$ (value as given in the notes); a 1 K rise needs ≈ 3.8 W/m$^2$ forcing on this atmosphere-free planet.
- Textbook: Ch. 3 "The IR Flux/Temperature Feedback", "Climate Feedbacks" (pp. 53–54).

**Variable albedo (slide 44)**
- Albedo depends on temperature (ice melts when warmer). Feedback factor $f_\alpha=\dfrac{\partial T_s}{\partial\alpha}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\alpha}{\partial T_s}$. Ice-albedo: $\partial\alpha/\partial T_s<0$ and $\partial T_s/\partial\alpha<0$, so $f_\alpha>0$ (positive feedback).
- Total sensitivity (chain rule): $\Lambda=\dfrac{\Lambda_F}{1-f_\alpha}$. With $f_\alpha>0$, $1-f_\alpha<1$, so $\Lambda>\Lambda_P$: less than 3.8 W/m$^2$ is needed per kelvin; feedbacks amplify the initial change.
- Clarification added to slide 44 (announcement 2026-08-17): definition of the feedback and sensitivity factors.
- Textbook: Ch. 3 "Snow and Ice Albedo Feedback" (p. 54).

**Multiple steady states and stability (slides 45–47)**
- Temperature-dependent albedo: 0.7 for $T<253$ K (ice covered); linear drop for $253\le T\le293$ K; 0.2 for $T>293$ K (ice free).
- $\mathrm{SW_{net}}$ and $\mathrm{LW_{out}}$ curves intersect at three points: (1) stable snowball planet at low $T$; (2) unstable intermediate point (bifurcation: cooling below it gives runaway ice-albedo feedback into a Snowball); (3) stable ice-free planet at high $T$ (like modern or future greenhouse climate).

**Snowball Earth, Cryogenian (~720–635 Ma) (slide 48)**
- Evidence: (i) glacial sediments (tillites, striations, dropstones) in Neoproterozoic rocks that paleomagnetism places within 15° of the equator; (ii) cap carbonates directly on glacial deposits (warm-water rocks, so an abrupt icehouse-to-hothouse transition); (iii) a sharp $\delta^{13}$C drop, indicating collapse of primary productivity under a sealed ocean.
- Trigger: big CO$_2$ drawdown. Rodinia's breakup created continental shelves (more organic carbon burial, faster silicate weathering); continents in the tropics kept silicate weathering going even as the planet cooled, removing the stabilising feedback.
- Runaway: if polar ice reaches ~30° latitude, ice-albedo feedback becomes unstoppable until the ocean freezes to the equator; the Snowball is a stable steady state (slide 47).
- Escape: ice cover shut down silicate weathering while volcanoes kept emitting CO$_2$; over roughly 10 Myr CO$_2$ reached ~0.1 bar (~300 times modern) and its greenhouse effect overcame the ice's albedo. Melting dropped albedo rapidly, giving a very hot state (50–60 °C), heavy weathering and the cap carbonates.
- Textbook: Ch. 12 Box "Thinking Quantitatively: Energy Balance Modeling of the Snowball Earth".
