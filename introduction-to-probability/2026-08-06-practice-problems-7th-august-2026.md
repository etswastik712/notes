# Practice problems 7 August 2026

## Covered
The first problem sheet, for the Friday 7 August tutorial and the weekend after. It was posted twice: a first draft on 2026-08-05 with problems 1-5 (problem 6 blank), and the full update of 12 problems on 2026-08-06, summarised here (Classroom: Practice problems 7th August 2026.pdf).

The TAs discuss some of these in the Friday tutorial, possibly with similar problems instead. Further practice: the problems at the back of Chapter 1 of Blitzstein and Hwang, and Sheldon Ross.

| # | Problem | What it practises |
|---|---------|-------------------|
| 1 | Ranking of $n$ race finishers; outcomes if only the top three matter | permutations, $n!$ and $n(n-1)(n-2)$ |
| 2 | One card from 52: sample space as a set; $A$ = ace, $B$ = heart; $P[A]$, $P[B]$, $A\cup B$, $A\cap B$ with interpretations | sample space, naive definition, union and intersection of events |
| 3 | Two dice rolled one by one; probability the sum is 11, or 12 | naive definition on an ordered sample space |
| 4 | Club of $n$: choose a team of 3; choose president, vice president, secretary | combinations versus permutations |
| 5 | 5-card hand: probability of a full house; of a straight flush | counting with combinations, naive definition |
| 6 | Round-robin with $n$ players: number of tournament outcomes (winner of each game); number of matches | basic counting principle, $\binom n2$ |
| 7 | Fred's dinners Monday-Friday at ten restaurants: no repeats; repeats allowed but at most twice in a row | permutations; counting with a restriction |
| 8 | Split 12 people into teams of 2, 5, 5; and into three teams of 4 (teams unlabelled) | multinomial coefficients, dividing out identical groups |
| 9 | Prove Pascal's identity by a combinatorial ("story") argument | story proofs |
| 10 | Three people in an elevator, 10 floors, each equally likely to leave at floors 2-10 independently: probability that three consecutive floors are pressed | naive definition with independent choices |
| 11 | 6 children (3 boys, 3 girls), all birth orders equally likely: probability the 3 eldest are girls | arrangements with indistinguishable types |
| 12 | $A\subset B$: show $P[B\setminus A]=P[B]-P[A]$ from an axiom | axiom 3 (additivity), see [[introduction-to-probability/2026-08-05-class-notes-6th-august-2026]] |

Note from the sheet: "people are always distinguishable" will not be repeated in quizzes or exams; unlabelled teams (problem 8) are to be understood by common sense.
