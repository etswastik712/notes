# Arithmetic on the Natural Numbers from the Successor

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span> <span class="scope-extra-beyond">extra · beyond</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** [[L-25.pdf#page=1|Lecture 25]]: the definitions of $+$ and $\cdot$, Theorem 3 ($1+n=n+1$), Theorem 4 (associativity), the list of further results.
> 🔵 **extra · beyond:** full proofs of commutativity, cancellation and $a+z\ne a$ (mine), and a machine check on $1\le n\le40$.

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Adding $m$ means *taking the successor $m$ times*; multiplying by $m$ means *adding $n$ to itself $m$ times*. Every law about them is proved by induction on one variable, with the others held fixed.

> [!key] Definition
> $n+1:=s(n)$, $n+s(m):=s(n+m)$ (so $n+(m+1)=(n+m)+1$ by definition); $n\cdot1:=n$, $n\cdot(m+1):=n\cdot m+n$. That these rules define functions on $N\times N$ is the **Recursion Theorem**. Proved by induction: $1+n=n+1$; $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$; $a+b=b+a$; $a+c=b+c\Rightarrow a=b$ (uses (P2)); multiplication commutative, associative, distributive.

Worked: $2+2=2+s(1)=s(2+1)=s(s(2))=s(3)=4$. The usual trap: the rule $n+(m+1)=(n+m)+1$ is the *definition* of $+$ and the **base case** of associativity, not associativity itself.
