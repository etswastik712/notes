**One-parameter subgroups (DG25)**
- Definition: a Lie group homomorphism $\gamma:\mathbb R\to G$ is called a one-parameter subgroup of $G$.
- Thm 20.1: the one-parameter subgroups of $G$ are exactly the maximal integral curves of left-invariant vector fields on $G$ starting at $e\in G$.
- Proof ($\Rightarrow$): let $\gamma:\mathbb R\to G$ be a Lie group homomorphism. $d/dt$ is a left-invariant field on $\mathbb R$; let $X$ be the unique left-invariant field on $G$ with $X_e=\gamma'(0)$, which is $\gamma$-related to $d/dt$. So $\gamma'(t)=X_{\gamma(t)}$ and $\gamma(0)=e$: $\gamma$ is the maximal integral curve of $X$ through $e$.
- Proof ($\Leftarrow$): let $\gamma$ be the maximal integral curve of a left-invariant $X$ starting at $e$. Left-invariant fields are complete (Prop 9.18), so $\gamma$ is defined on $\mathbb R$. Fix $s$. $L_{\gamma(s)}$ takes integral curves of $X$ to integral curves of $X$ (left-invariance), so $t\mapsto\gamma(s)\gamma(t)$ is an integral curve starting at $\gamma(s)$; so is $t\mapsto\gamma(s+t)$. By uniqueness they agree: $\gamma(s+t)=\gamma(s)\gamma(t)$, so $\gamma$ is a homomorphism.

**Matrix exponential (DG26)**
- Prop 20.2: let $A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb R)$. (i) The series $\sum_{k\ge 0}A^k/k!$ converges to a matrix in $GL(n,\mathbb R)$, written $e^A$. (ii) The one-parameter subgroup generated by $A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb R)$ is $\gamma(t)=e^{tA}$.
- Proof of (i): with $\|A\|\le c$, $\|A^k/k!\|\le c^k/k!$; comparison with $\sum c^k/k!$ and the Weierstrass M-test give uniform convergence on bounded sets of $M(n,\mathbb R)$. Invertibility: $e^{A}e^{-A}=I$ and likewise $e^{-A}e^{A}=I$ (the derivation is partly illegible: it uses $\sigma(t)=e^{tA}e^{-tA}$ having derivative $0$, hence constant $=I$).
- Proof of (ii): $\gamma(t)=e^{tA}$ satisfies $\gamma(0)=I$, $\gamma'(t)=\gamma(t)A$ (termwise differentiation is allowed since convergence is uniform on bounded sets) and $\gamma(s+t)=\gamma(s)\gamma(t)$; so it is the one-parameter subgroup with initial velocity $A$.
- Prop 20.3: let $H$ be a Lie subgroup of $G$. The one-parameter subgroups of $H$ are those one-parameter subgroups of $G$ whose initial velocity lies in $T_eH$.
- Example: $SO(n)$ with $\mathfrak{so}(n)=\{A: A+A^T=0\}$ (skew-symmetric matrices). Every Lie group homomorphism $\mathbb R\to SO(n)$ has the form $t\mapsto e^{tA}$ with $A$ skew-symmetric.

**Exponential map (DG26)**
- Definition: $\exp:\mathfrak g\to G$ by $\exp(X)=\gamma_X(1)$, where $\gamma_X$ is the one-parameter subgroup generated by $X$ (the maximal integral curve of the left-invariant field with $X_e$ given, through $e$).
- Prop 20.5: for $X\in\mathfrak g$, $s\mapsto\exp(sX)$ is the one-parameter subgroup generated by $X$. Proof: let $\gamma$ be the maximal integral curve of $X$ at $e$ and fix $s$; $\tilde\gamma(t)=\gamma(st)$ has $\tilde\gamma'(t)=sX_{\tilde\gamma(t)}$, so it is the maximal integral curve of $sX$ starting at $e$, i.e. $\exp(tsX)=\gamma_{sX}(t)=\gamma(st)$; at $t=1$ this is $\exp(sX)$.
- Example: for $G=GL(n,\mathbb R)$, $\exp A=e^A$.
- Properties, for $X\in\mathfrak g$:
  - (a) $\exp$ is smooth. Proof: define the vector field $V$ on $\mathbb R\times G$... precisely, the field $\tilde X$ on $\mathfrak g\times G$ given by $\tilde X_{(Y,g)}=(0,\,d(L_g)_e Y)$ (the slide's definition is partly garbled); its flow is $\Phi_t(Y,g)=(Y,\,g\exp(tY))$, which is smooth by the Fundamental Theorem on Flows, and $\exp=\pi_G\circ\Phi_1(\cdot,e)$ is smooth.
  - (b) $\exp((s+t)X)=\exp(sX)\exp(tX)$.
  - (c) $\exp(-X)=(\exp X)^{-1}$.
  - (d) for $n\in\mathbb Z$, $(\exp X)^n=\exp(nX)$.
- $d\exp_0:T_0\mathfrak g\cong\mathfrak g\to T_eG$ is the identity map (under $\varepsilon$). Hence by the Inverse Function Theorem there are open $U\ni 0$ in $\mathfrak g$ and $V\ni e$ in $G$ with $\exp:U\to V$ a diffeomorphism.
- Last slide (cut off, partly illegible): relation of $\exp$ to a homomorphism $F:G\to H$ (presumably $F\circ\exp_G=\exp_H\circ F_*$, via integral curves of $X$ and $F_*X$; check the PDF). The slide also uses $d\exp_X=d(L_{\exp X})_e\circ\dots$ for an expression of $d\exp$, which follows from the Inverse Function Theorem argument.
