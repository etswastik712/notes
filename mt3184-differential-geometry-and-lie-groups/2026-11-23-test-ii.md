# Test II (2026-11-23)

## Covered
The exam paper (IISER Pune, MT3184: Differential Geometry and Lie Groups, Test 2, held 2026-11-02, solutions posted 2026-11-23) and the instructor's handwritten solutions. Conventions: $G$ is a Lie group, $\mathfrak g = \mathrm{Lie}(G)$ its Lie algebra of left-invariant vector fields. Each question is worth 5 points (total 20 points).

### Questions and Complete Solutions

1. (5 points) Let $G$, $N$, and $H$ be Lie groups. Prove that $G$ is isomorphic to a semidirect product $N \rtimes H$ if and only if there are Lie group homomorphisms $\varphi : G \to H$ and $\psi : H \to G$ such that $\varphi \circ \psi = \mathrm{Id}_H$ and $\ker \varphi \cong N$.
   - **$(\Rightarrow)$**: Suppose $G \cong N \rtimes_\theta H$ for a smooth action $\theta: H \to \mathrm{Aut}(N)$ where the action map $H \times N \to N$, $(h, n) \mapsto \theta(h)(n)$ is smooth. The group multiplication on $N \times H$ is $(n_1, h_1)(n_2, h_2) = (n_1 \theta(h_1)(n_2),\, h_1 h_2)$.
     Define $\varphi: N \rtimes H \to H$ by $\varphi(n, h) = h$ (the projection map) and $\psi: H \to N \rtimes H$ by $\psi(h) = (e_N, h)$ (the canonical inclusion). Both are smooth maps between Lie groups.
     They are Lie group homomorphisms:
     $$\varphi((n_1, h_1)(n_2, h_2)) = h_1 h_2 = \varphi(n_1, h_1)\varphi(n_2, h_2),$$
     $$\psi(h_1 h_2) = (e_N, h_1 h_2) = (e_N \theta(h_1)(e_N),\, h_1 h_2) = (e_N, h_1)(e_N, h_2) = \psi(h_1)\psi(h_2).$$
     Their composition satisfies $(\varphi \circ \psi)(h) = \varphi(e_N, h) = h = \mathrm{Id}_H(h)$ for all $h \in H$.
     The kernel is $\ker \varphi = \{(n, h) \in N \rtimes H : h = e_H\} = N \times \{e_H\} \cong N$.
   - **$(\Leftarrow)$**: Suppose there exist Lie group homomorphisms $\varphi: G \to H$ and $\psi: H \to G$ satisfying $\varphi \circ \psi = \mathrm{Id}_H$ and $\ker \varphi \cong N$.
     Let $\widetilde N = \ker \varphi \subset G$ and $\widetilde H = \psi(H) \subset G$.
     Since $\varphi$ is a Lie group homomorphism, $\widetilde N = \ker \varphi$ is a closed, embedded normal Lie subgroup of $G$.
     Since $\varphi \circ \psi = \mathrm{Id}_H$, the differential at the identity satisfies $d\varphi_e \circ d\psi_e = \mathrm{id}_{T_e H}$, so $d\psi_e$ is injective. Thus $\psi$ is an injective immersion. Since $\psi$ is a homomorphism, $\widetilde H = \psi(H)$ is a Lie subgroup of $G$, and $\psi: H \to \widetilde H$ is a Lie group isomorphism.
     We establish the internal semidirect product decomposition $G \cong \widetilde N \rtimes \widetilde H$:
     1. *Trivial intersection*: If $g \in \widetilde N \cap \widetilde H$, then $g = \psi(h)$ for some $h \in H$. Since $g \in \ker \varphi$, $e_H = \varphi(g) = \varphi(\psi(h)) = (\varphi \circ \psi)(h) = h$. Thus $h = e_H$, so $g = \psi(e_H) = e_G$. Hence $\widetilde N \cap \widetilde H = \{e_G\}$.
     2. *Factorization*: For any $g \in G$, set $h = \varphi(g) \in H$ and $\tilde h = \psi(h) = \psi(\varphi(g)) \in \widetilde H$.
        Consider $n = g \tilde h^{-1} = g (\psi(\varphi(g)))^{-1} \in G$. Applying $\varphi$:
        $$\varphi(n) = \varphi(g)\,\varphi(\psi(\varphi(g)))^{-1} = \varphi(g)\,(\varphi \circ \psi)(\varphi(g))^{-1} = \varphi(g)\,\varphi(g)^{-1} = e_H.$$
        Therefore $n \in \ker \varphi = \widetilde N$. Hence every $g \in G$ factors as $g = n \tilde h$ with $n \in \widetilde N$ and $\tilde h \in \widetilde H$, so $G = \widetilde N \widetilde H$.
     3. *Action and diffeomorphism*: Because $\widetilde N$ is normal in $G$, conjugation defines a smooth action of $\widetilde H$ on $\widetilde N$: $\alpha_{\tilde h}(n) = \tilde h n \tilde h^{-1} \in \widetilde N$. Via the isomorphisms $\widetilde N \cong N$ and $\widetilde H \cong H$, this induces a smooth action $\theta: H \times N \to N$.
        The multiplication map $\Phi: \widetilde N \rtimes \widetilde H \to G$, $\Phi(n, \tilde h) = n \tilde h$ is a bijective group homomorphism. Its inverse is $g \mapsto (g(\psi(\varphi(g)))^{-1},\, \psi(\varphi(g)))$, which is smooth as a composite of smooth Lie group operations ($\varphi$, $\psi$, inversion, multiplication). Thus $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism and a group isomorphism, giving a Lie group isomorphism $G \cong \widetilde N \rtimes \widetilde H \cong N \rtimes H$.
   - **Practises**: Split short exact sequences of Lie groups, internal vs external semidirect products.

2. (5 points) Prove that $\mathrm{Lie}(SO(n)) = \{A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb R) : A^T + A = 0\}$.
   - **Solution**:
     $SO(n) = \{A \in GL(n, \mathbb R) : A^T A = I,\ \det A = 1\}$.
     Consider the map $\Phi : GL(n, \mathbb R) \to \mathrm{Sym}(n, \mathbb R)$ defined by $\Phi(A) = A^T A$, where $\mathrm{Sym}(n, \mathbb R) = \{S \in \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb R) : S^T = S\}$ is the vector space of real symmetric $n \times n$ matrices ($\dim \mathrm{Sym}(n, \mathbb R) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$).
     The orthogonal group is $O(n) = \Phi^{-1}(I)$.
     Since $\det: GL(n, \mathbb R) \to \mathbb R^*$ is continuous and $\det I = 1 > 0$, the set $GL^+(n, \mathbb R) = \{A \in GL(n, \mathbb R) : \det A > 0\}$ is open in $GL(n, \mathbb R)$.
     Thus $SO(n) = O(n) \cap GL^+(n, \mathbb R)$ is an open submanifold of $O(n)$ containing $I$. In particular, $T_I(SO(n)) = T_I(O(n))$.
     Compute the differential $d\Phi_I : T_I(GL(n, \mathbb R)) \to T_I(\mathrm{Sym}(n, \mathbb R)) \cong \mathrm{Sym}(n, \mathbb R)$. For any $A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb R)$, take the smooth curve $\gamma(t) = I + tA$ with $\gamma(0) = I$, $\gamma'(0) = A$:
     $$d\Phi_I(A) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} \Phi(I + tA) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} (I + tA^T)(I + tA) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} (I + t(A^T + A) + t^2 A^T A) = A^T + A.$$
     Hence $\ker d\Phi_I = \{A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb R) : A^T + A = 0\} = \mathfrak{so}(n)$ (the space of skew-symmetric matrices).
     For any $S \in \mathrm{Sym}(n, \mathbb R)$, taking $A = \frac{1}{2}S$ gives $d\Phi_I(A) = \frac{1}{2}(S^T + S) = S$, so $d\Phi_I$ is surjective.
     By right translation, for any $Q \in O(n)$, $d\Phi_Q(X) = Q^T X + X^T Q$ is likewise surjective (take $X = \frac{1}{2}QS$). Thus $I$ is a regular value of $\Phi$.
     By the Regular Level Set Theorem, $O(n)$ is a closed embedded Lie subgroup of $GL(n, \mathbb R)$ with:
     $$\dim O(n) = \dim GL(n, \mathbb R) - \dim \mathrm{Sym}(n, \mathbb R) = n^2 - \frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}.$$
     Its tangent space at $I$ is $T_I(SO(n)) = T_I(O(n)) = \ker d\Phi_I = \{A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb R) : A^T + A = 0\}$.
     Identifying $\mathrm{Lie}(SO(n))$ with $T_I(SO(n))$ via the isomorphism $X \mapsto X_I$, we conclude $\mathrm{Lie}(SO(n)) = \{A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb R) : A^T + A = 0\}$.
   - **Practises**: Regular level sets, submersion theorem, Lie algebra of classical matrix groups.

3. (5 points) Let $X \in \mathfrak g$. Prove that $X$ is a complete vector field.
   - **Solution**:
     By the local existence and uniqueness theorem for integral curves of smooth vector fields, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ and an integral curve $\gamma : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to G$ of $X$ starting at $e$, i.e., $\gamma(0) = e$ and $\gamma'(t) = X_{\gamma(t)}$ for all $t \in (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$.
     For any arbitrary $g \in G$, define $\sigma_g : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to G$ by left translation:
     $$\sigma_g(t) = L_g(\gamma(t)) = g \cdot \gamma(t).$$
     Then $\sigma_g(0) = L_g(e) = g$.
     Using the chain rule, for all $t \in (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$:
     $$\sigma_g'(t) = d(L_g)_{\gamma(t)}(\gamma'(t)) = d(L_g)_{\gamma(t)}(X_{\gamma(t)}).$$
     Because $X \in \mathfrak g$ is left-invariant, $(L_g)_* X = X$, meaning $d(L_g)_p(X_p) = X_{L_g(p)}$ for all $p \in G$. Taking $p = \gamma(t)$:
     $$d(L_g)_{\gamma(t)}(X_{\gamma(t)}) = X_{L_g(\gamma(t))} = X_{\sigma_g(t)}.$$
     Therefore $\sigma_g$ is an integral curve of $X$ starting at $g$, defined on the interval $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$.
     Notice that the interval $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ is uniform: $\varepsilon > 0$ is chosen once at $e$ and works for every $g \in G$.
     By the **Uniform Time Lemma** (Lee, Lemma 9.15): if there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that the flow $\theta_t(p)$ of a smooth vector field is defined for all $t \in (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ and all $p \in M$, then the vector field is complete (every maximal integral curve has domain $\mathbb R$).
     Thus $X$ is complete.
   - **Practises**: Left-invariance, integral curves under pushforwards, Uniform Time Lemma.

4. (5 points) Let $A, B \subset \mathfrak g$ such that $A \oplus B = \mathfrak g$. Show that the map $\Phi : A \oplus B \to G$ given by $\Phi(X, Y) = \exp X \exp Y$ is a diffeomorphism on some neighbourhood of $(0, 0)$.
   - **Solution**:
     The direct sum $A \oplus B = \mathfrak g$ is a finite-dimensional real vector space with $T_{(0,0)}(A \oplus B) \cong A \oplus B$.
     The exponential map $\exp : \mathfrak g \to G$ is smooth, and group multiplication $m : G \times G \to G$ is smooth.
     Thus $\Phi(X, Y) = m(\exp X, \exp Y) = \exp X \exp Y$ is smooth as a composition of smooth maps.
     We compute the differential $d\Phi_{(0,0)} : T_{(0,0)}(A \oplus B) \to T_{\Phi(0,0)} G = T_e G \cong \mathfrak g$.
     Under the canonical identification $T_{(0,0)}(A \oplus B) \cong A \oplus B$:
     For $(X, 0) \in A \times \{0\}$, take the curve $c_1(t) = (tX, 0)$ with $c_1(0) = (0,0)$ and $c_1'(0) = (X, 0)$:
     $$d\Phi_{(0,0)}(X, 0) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} \Phi(tX, 0) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} (\exp(tX) \exp(0)) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} \exp(tX) = X.$$
     For $(0, Y) \in \{0\} \times B$, take the curve $c_2(t) = (0, tY)$ with $c_2(0) = (0,0)$ and $c_2'(0) = (0, Y)$:
     $$d\Phi_{(0,0)}(0, Y) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} \Phi(0, tY) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} (\exp(0) \exp(tY)) = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0} \exp(tY) = Y.$$
     By linearity of the differential:
     $$d\Phi_{(0,0)}(X, Y) = d\Phi_{(0,0)}(X, 0) + d\Phi_{(0,0)}(0, Y) = X + Y.$$
     Since $\mathfrak g = A \oplus B$, every vector in $\mathfrak g$ can be uniquely decomposed as $X + Y$ with $X \in A$ and $Y \in B$.
     Hence $d\Phi_{(0,0)} : A \oplus B \to \mathfrak g$ is a linear isomorphism (bijective linear map between vector spaces of equal dimension).
     By the **Inverse Function Theorem** on manifolds, since $d\Phi_{(0,0)}$ is invertible, $\Phi$ is a local diffeomorphism at $(0, 0)$.
     That is, there exists an open neighbourhood $U$ of $(0, 0)$ in $A \oplus B$ and an open neighbourhood $V$ of $e$ in $G$ such that $\Phi|_U : U \to V$ is a diffeomorphism.
   - **Practises**: Exponential map differential at $0$, group multiplication differential, Inverse Function Theorem.
