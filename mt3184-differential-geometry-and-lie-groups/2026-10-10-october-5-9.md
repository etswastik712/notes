**Revision of Lie groups and subgroups (DG19)**
- A Lie group is a manifold $G$ with a group structure such that $m:G\times G\to G$ and $i:G\to G$, $g\mapsto g^{-1}$, are smooth. For $F(g,h)=(g,gh)$ (and similar maps), $F$ is a bijective smooth map with invertible differential, hence a diffeomorphism; so $\pi_2\circ F^{-1}$ is smooth.
- Theorem 7.5: every Lie group homomorphism has constant rank. Theorem 7.25 (Equivariant Rank Theorem): for a transitive $G$-action on $M$ and an equivariant $F:M\to N$, $F$ has constant rank.
- An open subgroup $H\le G$ is embedded and closed. The identity component $G_0$ is the only connected open (normal) subgroup, and each component of $G$ is diffeomorphic to $G_0$ (a left translate).
- If $F:G\to H$ is a Lie group homomorphism, then $\ker F$ is an embedded Lie subgroup of dimension $\dim G-\mathrm{rank}\,dF$, and $F(G)$ is a Lie subgroup.
- Examples: $SL(n,\mathbb R)=\ker\det$ (embedded Lie subgroup of $GL(n,\mathbb R)$); $O(n)$ is a compact embedded Lie subgroup of $GL(n,\mathbb R)$ and $SO(n)$ is its identity component (as $\det:O(n)\to\{\pm1\}$ is continuous, $SO(n)$ is open and closed); $SL(n,\mathbb C)$ is a Lie subgroup of $GL(n,\mathbb C)$ of dimension $2n^2-2$ (the slide's figure; check).
- Representation: a Lie group homomorphism $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ with $V$ a finite-dimensional vector space; it is faithful if injective. Examples on the slide: $\mathbb C^*\to GL(n,\mathbb C)$ type embeddings are faithful; a map that factors through a quotient (e.g. $z\mapsto \mathrm{diag}(\dots)$ with $e^{2\pi i\cdot}$ phases) is not faithful. The universal cover of $SL(2,\mathbb R)$ has no faithful representation.
- Closed Subgroup Theorem: a subgroup $H\le G$ is an embedded Lie subgroup iff it is closed.

**Lie algebras and left-invariant fields (DG19, DG20)**
- Lie algebra: a vector space $\mathfrak g$ with a bilinear, antisymmetric bracket satisfying the Jacobi identity. $\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ is the space of left-invariant vector fields on $G$.
- If $X,Y$ are $F$-related to $\tilde X,\tilde Y$ then $[X,Y]$ is $F$-related to $[\tilde X,\tilde Y]$; hence the bracket of two left-invariant fields is left-invariant, so $\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak X(G)$. Example: $\mathbb R^3$ with the cross product as bracket.
- Theorem: $\varepsilon:\mathrm{Lie}(G)\to T_eG$ is a vector space isomorphism. Proof of surjectivity: for $v\in T_eG$ define $V_g=d(L_g)_e(v)$; show it is smooth by writing $V f(g)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big|_{0}f(g\gamma(t))$ with $\gamma'(0)=v$ and the smooth map $\varphi(t,g)=f(g\gamma(t))$.
- Consequences: every left-invariant rough field is smooth; $G$ admits a left-invariant smooth global frame, so every Lie group is parallelizable. The slide notes that the spheres $S^1,S^3,S^7$ are parallelizable and that $S^7$ is not a Lie group (check against the PDF; DG11 only mentions $S^1$, $S^3$).
- Proposition: the composition $\mathrm{Lie}(GL(n,\mathbb R))\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}T_IGL(n,\mathbb R)\cong M(n,\mathbb R)$ is a Lie algebra isomorphism with $[A,B]=AB-BA$. Proof in global coordinates $x^{ij}$ on $M(n,\mathbb R)$: the left-invariant field $\tilde A$ with $\tilde A_I=A$ is $\sum_{i,j}\big(\sum_k x^{ik}A^{kj}\big)\partial/\partial x^{ij}$; its bracket with $\tilde B$ is the field of $AB-BA$.
- Theorem 8.44: for a Lie group homomorphism $F:G\to H$, $X\in\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ is $F$-related to a unique $Y\in\mathrm{Lie}(H)$, and $F_*:X\mapsto Y$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism. For an immersed subgroup $G\le M$ (Lie group) the inclusion identifies $\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ with the Lie subalgebra of $\mathrm{Lie}(M)$ of left-invariant fields tangent to $G$ (evaluation at $e$ gives $T_eG\subset T_eM$).
- Lemma: if $F:M\to N$ has constant rank and $S=F^{-1}(q)$, then $T_pS=\ker dF_p$ (via the inclusion $\iota:S\to M$: $F\circ\iota$ is constant, so $d\iota_p(T_pS)\subset\ker dF_p$, and the dimensions agree).
- Example: $\mathrm{Lie}(SO(n))$ via $\Phi(A)=AA^T$ (equivariant, constant rank): $\mathrm{Lie}(O(n))=\ker d\Phi_I=\{A: A+A^T=0\}$, the skew-symmetric matrices; in particular the bracket of skew-symmetric matrices is skew-symmetric.
- Representation of a Lie algebra: a Lie algebra homomorphism $\mathfrak g\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)$. If $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ is a representation then $\rho_*:\mathfrak g\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is one.
- Ado's Theorem: every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra admits a faithful representation. Corollary: every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra is isomorphic to a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb R)$.
- Lie correspondence: $G\mapsto\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ from simply connected Lie groups to finite-dimensional Lie algebras is well defined, injective (Homomorphism Theorem, see [[mt3184-differential-geometry-and-lie-groups/2026-09-15-september-14-18]]) and surjective (Lie's Third Theorem).

**Integral curves and flows (DG21)**
- Integral curve of $V$ starting at $p$: a curve $\gamma:J\to M$ with $\gamma'(t)=V_{\gamma(t)}$ and $\gamma(0)=p$. In $\mathbb R^2$ with $V=V^1\partial_x+V^2\partial_y$ this means solving the ODE system $\dot x=V^1(x,y)$, $\dot y=V^2(x,y)$ with initial values $(a,b)$.
- Example: $V=-y\,\partial_x+x\,\partial_y$ on $\mathbb R^2$: the integral curve from $(a,b)$ is $(a\cos t-b\sin t,\ a\sin t+b\cos t)$, defined on all of $\mathbb R$ (the slide's formula is garbled; this is the rotation, check).
- Proposition 9.2: for $V\in\mathfrak X(M)$ and $p\in M$ there is an integral curve of $V$ starting at $p$ (existence and uniqueness from ODE theory).
- Global flow: a smooth action $\theta:\mathbb R\times M\to M$ with $\theta_0=\mathrm{id}$ and $\theta_s\circ\theta_t=\theta_{s+t}$; each $\theta^{(p)}(t)=\theta(t,p)$ is an integral curve of its infinitesimal generator $V_p=\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\theta(t,p)$.
- Proposition 9.7: if $\theta:\mathbb R\times M\to M$ is a smooth $\mathbb R$-action (global flow) and $V_p=\frac{d}{dt}|_{0}\theta^{(p)}(t)$, then $V$ is a smooth vector field and each $\theta^{(p)}$ is an integral curve of $V$ starting at $p$. $V$ is the infinitesimal generator of $\theta$. Proof idea: $V=\partial\theta/\partial t|_{t=0}$ is smooth; $\theta^{(p)\prime}(t)=\frac{d}{ds}|_{0}\theta(s+t,p)=d(\theta_t)_p V_p\ldots=V_{\theta(t,p)}$ using $\theta_s\circ\theta_t=\theta_{s+t}$.
- Not all integral curves are defined on all of $\mathbb R$. Examples: restriction of a field to $M\setminus\{0\}$ or the field $x^2\partial_x$ on $\mathbb R$ give integral curves defined on a proper interval (the slides' examples are only partly legible).
- Flow domain: an open set $D\subset\mathbb R\times M$ such that for each $p$, $D^{(p)}=\{t:(t,p)\in D\}$ is an open interval containing $0$. A flow on $M$ is a smooth map $\theta:D\to M$ with $\theta(0,p)=p$ and $\theta(s,\theta(t,p))=\theta(s+t,p)$ whenever $(t,p)\in D$ and $(s,\theta(t,p))\in D$.
- Theorem 9.12 (Fundamental Theorem on Flows): for $V\in\mathfrak X(M)$ there is a unique smooth maximal flow $\theta:D\to M$ with infinitesimal generator $V$. (a) For each $p$, $\theta^{(p)}$ is the unique maximal integral curve of $V$ starting at $p$. (b) If $s\in D^{(p)}$ then $D^{(\theta(s,p))}=D^{(p)}-s$. (c) For each $t$, $M_t=\{p:(t,p)\in D\}$ is open in $M$ and $\theta_t:M_t\to M_{-t}$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $\theta_{-t}$.
