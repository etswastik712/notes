## Mid-semester (Fall 2021)

> [!example]- Acid–base part (Vaidhyanathan) · key checked ([[CH04 Acid-Base Concepts]], [[CH05 Acid Strength Lewis Acidity and Reactions]])
> **2-mark questions**
> 1. 4 × 10⁻⁴ M HCl: pH 3.40, so **pOH 10.6 (b)**. [key] ✓
> 2. Increasing acidity: **Ba²⁺ < Sr²⁺ < Hg²⁺ (c)**. Hg²⁺ has a high Z* (poor d-shielding); Ba²⁺ is the largest ion. [key] ✓
> 3. A strong acid has a large $K_a$: **True**. [key] ✓
> 4. 1.0 M HCl: **[OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴ M (a)**. [key] ✓
> 5. The pH of a solution changes with temperature: **True**. $K_w$ is temperature-dependent.
> 6. "Phenol's p$K_a$ is 29.1 in MeCN and 10 in water, so MeCN is more acidic": **False**. [key] ✓ The key's reason ("less polar") is incomplete. The real reason: MeCN solvates and stabilises the phenoxide anion far less well than water does, so phenol is a weaker acid *in* MeCN.
>
> **3-mark questions**
> 1. 10⁻⁴ M HA with $K_a=10^{-10}$. Water's autoionisation is not negligible here: $[\mathrm H^+]^2=K_aC+K_w=2\times10^{-14}$, so **pH = 6.85**. The key leaves this blank.
> 2. $K_w=10^{-13}$ at 60 °C, larger than at 25 °C. (a) By Le Chatelier, raising $T$ shifts the equilibrium right, so autoionisation is **endothermic**. (b) Neutral water: $[\mathrm H^+]=[\mathrm{OH^-}]=3.16\times10^{-7}$ M (pH 6.5).
> 3. Pauling's rule p$K_a\approx8-5p$, with $p$ the number of oxo O atoms:
>    - HClO₃ = (HO)ClO₂, $p=2$: p$K_a$ ≈ −2;
>    - HClO₂ = (HO)ClO, $p=1$: p$K_a$ ≈ 3 (observed 1.94).
>
>    [key] ✓
>
> **5-mark questions**
> 1. (a) Lactic acid, 0.100 M, 3.7 % dissociated: $K_a=\dfrac{0.0037^2}{0.0963}=$ **1.4 × 10⁻⁴**.
>    (b) H₂SO₃ = (HO)₂SO, $p=1$, so Pauling predicts ≈ 3; observed 1.8. The key: the molecule OS(OH)₂ is not detected in solution, because SO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂SO₃ has $K<10^{-9}$. "Sulfurous acid" is really SO₂(aq) in equilibrium with HSO₃⁻ and S₂O₅²⁻. [!verify] The question says the observed p$K_a$ is "much higher" than predicted, but 1.8 is *lower* than 3. The key's explanation (no true H₂SO₃) is still the point.
> 2. (a) **Ag⁺** gives the more acidic aqua ion: poorly shielding d electrons give it a higher Z* at the same radius as Na⁺. [key] ✓
>    (b) Al³⁺ (1s²2s²2p⁶) accepts six water lone pairs into empty 3s/3p(/3d) orbitals, giving octahedral [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺. [key] Caveat: the key's sp³d² hybrid picture is the textbook VB story; d-orbital participation is not needed in MO terms.
>    (c) In ammonia, Al³⁺ precipitates as Al(OH)₃. Ag⁺ dissolves as linear **[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺**. [key] ✓

> [!example]- Bonding part (Boomishankar) · key checked ([[CH01 VSEPR and Valence Bond Theory]], [[CH02 Molecular Orbital Theory Diatomics]], [[CH03 Molecular Orbital Theory Polyatomics]])
> **Part 1** [key] ✓
> - HCO₃⁻: structure (b), with formal charges minimised.
> - XeOF₄: **square pyramidal, lone pair trans to O (a)**.
> - ClOF₃: **see-saw, sp³d (b)**.
> - CH₂N₂: structure (c), H₂C=N⁺=N⁻.
>
> **Part 2**
> 1. BH₃ (D₃h): B 2s → a₁′; B 2pₓ,2p_y → e′; 2p_z → a₂″ (empty, non-bonding). H₃ group orbitals: a₁′ + e′. Six electrons fill 1a₁′² 1e′⁴, leaving a₂″ empty. That empty orbital makes BH₃ a Lewis acid.
> 2. **Bent's rule** (key): where promotion energy is high and overlap good, the hybridisation energy is small and the hybrid is s-rich. Poor overlap and low promotion energy give p-rich hybrids. In short: s character concentrates in orbitals toward electropositive substituents and lone pairs.
> 3. [PF₆]⁻ hypervalence. VB: the 3d orbitals of P expand the octet. MO (as for SF₆): bonding a₁g + t₁u, non-bonding e_g on the ligands, antibonding t₁u* and a₁g* empty. The species is stable without any d participation. [key] ✓
>
> **Part 3**
> 1. O₂ UPS, key assignment: 12.5 eV 1π_g, 17 eV 1π_u, 18.5 eV 2σ_g, 20.5 eV 1σ_u. The fine structure is vibrational: the ion is left in different vibrational levels. [!verify] In the literature, the 18.2 and 20.3 eV bands are *both* ionisations of 3σ_g, leaving the ion in quartet and doublet states (because O₂ has two unpaired π* electrons), and 2σ_u lies at ~24.6 eV. The key's labels follow the simpler one-band-per-MO picture taught.
> 2. CH₂F₂ with F–C–F = 108.49°. From $\cos\theta=\dfrac{s}{s-1}$: $s_{\rm CF}=0.241$ (24.1 % s, 75.9 % p). Then $s_{\rm CH}=0.259$, and H–C–H = **110.46°**. [key] ✓ (recomputed: 110.48°). The key's "75.1 % P" for C–F is a typo for 75.9 %.
> 3. Cyclic H₄ group orbitals: a, e (pair), b. For square-planar XeH₄, the key builds the levels 1a₁ < 1e < 1a₂ (Xe p_z, non-bonding) < 1b₁ (H₄, non-bonding) < 2e < 2a₁ and fills 12 electrons. [!verify] 12 electrons fill 1a₁² 1e⁴ 1a₂² 1b₁² plus one more pair. In the usual s,p-only model that pair is 2a₁ (Xe–H antibonding, Xe-s lone-pair-like), and the antibonding 2e set (e_u*) stays empty. Net result: two 3c–4e bonds, Xe–H bond order ½. The key's ordering puts 2e below 2a₁.

## End-semester (Dec 2021)

> [!example]- Section I (MCQs; no key, mine)
> 1. SO₃²⁻ + HF ⇌ HSO₃⁻ + F⁻ ($K=10^4$) "violates HSAB" because **SO₃²⁻ is a soft base compared to F⁻ (a)**: the hard acid H⁺ goes to the softer base. Brønsted basicity, not HSAB, decides here. ([[CH06 Hard and Soft Acids and Bases]])
> 2. B₂H₆ + NH₃: unsymmetrical cleavage to **[BH₂(NH₃)₂][BH₄] (a)**. Bulky amines give the symmetrical adduct H₃B·NR₃ instead. [!verify] ([[CH07 Main Group Cages and Clusters]])
> 3. The hard–hard pair is the more ionic one, with the higher melting point: **complex B (b)**.
> 4. B₅H₁₁: $(5\cdot3+11-2\cdot5)/2=8$ pairs $=n+3$, so **8 pairs, arachno (d)**.
> 5. pH = p$K_a$ + log 2 ⇒ **p$K_a$ = 2.7 (a)**.
> 6. [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (d⁷ high spin), bands at 8100, 16 000 and 19 400 cm⁻¹. The t₂g stabilisation is $0.4\Delta_o$. With $\Delta_o\approx\nu_1=8100$ cm⁻¹ this gives **38.7 kJ mol⁻¹ (b)**. [!verify] For d⁷ the rigorous value is $\Delta_o=\nu_2-\nu_1=7900$ cm⁻¹ (37.8 kJ mol⁻¹). The options are built on $\nu_1$. ([[CH10 Crystal Field Theory]])
> 7. At equilibrium: **ΔG = 0, Q = K (b)**. ([[CH09 Redox]])
> 8. Strong Jahn–Teller distortion: **[Co(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (c)**, low-spin d⁷ with one e_g electron.
> 9. $E^\circ_{\rm cell}$ is **the difference of the two half-reaction potentials (b)**.
> 10. [Os(dmpe)₂(η²-C₂H₄)₂]²⁺: Os(II) d⁶ + 4 P donors × 2 + 2 alkenes × 2 = 18. **Yes (a)**.

> [!example]- Section II (3 marks each)
> 1. Soft–soft interactions.
>    - **Mulliken and Chatt**: soft acids (low-valent, d-rich metals) **π-back-donate** from filled d orbitals into empty d or π* orbitals of soft bases (P, S, I, CO), adding covalent π bonding.
>    - **Pitzer**: **London dispersion** between two large, polarisable atoms grows with polarisability.
>
>    ([[CH06 Hard and Soft Acids and Bases]])
> 2. Borazine → polyborazylene → (BN)ₓ. [key] Heating B₃N₃H₆ at ~70 °C eliminates H₂ and links the rings by B–N bonds (polyborazylene). Pyrolysis at 1200 °C then gives hexagonal (BN)ₓ. ([[CH08 Main Group Rings and Polymers]])
> 3. Al | Al³⁺ (0.15 M) ‖ Cu²⁺ (0.025 M) | Cu. Overall 2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu, so $n=6$ and $Q=\dfrac{[\mathrm{Al^{3+}}]^2}{[\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}]^3}=1440$. $E^\circ=0.34+1.66=2.00$ V, so $E=2.00-\frac{0.0592}{6}\log1440=$ **1.97 V**: spontaneous.
> 4. Cyclophosphazenes, two bonding models. [key]
>    - **Craig–Paddock**: P d_xz overlaps N p_z (dπ–pπ). The gerade d orbital forces a sign mismatch, hence a node, somewhere in the odd-membered (P₃N₃) ring.
>    - **Dewar**: d_xz and d_yz on each P hybridise into two orthogonal lobes, one toward each neighbouring N. This gives 3-centre P–N–P "islands", with nodes at each P.
>
>    Both explain why the rings are not aromatic.
> 5. 2Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Cu: $E=0.52-0.16=0.36$ V, so $K=10^{0.36/0.0592}=$ **1.2 × 10⁶**. This is a **disproportionation** and it is **spontaneous**, which is why Cu⁺(aq) is unstable.
> 6. Silicates. (i) The repeat units of the two double chains: [key] **[Si₆O₁₇]¹⁰⁻** (a: xonotlite type) and **[Si₂O₅]²⁻** (b). (ii) Cyclic [Si₆O₁₈]¹²⁻: a six-membered ring of corner-sharing SiO₄ tetrahedra (beryl).

> [!example]- Section III (5 marks each)
> 1. **[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ MO diagram** [key]. Metal: 4s (a₁g), 4p (t₁u), 3d (t₂g + e_g). Ligand σ-SALCs: a₁g + t₁u + e_g. Bonding a₁g, t₁u, e_g (12 e from the ligands); non-bonding t₂g⁶; antibonding e_g*³; a₁g* and t₁u* empty. The single hole in e_g* gives the Jahn–Teller distortion. ([[CH11 Ligand-Field Theory - Molecular Orbitals of Complexes]])
> 2. (a) ClO₄⁻ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → ClO₃⁻ + H₂O, E° = 1.20 V.
>    (b) Concentration cell. Anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ (0.10 M) + 2e⁻. Cathode: Zn²⁺ (0.50 M) + 2e⁻ → Zn. $E=-\frac{0.0592}{2}\log\frac{0.10}{0.50}=$ **+0.021 V**: spontaneous. $E^\circ_{\rm cell}=0$ for any concentration cell.
>    (c) **F (front) strain**: steric clash between bulky groups on the acid and the base in the adduct (e.g. Me₃N·BMe₃ against bulkier amines), which weakens the adduct.
> 3. [Fe(phen)₂(NCS)₂] is a **spin-crossover** complex. [key] Fe(II) d⁶: high spin t₂g⁴e_g² (4 unpaired, spin-only $\sqrt{24}=$ 4.90 BM) at room temperature; low spin t₂g⁶ (0 BM) below 100 K. The measured 5.90 BM exceeds the spin-only value because of an orbital contribution from the ⁵T₂g ground term. (b) Low-spin Fe(III) (1 unpaired) and superoxide (1 unpaired) couple **antiferromagnetically**, so oxy-heme is diamagnetic. [key] ✓
> 4. Mn redox diagrams. ([[CH09 Redox]] §11)
>    - (a) Frost diagram, pH 0: **MnO₄⁻** is the highest point, the least stable and strongest oxidant. **H₃MnO₄** (Mn(V)) is the most unstable to disproportionation: it lies ~0.8 V above the line from MnO₂ to HMnO₄⁻. In base (pH 14) the lowest point is **Mn₂O₃** (≈ −3.3 V), just below MnO₂ and Mn(OH)₂. [!verify] Values read from the figure.
>    - (b) MnO₄⁻ → MnO₂: $E^\circ=\dfrac{0.564+0.274+4.27}{3}=$ **1.70 V**.
>    - (c) In the Fe Pourbaix diagram, **0.77 V is the Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ couple**. It is horizontal because no H⁺ is involved. The vertical line at pH ≈ 3 is **Fe³⁺ + 3H₂O → Fe(OH)₃(s) + 3H⁺**: a precipitation with no electron transfer, which is why the line does not depend on E. (The question calls it a "redox" reaction, which it is not.)
