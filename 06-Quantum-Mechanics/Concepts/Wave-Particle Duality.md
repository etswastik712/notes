# Wave-Particle Duality

<span class="scope-taught">taught · in syllabus</span>

> [!syllabus] In the syllabus vs extra
> 🟢 **taught:** [[PH2113_Wavefunction_to_Uncertainty (1).pdf#page=1|double slit, WU §1]] and [[PH2113_Lecture6-7.pdf#page=2|L7 §4]].

> [!intuition] In one picture
> Electrons arrive one at a time as single flashes, yet the flashes pile up into an interference pattern: it is neither a classical particle nor a classical wave.

> [!key] Definition
> Quantum objects show **particle-like** detection (discrete, localised) and **wave-like** statistics (interference, diffraction). The two are reconciled by a single mathematical object, the **wavefunction** $\psi$, whose squared modulus gives the probability of a detection ([[Born Rule]]).

## What the experiments say
- **Light as particles:** the photoelectric effect and **Compton scattering** show energy $h\nu$ and momentum $h/\lambda$ delivered at a point ([[Compton Scattering]]).
- **Matter as waves:** electrons diffract from a crystal with $\lambda=h/p$ ([[de Broglie Relation]]); a double slit gives fringes even one electron at a time.
- **The resolution** (lecture notes): "abandon the prediction of individual arrivals and predict instead the probability of arrival, with the probability obtained from a wave amplitude, so that amplitudes can cancel where the pattern is dark."

> [!example] Numbers
> Electrons at 100 V have $\lambda=0.1226$ nm: comparable to atomic spacings, so crystals diffract them. A baseball (0.145 kg, 40 m/s) has $\lambda=1.1\times10^{-34}$ m: no wave effect can be observed.

> [!pitfall] Not "sometimes a wave, sometimes a particle"
> A single entity is described by a single $\psi$. What you *see* depends on the measurement: a position detector gives a localised hit, a set of slits gives fringes. Classical wave and classical particle are two **idealisations**; the lecture notes stress that quantum matter fits neither column ([[QM01 From Waves to Probabilities]] §5).
